The National Survey of Sexual Attitudes and Lifestyles (Natsal) is the crowdpleaser of the data world. Nerds love it because of its extraordinary thoroughness. It’s one of the largest studies of sexual behaviour in the world, surveying nearly 7,000 people in the UK between the ages of 16 and 59, once a decade. Everyone else loves it because they get to talk about sex. Some of the statistics speak for themselves – among 25- to 34-year-old women, the number who say they never want children has gone up fourfold, a sad, completely understandable reflection of the outside world.
Other figures, though, are mysterious: the number of women aged 16 to 24 who are on the pill has dropped precipitously, from nearly half to less than a third. Over the same period, the online space has been flooded with information about hormonal contraception, some of it better than it has ever been – more accessible, more accurate – and some of it plain wrong. Is there strategic, funded disinformation coming from Christian fundamentalists, such as we have already seen in the abortion space, where anti-abortion activists hose money at putatively “grassroots” online influencers? Or is it a quirk of the algorithms that once you go down the rabbit hole of “has the pill made me depressed?”, you will be shown endless influencers who have experienced that very (improbable) thing? It’s hard for a young person to navigate what’s real, and it’s hard to distinguish superstition from preference.
What we can say for certain, though, is that everyone is having less sex. Natsal started in 1990, but the face-to-face fieldwork was interrupted by Covid. Instead, researchers released interim statistics, which described a sex drought from a number of different angles – fewer new encounters reported, more celibacy, particularly among the young, less sex within relationships. It felt at the time like the least bad side-effect of a global pandemic, but that picture has persisted. Under-45s typically have sex twice a month; in 1990 the average was four times. And one in three under-25s have never had sex at all.
Researchers, commentators, radio phone-in participants – everyone tends to find the thing they like least about modern life and project it on to this panda-like cohort. In the past 20 minutes (I’m not exaggerating), I’ve heard that it’s all down to the prevalence of porn; and that it’s all because of the vexed relationship between young men and young women, who are too anxious about consent and miles apart in their gender politics. A guy on LBC just said that it was because the stigma had been lifted on asexuality, and young people were simply expressing their desire not to have sex by not having it. Others cite a Covid hangover, a generation that didn’t learn how to socialise and so finds it more comfortable to conduct their intimacy from behind a screen.
Any of those things could be true, or none of them could. Even if respondents were called on to explain their sexual behaviour – and the survey tends not to ask questions that amount to “why are you weird?” – a lack of sexual encounters is not a thing you could readily explain. Did you not go out enough, or get drunk enough, or drop your inhibitions enough, or did others fail to disinhibit around you? Is that because none of you want it any more, or because the have-a-go jeopardy feels greater?
I could wade in here and talk about economic precariousness and the impact that may have on Jungian libido and animal magnetism, but I’d simply be trying to mould the low-sex phenomenon into my own talking point.
Very solid correlations do exist, however, when we look at the reproduction and contraception data from the same study. The proportion of women aged 16–54 who have had an abortion in their lifetime rose from 16% to 21% over the past decade, an increase mainly seen among those over 35 (the persistent idea of teenagers driving unwanted pregnancy rates has been wrong for years). What is really stark is that the rise is heavily determined by deprivation – MSI Reproductive Choices (formerly Marie Stopes) went so far as to state that the rise is accounted for entirely by women without a university degree (rising from 16% to 25%), and/or living in the most deprived areas (rising from 14% to 26%).
MSI’s deputy medical director, Dr Sarah Salkeld, underlined to me that, while Natsal wouldn’t ask why, MSI legally has to ask women why they want an abortion, so these statistics come as no surprise to them at all. It’s not only that larger families have become an impossibility with the cost of living crisis. Everything has become more difficult; if you struggle to find a GP appointment, still less a walk-in service, and your work isn’t flexible and your caring responsibilities won’t wait, the unwanted pregnancy itself is an economic effect.
I have been pro-choice all my life, never more so than after I had my (much loved) children, and realised the truth of how they shred your self-determination. So I’m staggered to find myself saying that these abortion numbers, rising in a very specific demographic, are not OK. This is not reproductive choice: it’s a eugenics programme quietly imposed by energy bills and childcare costs.
- Zoe Williams is a Guardian columnist
