A short disclaimer before we get things started — if you're hoping for more sandpaper revelations, then this story isn't the one for you.
Usman Khawaja's 430-page autobiography, Sabr, has dominated the news cycle in Australia since its release earlier this month.
Unfortunately for Khawaja, virtually none of the discourse following the release has actually been about Sabr, the theme that has been the staple of not only his cricketing career, but his life.
You can argue that perhaps he should have seen that coming, and maybe he did, but the reaction remains disappointing.
Sabr is an Arabic word used in Urdu, and other languages across the Muslim world. The closest translation in the English language is patience, although it is also related to endurance, perseverance and persistence. It is one of the key tenets of Islam, the faith Khawaja devoutly follows
While Khawaja's book dedicates an entire chapter to the infamous sandpaper scandal from Australia's 2018 tour of South Africa, when you actually read the book, you realise the whole episode is a very small part of his story.
That so much of the response to Khawaja's book has been centred around sandpaper rather than his actual story shows that Khawaja will once again be forced to summon up a healthy dose of sabr.
The reaction also shows that despite all its progress when it comes to multiculturalism and the understanding of it, Australia still has a long way to go.
While Khawaja may be disappointed at the response, he will not be surprised at it. He has needed to practise sabr his whole life, well before he'd faced a ball for Australia.
Throughout Khawaja's story, you are able to see a young man who matures and deals with every single setback better than the previous one. Sabr isn't something that you master once and then it sits like a trophy on a mantelpiece. Rather, it is something you practise constantly and, as Khawaja constantly mentions in his book, it is incredibly tough to keep doing.
Khawaja never professes to deal with any issue perfectly, nor does he consider himself a perfect human, but it becomes evident that he is ever progressing.
There are several occasions throughout his career when Khawaja butts heads with someone, only to form strong relationships with them later on. Darren Lehmann, Australia's coach during the sandpaper scandal, is notably one of those characters.
One of the big questions surrounding Khawaja's sandpaper chapter from his critics has been: "Why now? Why not let sleeping dogs lie?"
Once you read his entire story, it is obvious that living with a lie over the past eight years became entirely impossible for Khawaja to do, especially when it involved seeing two of his closest cricketing mates, Steve Smith and David Warner, taking the blame for something he himself was also complicit in.
Throughout the journey, Khawaja constantly fights the same old stereotypes us South Asians deal with, as he is called lazy and selfish among other things. They are the same stereotypes that have been thrust upon him once again in the wake of him sharing his story.
Whether you like it or not, Khawaja is undoubtedly one of this country's most important cricketers.
Modern-day Australia is a cultural melting pot, not simply white, and Khawaja is the perfect representation of it.
Khawaja is unashamedly a Pakistani-born Muslim, but he is a very modern-day Aussie despite not being born here.
This is a man who never goes a day without doing his five prayers, but is also happy to sledge Hollywood actress Dakota Johnson on a call with her then-partner, Coldplay's Chris Martin, about "yanks" not knowing anything about cricket. You don't get more Aussie than sledging a Hollywood star.
He also mimics LeBron James and Snoop Dogg with his celebrations after scoring centuries and calls himself the "People's Champ" like kids around the country might after achieving similar feats.
That Khawaja is still the only cricketer of South Asian descent to have enjoyed a solid career for Australia is an indictment on the sport's pathways in this country.
Cricket Australia needs only look at the multicultural make-up of some of Australia's other national teams — the Socceroos, the Matildas, the Wallabies or the Kangaroos — to see there is a serious representation problem. There is a reason why those teams are much more universally loved than the men's Test team currently is.
It can also look within its own sport at the likes of South Africa, England and New Zealand and understand that the answer simply cannot be that Australia's South Asian diaspora happens to be less talented at cricket than its counterpart in other countries.
Khawaja has often spoken about not supporting the Australian team because its players didn't look like him, but rather looked like the guys bullying him at school as he grew up.
While his representation was important for the South Asian community throughout Khawaja's career, the Aussie team still isn't really fully loved, even with him in it.
The dream scenario for a lot of us when watching Australia during Khawaja's career was for him to make a century but Australia to be all out for 200 in the very same innings.
Khawaja's hope is that in time, more representation allows for all of us in the South Asian community to fall in love with the Australian team, just as he did in the late 90s.
He, along with Pat Cummins and other veterans, have been pivotal in the culture of the team changing and being more accepting and accessible to those who aren't white Australians.
Khawaja may no longer be on our TV screens, but he has paved the way for other South Asian cricketers to represent Australia. The hope is that their path to the Test side is much easier than what he encountered.
Whether that is in this generation of players or in future generations remains to be seen.
That is ultimately the purpose of him releasing his book, not getting royalties after "snitching" about Sandpapergate.
If you're wondering what is actually the most important part of the book if not the sandpaper chapter, Khawaja actually tells you.
It is chapter 20, titled: "Gratitude, mindfulness, patience."
That is what Usman Khawaja's legacy is. Not sandpaper.
