Right now, the prime minister faces a choice. Does he stand with Jimmy Barnes and the Working Class Man or does he stand with Big AI?
This week, some of the biggest names in Australian music, publishing, journalism and art have thrown down the gauntlet to the government. From Paul Kelly to Anna Funder and Peter Garrett, household names and Australian icons are all singing from the same song sheet.
Their message is simple: Australia, and our culture, is not AI’s to take.
Australia’s copyright protections must apply to everyone, even the world’s biggest tech companies. The stories, photographs and books that make Australia are not free building blocks for the world’s wealthiest corporations. Big AI wants access to the world’s vast creative library to train their models. They’re lobbying the Albanese Labor government for loopholes in copyright laws, so they can scrape and use other people’s hard work without having to pay or even ask for permission.
Big AI is dangling the promise of a seat at the table of the global AI boom, in exchange for unfettered access to our cultural treasures and a free pass to avoid the basic notion of property rights.
What this is really about is the need for Big AI to build datacentres in Australia. The enormous, power-hungry warehouses are packed with servers that power AI models like ChatGPT. These centres feed their machines vast amounts of data, including Australian music, books, journalism, culture and knowledge.
But here’s the thing: big tech is already building datacentres in Australia. They want our land, our water and our energy. The copyright debate only exists because Labor is desperate to offer yet another incentive to set up shop here.
Australian artists, creatives and journalists shouldn’t be the sacrificial lamb to big AI’s slaughter.
The price big AI wants us to pay is to undermine more than a century of copyright protections that give Australian artists control over their own work and the ability to earn a living.
Get this wrong, and it won’t just be our favourite musicians who pay the price.
Journalists risk having their reporting swallowed by AI models that reproduce their work and compete with their publications. Artists, authors, performers and screenwriters’ work could be used to churn out AI slop at the expense of their livelihoods.
First Nations artists are already having their work stolen and sold on platforms like Temu without permission or payment. It is outrageous that the world’s oldest continuing living culture should have to compete with computer servers.
Any Australian who has shared a photo, story or artwork online could have it scraped and fed into commercial AI systems without their knowledge or consent.
Make no mistake – for AI companies, this is a global play with big consequences. Any copyright loopholes Australia creates will undermine protections worldwide. Big AI will have a backdoor to scrape and steal cultural works everywhere, using Australian datacentres. Other countries have read the writing on the wall, strengthening copyright laws to protect their own culture and stop big AI exploiting their laws. Australia can’t afford to be the weak link in the chain.
We risk becoming the Cayman Islands of AI, a safe haven for big tech – a global pariah riddled with loopholes where AI can exploit culture and knowledge and dodge the laws that protect it.
The prime minister has promised to do the right thing by Australian creators. But words are cheap. The test is whether his laws guarantee consent, credit and compensation, rather than an opt-out system that forces artists, freelancers and small newsrooms to chase billion-dollar tech companies to protect their rights.
Australian music, films, and art are our memories, our connections to each other, our heartbreaks and our identity – the story of our nation. It’s not a bargaining chip for big AI and they are not AI’s to take.
- Sarah Hanson-Young is a Greens Senator for South Australia
