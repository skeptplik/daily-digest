For many people, McMansions are an eyesore, but for the protagonist of Keepsake County, a renowned burglar, they are an enticing opportunity. Prowling at purple-hued dusk, this unfussy thief is just as likely to steal a half-eaten slice of pizza or a naff ceramic ornament as he is an expensive set of golf clubs. It’s a sad, desperate way to make a living, a sense compounded by what this fledgling kleptomaniac carries his loot away in: a plastic shopping bag.
Countless works have taken us to breadline America, from classic novel East of Eden to acclaimed TV crime drama Mare of Easttown. But Keepsake County is no prestige examination of the economic underbelly of the US. Rather, it is a stealing simulator with eccentric Adult Swim-style art and a darkly absurdist sense of humour that is expressed in its moment-to-moment action. For example, when I set off the alarm at a glinting modernist abode, I had to quickly dodge the gunshot fired my way by its maniacal owner, and as I scarpered across the surrounding grassy verge, I grabbed underpants off the clothesline just to make the long drive through the night worth it. I was reminded of Shameless (the US version) as much as Steinbeck.
Keepsake County also takes inspiration from the popular extraction shooter genre, which sees you dropping into a map and trying to get out with sackfuls of loot. But you don’t kill anyone in Keepsake County – you merely tie pesky proprietors up with bits of lace lifted from old trainers. Nor do you use your hard-earned stash to craft upgrades. Rather, it’s straight to the pawn shop with each haul.
Quickly, I found a pleasant groove, slipping into houses and robbing each owner blind. I would find an appropriate point of entry, fiddle with the locks, and then get to thieving work, carefully evading various residents such as teen babysitters and retired boomers. Over time, as I discovered more elements of lead character Samuel’s debt-ridden backstory, the break-ins began to feel a little more tragic, as if the only way he might experience a more comfortable life is as a voyeur in these massive houses. The loading screens – shots of empty, pastoral countryside as if viewed from the side window of his dilapidated car – reinforce the melancholy. What a hard, lonely existence.
The story is artfully told, economically relayed, simultaneously a satire of nowheresville suburban culture and an earthy, acid-tinged love letter to the midwest (and would surely make a nice complement to whatever Grand Theft Auto VI has to say about the US in a few days). Yet you can feel where the tiny DIY team, Forward Instinct, has compromised. I came across repeated lines of dialogue from a diner waitress, while the visual distinctions of each region (including plains and backwoods) do little to impact how the game plays. And while aspects of the levels, including item locations and building architecture, are remixed every time you load them up, the action can begin to feel a little one note, as if you’re stealing from the same house over and over.
But Keepsake County also derives a great deal of bite from such monotony. You come to realise that Samuel has no chance of paying off his medical debts and legal fees. Financially, his situation is hopeless. And so, with little possibility of getting into the black, these robberies take on a nihilistic bent – our antihero has nothing left to lose. Yet Keepsake County is not wholly despairing even as it shows how undignified day-to-day survival in America can be. There is a youthful, delinquent charm to this game: one moment you are bribing a guard dog with a juicy bone, the next you are stealing homeware from someone so comparatively rich they might never even notice it’s gone.
