Earlier this month, Yodo1 Games announced a strategic digital licensing partnership with the American firm AMC Global Media, which is behind television series such as The Walking Dead and Anne Rice's Interview with the Vampire. Yodo1 is tasked with finding and supporting Chinese game developers and publishers in order to create video games based on AMC's franchises.
There has been much talk in recent years of how Chinese games are starting to make huge inroads into the North American and European markets, through blockbuster titles like Genshin Impact. But the opportunity also goes the other way: Human: Fall Flat, for example, which has now reached 60 million players, owes a large part of its success to its popularity in China..
Yodo1 has already worked with big Western firms like Hasbro and Netflix, and the firm's co-founder and co-CEO, Henry Fong, talked to GamesIndustry.biz about the latest deal with AMC, the challenges of translating IP for Chinese audiences, and why firms should consider licensing their IP.
Can you tell me more about Yodo1? How did it start, and where are you based?
Yodo1 was founded in Beijing in 2011 primarily as a mobile game publisher. Over time, we recognised a greater opportunity to connect China's domestic gaming industry with global entertainment brands. This grew to the point that in 2019, we began developing our Brands in Games business, focused on bringing established IP into games through licensing partnerships, collaborations, and original game development. Today, we work with major entertainment companies, including Hasbro, Netflix, AMC Networks, Paramount, and others.
Although our roots are in China, we're now a fully remote, global company, with team members across Asia, Europe, and the Americas. That international presence is particularly important because we are working across very different markets, business cultures and licensing environments.
What kinds of companies are you approaching about licensing IP?
We work with a broad range of IP owners, but we primarily focus on entertainment brands, which means film and television studios, animation companies, gaming publishers, toy companies, and other major consumer brands.
Our partnerships cover different business models. Sometimes we're bringing an entertainment franchise into an existing successful mobile game through a limited-time collaboration. Other times, we're exploring standalone games built entirely around a particular IP.
It's important to say that our involvement is much deeper than simply acting as a conduit between licensee and licensor. Our key value is our experience in both the Chinese and Western markets and our ability to bring together the best aspects of both. So for every project, we want to develop a deeper understanding of a franchise's priorities, upcoming releases, target audiences and long-term ambitions. This allows us to proactively identify the right gaming opportunities, whether that's a limited-time collaboration, a new game, or a broader partnership involving multiple developers.
What challenges have you faced when it comes to The Walking Dead and Interview with the Vampire? Are Chinese developers familiar with these franchises? Has there been a steep learning curve?
The Walking Dead actually has a big fanbase in China, which has grown ever since the streaming platform Youku launched it in 2012. In fact, I think it was the most-watched show on the whole platform. Plus, its themes of survival, zombies, post-apocalyptic environments, and human conflict translate naturally into genres that are already extremely popular among Chinese developers, particularly strategy and survival games. We've seen this through previous collaborations, including Puzzles & Survival and Last Shelter: Survival.
Chinese consumers aren't already familiar with Interview with the Vampire, but that gives us an opportunity to bring something fresh and new to gamers. It's a sophisticated franchise with rich storytelling, complex characters, and a very specific aesthetic, so it's about explaining what makes Interview with the Vampire distinctive, from its character relationships and gothic atmosphere to the emotional depth of its storytelling. So while they may not know this specific IP, the universal themes within it are what gamers love.
Another important aspect of what Yodo1 brings to our relationship with IP holders is the work we do to keep franchises top of mind within the Chinese developer ecosystem, regardless of whether a particular brand is active within China. Many domestic developers operate games with large international audiences, so players are often interested in global entertainment franchises. That means there's an opportunity to keep developers engaged, informed, and excited about brands that may not necessarily have the same level of local visibility, but still have strong appeal among their global players.
One of the most important lessons is that brand recognition alone is not enough. Using a known IP is often seen as a shortcut to success, but we've learned that part of our role must be to translate the creative identity of the franchise into something developers can understand, execute, and monetise without losing what makes that IP special, and above all, to ensure the use of the IP feels authentic rather than forced.
Why should companies consider giving their IP to Chinese developers?
I think one of the biggest misconceptions is that licensing to a Chinese developer means you're only targeting the Chinese market. Yes, China has become a big market in its own right, but Chinese studios are behind many of the big global hits – especially on mobile, but now increasingly they are having global success on PC and console too.
To put the influence of Chinese game companies into context, according to Sensor Tower's December 2025 data, 33 of the world's top 100 mobile game publishers by revenue were Chinese companies, collectively accounting for approximately 34.6% of the revenue generated by those top 100 publishers. In 2025 alone, Chinese-developed games generated approximately US$20.5 billion in overseas revenue, with the United States, Japan, and South Korea among their largest international markets.
The other important point is that Chinese developers have developed substantial expertise in live operations, player engagement, monetisation, and running games over long periods. So for IP holders, it's not just about accessing China, but instead accessing some of the most commercially experienced game developers in the global market.
What kinds of IP would work in terms of being transferred to a Chinese development team? And what wouldn't work well?
It's less about what IP they can work with than about whether the IP fits the game's mechanics, audience, visual style, and commercial objectives. For example, action, science fiction, fantasy, horror, and survival franchises can translate very well into strategy, RPG, or survival games because they often feature recognisable characters, environments, weapons, and other elements that naturally integrate into gameplay. But we've also seen successful opportunities with lifestyle, fashion, and character-driven brands, particularly in casual, simulation, and merge games. A romantic drama franchise, for example, might not be an obvious match for a combat game, but it could work extremely well in a narrative-driven or makeover experience.
This is also where data and market intelligence become increasingly important. We've developed tools such as IPverse to bring a more structured, data-informed approach to IP licensing. By combining insights into entertainment franchises, game genres, developer profiles, and market opportunities with our team's industry expertise, we can make better-informed decisions about which brands and games have the strongest potential to work together.
Where things become more challenging is when the collaboration requires a significant change to the core identity of either the IP or the game. Some properties also depend heavily on specific actors, performances, music, or complex storytelling, which can introduce additional licensing and creative restrictions. So, to avoid these complications, we try to avoid situations where a brand is simply added to a game just because it's famous.
How are you accommodating China's censorship laws?
As you know, China has a very specific regulatory environment for games, so this is something that has to be considered early on in any licensing discussion. But equally, a game developed in China doesn't automatically mean that every collaboration will launch in China.
For games that will be published in mainland China, we need to assess local regulatory requirements, including content restrictions and game approvals, and make any necessary modifications. In some cases, you can have a game made for a global launch, with the China version having a few differences. Really, it's not all that different to some Western countries that have specific rules – we can always go back to the famous example of Carmageddon, where the German version needed to change all the NPCs to robots in order to get approval.
What kind of deal do you offer IP holders?
Because each collaboration is essentially unique, we don't believe in a one-size-fits-all licensing model. The commercial structure depends on the IP, the developer, the type of game, the length of the collaboration, and the territories involved – plus the scope and risks involved.
From our perspective, the most important thing is creating a commercially sustainable deal for both sides. A successful partnership needs to generate meaningful value for the IP holder while giving the developer enough flexibility and commercial incentive to create a strong experience for players. Ideally, we want to build partnerships that can generate value over time, rather than one-off deals.
Again, this is something that's becoming more common as games become a bigger part of a brand's broader strategy, rather than simply another licensing category. I think it's positive when brands create new ways for fans to interact with characters and stories, introduce the franchise to new audiences, and support future entertainment releases. But to do this well, you will always need to work with partners who understand how to creatively leverage IP in ways gamers will love.
In practical terms, how does Yodo1 handle the transfer of IP? What does that look like in terms of support, translation, and so on?
This is probably one of the areas where Yodo1 brings the most value.
We don't just introduce a developer to an IP owner and leave them to negotiate on their own. We act as a bridge between both parties throughout the collaboration process, from the initial opportunity to the actual launch. It usually begins with understanding the IP holder's objectives and the developer's requirements. We then evaluate the brand's compatibility with the game, review the proposed creative integration, and align the commercial structure. Once a project moves forward, our team helps coordinate the licensing process, creative reviews, approvals, and communication between the different stakeholders.
Translation is certainly part of that, but it's not just language translation or localisation. It's also about translating expectations into player and market opportunities. Entertainment companies and game developers often operate very differently. A film or television studio might focus on character authenticity, franchise continuity, and brand protection, while a game developer will be thinking about player engagement, technical feasibility, development schedules and monetisation. Both perspectives are valid, but they don't always naturally align.
One way we've addressed this is by establishing a structured internal creative review and approval process. We work closely with IP holders to understand their brand guidelines, creative requirements, and approval expectations, and then translate that knowledge into practical resources for developers, including brand bibles, creative guidance, and learnings from previous collaborations.
This means that by the time a developer's creative materials are formally submitted to an IP holder, our team has already reviewed the assets, identified potential issues, and worked with the developer to bring the materials as close as possible to what the brand is looking for, avoiding unnecessary revisions, reducing back-and-forth, and making the process more efficient for both developers and IP holders.
Ultimately, we see ourselves as more than a licensing intermediary. We're building the expertise, processes, and infrastructure that allow global entertainment brands and game developers to work together more effectively, protecting the creative integrity of the IP while making collaborations commercially viable and operationally manageable.
