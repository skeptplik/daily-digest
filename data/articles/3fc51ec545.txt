Speaking to Euronews’ The World Conversation, Kim Aris described his relief at hearing his mother’s voice after five years, called for tougher sanctions enforcement and defended her record on the Rohingya genocide ruling.
Aung San Suu Kyi's son says he was relieved to hear his mother's voice for the first time in five years but remains concerned about her condition after Myanmar's military moved the 81-year-old to house arrest at an undisclosed location, questioning whether the gesture was genuine.
Kim Aris, who has campaigned for his mother's release since the military seized power in 2021, speaking at universities and meeting with governments to press for action, spoke to Euronews' The World Conversation after the call — the first contact the family had had with her in five years.
"Obviously I was very happy to hear her voice and it was a huge relief to actually know that she's alive and well after all this time," Aris said, adding that for two or three years the family had not even known whether she was alive.
Aris, who holds British citizenship through his late father, academic Michael Aris, said he had run a proof-of-life campaign last year, and that the Red Cross visited her earlier this year and confirmed she was alive.
The call was arranged after the Myanmar embassy in London contacted the British Foreign Office the week before it took place, Aris said. It was recorded and monitored, and the two kept to family matters. His mother's first question was whether he had been looking after himself.
"They call this house arrest, but I do not call this a house arrest. She's not in her own home. She's allowed no access to any sort of media, as far as I'm aware," Aris said.
He said she had been held in solitary confinement in a prison in the capital Naypyidaw, with access only to military doctors and no contact with anyone outside military or police personnel.
He said the military was using the transfer to project an image of moderation and urged the international community to maintain pressure rather than re-engage.
"The military have been able to circumnavigate these sanctions for all of this time, and they're still receiving the money and the weaponry that they need to suppress their people," he told Euronews.
No return to civilian rule yet, Aris says
Suu Kyi led the National League for Democracy to a landslide election victory in 2020 and served as Myanmar's de facto head of government. The military refused to recognise the result, seized power in February 2021 and has held her ever since.
The coup triggered a civil war in which pro-democracy forces and ethnic armed groups have taken control of large parts of the country.
More than 4 million people are internally displaced and a further 2 million have fled abroad, Aris said, in part due to conscription laws introduced by the military.
Min Aung Hlaing, who led the coup, was elected president in April by the military-dominated parliament. Aris dismissed suggestions that the country was returning to civilian rule.
"The so-called elections that they held at the end of last year or the beginning of this year were so far from being free and fair," he said.
Asked why young people had taken up arms despite Suu Kyi's commitment to non-violence, Aris said the military had left them no alternative.
"When they came out in peaceful protests at the start of the coup, they were shot in the streets," he said.
He called on the military to stop bombing civilians and release all political prisoners as conditions for peace. "Without those steps, I can't see peace coming back to Burma soon," he said.
'Trying to hold together a very fragile democracy'
Last Thursday, the International Court of Justice ruled for the first time that Myanmar had committed genocide against its Rohingya Muslim minority during a military crackdown in 2017.
The court ordered Myanmar to pay reparations, ensure the safe return of displaced Rohingya and prosecute those responsible.
On Monday, the UK formally recognised the genocide, sanctioned five senior Myanmar military officers and announced plans to restrict aviation fuel supplies to the country.
Min Aung Hlaing has since said his government would reject some of the court's demands, describing the case as a "one-sided accusation".
Suu Kyi appeared before the ICJ in December 2019 to defend Myanmar against the genocide allegations.
Aris defended the decision, saying his mother established the Kofi Annan Commission on the Rakhine State on the second day of her term in office, before the issue drew wider international attention.
"They say that she went to The Hague to defend the military. That's just not the case," he said. "She was trying to hold together a very fragile democracy, which the military could have dismantled at any point, which they have done now."
Five years after the coup, Aris said he believed his mother could still play a role in rebuilding Myanmar.
"It's very hard to know what the situation in Burma will be like in 10 years. I hope that the civil war will have finished and democracy will have been re-established," Aris said.
"And whilst I know my mother will always be involved one way or another, I hope she's able to take a step back and look after herself a bit more."
