The governor of the Bank of England has warned that banks could look to pass on the £1.5bn cost of Labour’s increase to tax on their profits, a move that would put further pressure on household finances.
John Healey, the chancellor, used his first budget last month to temporarily increase the bank corporation tax surcharge from 3% to 6% for a 15 month period from January, with the proceeds to be used to fund “breathing space” measures for households.
While the level of the increase was less than banks had feared – there had been wide speculation that Healey would reinstate the 8% tax level that was in place until 2023 – the Bank governor, Andrew Bailey, said he would not be surprised if they looked to pass the costs on to customers to “protect their earnings”.
“It’s a market economy, so these are decisions banks will have to take,” Bailey said in an interview with the Times. “You could understand that they will look to pass it on because they will be targeting a rate of return.
“How successful they will be depends on all sorts of things: market conditions, competition within the industry and so on. It wouldn’t be surprising to find out that they will start by trying to do that.”
The JP Morgan chief executive, Jamie Dimon, has said that he intended to find ways to offset the impact of the tax increase on bank profits.
Speaking at an event in London last week, Dimon said he would seek to claw back the increase and was “setting an example so that [governments] understand the trade-offs of these unfair penalties”.
Healey’s intervention came as households face mounting pressure on finances. This week, the energy regulator for Great Britain said gas and electricity costs will soar by 26% in January, lifting average annual bills to more than £2,000.
House prices fell at their steepest rate in more than three years in October, as potential homebuyers find themselves prices out amid rising mortgage rates.
On Thursday, the Bank of England warned that 6.5 million households are facing higher mortgage costs in the coming years as their deals come up for renewal at significantly higher interest rates.
More than 400,000 UK homeowners will face a monthly increase of £500-a-month over the next three years.
Earlier this month the Bank of England’s monetary policy committee voted to increase the base rate from 3.75% to 4%, the first rise in more than three years.
