U.S. jury finds Chinese tech giant Huawei guilty of conspiring to steal trade secrets
Company acquitted of most serious charge of criminal racketeering conspiracy
A U.S. jury convicted Chinese tech giant Huawei Technologies on Tuesday of conspiracy to steal trade secrets and bank fraud as it grew into one of the world's biggest suppliers of gear for wireless telecommunications networks.
However, jurors acquitted the company of the most serious charge it faced: engaging in a criminal racketeering conspiracy. The charge typically is used in organized crime and drug cases, but prosecutors had used it to accuse Huawei of operating in an illicit fashion to steal intellectual property from American companies.
The verdict followed a lengthy trial in which U.S. prosecutors argued that Huawei repeatedly stole technological secrets from competitors and partners in the U.S. and elsewhere.
Huawei was also accused of doing business in countries subject to U.S. sanctions, including Iran and North Korea, and making false statements to U.S. government officials and banks as part of an effort to hide its misdeeds.
Lawyers for the company said U.S. investigators had used isolated episodes of wrongdoing by a few of Huawei's more than 200,000 employees to spin a false narrative about a grand conspiracy that didn't actually exist.
Huawei attorney Brian Heberlig said the company would appeal the convictions but saw the racketeering conspiracy acquittal as vindication.
"That was the only count that brought all of the allegations together," and the jury rejected it, he said outside court, saying that the verdict "repudiated the central government narrative in the case."
'Economic bullying'
It's unclear what effect the verdict might have on Huawei's global fortunes. The company hasn't been allowed to sell equipment to U.S. carriers for years because of security concerns. It has also been blacklisted by other Western allies, such as Canada and Britain.
But that hasn't stopped it from doing a robust business elsewhere. Its smartphones and other telecom equipment are sold globally. More recently, it has expanded into chipmaking as the boom in artificial intelligence fuels demand for microprocessors, becoming an important alternative in China to chips made by companies such as Nvidia.
The U.S. announced its criminal charges against Huawei in early 2019 and later expanded the allegations.
Chinese officials said the U.S. has engaged in "economic bullying" and improperly used national security as a pretext for oppressing Chinese companies.
'You can't steal a secret that isn't a secret'
The indictment against Huawei accused it of a long pattern of outright technology thefts. Huawei, prosecutors said, once sold internet routers containing source code copied from a competitor. In another episode, a person working for Huawei slipped into a competitor's booth at a Chicago trade show to surreptitiously photograph the circuits inside another company's networking device, the indictment said.
Prosecutors said that on another occasion, a Huawei worker removed a robotic arm from a competitor's lab so it could be photographed and measured.
The victim companies included Cisco Systems, Motorola Solutions, T-Mobile, Quintel USA and Fujitsu, according to court records.
"The tone was set at the top by Huawei leaders and executives who themselves engaged in criminal conduct, like lying to Congress, lying to banks, violating U.S. sanctions and trying to interfere with the very criminal investigation that led to this case," prosecutor Meredith Arfa told the jury at the trial.
In his closing argument to jurors, Huawei's lawyer said prosecutors leaned on "innuendo and facts ripped out of context," and testimony from ex-employees "with a bone to pick," to pursue a company that had relied on "hard work, innovation and competition" to succeed.
"Huawei didn't steal its way to success," Heberlig said, noting that much of the material the U.S. alleges was stolen was already public.
"You can't steal a secret that isn't a secret," he said.
He said Huawei paid billions of dollars in licensing fees over the years, with 75 per cent of that money going to U.S. companies, and had reprimanded or fired employees found to have engaged in wrongdoing.
Heberlig noted that lines of Cisco source code turned up in Huawei software more than two decades ago. He said when the source-code copying was discovered, Huawei recalled routers sold in the U.S. and offered to replace them with Cisco routers and created a "compliance culture that permeates Huawei today."

U.S. prosecutors had originally also brought criminal charges against Huawei's chief financial officer, Meng Wanzhou, accusing her of misleading HSBC bank about the company's business dealings in Iran.
Meng, the daughter of Huawei's founder, was arrested in Canada in 2018, but ultimately was allowed to return to China in 2021 in a prisoner swap that freed two Canadians held by China. As part of the deal, the U.S. dropped its case against her.
The trial in New York overlapped with a summit meeting between U.S. President Donald Trump, China's Xi Jinping and Russia's Vladimir Putin in the technology-centric city of Shenzhen, China.
