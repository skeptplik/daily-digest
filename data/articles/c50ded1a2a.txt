Recent Outages Have Economic Causes
This essay originally appeared in The Guardian. It’s an expanded version of the one I posted as “We Get the Infrastructure We Pay For.”
It’s been a bad week for our computerized infrastructure. We’ve seen outages at Microsoft, the FAA, a handful of banks, United Airlines, an unnamed classified NSA network, and Apple. People should be forgiven for suspecting a coordinated cyberattack, a common cause, or at least a conspiracy. But there is none.
The incidents are unrelated. They’re not the result of any cyberattack, coordinated or not. And, even more surprising, they don’t seem to be the result of AI. (We can’t say anything about the NSA; we’ll probably never know the details of that one.) It’s really just a series of unfortunate coincidences.
None of them should surprise, though. Our computerized infrastructure is both vulnerable and fragile.
Much of it is old and has been incrementally patched and upgraded over the decades. A surprising amount of it is maintained by hobbyists, without any recompense. There often aren’t good backup systems. And more and more of it is centralized, meaning that individual failures have cascading consequences across many different aspects of our lives and society.
A lot of our tech infrastructure just barely works. And when it fails, it can fail badly. It’s the very opposite of resilience.
There are a lot of technological reasons why this is true, but they are all downstream from the economic ones. The technologies we rely on are built and maintained within our economic system—and that’s where the core problems lie.
Security, safety, resilience, redundancy and recoverability are all essential parts of reliable technology, but they are all costs. More importantly, they are costs that don’t generate near-term profits.
Modern shareholder capitalism, and private equity in particular, prioritizes near-term profits over everything else. This squeezes all apparent inefficiencies—and, additionally, all other positive intangibles—out of a company in pursuit of those profits.
Market competition, essential for the forces that nudge companies to provide better services, is another perceived inefficiency. Monopolization removes it in pursuit of near-term profits.
From the perspective of a quarterly budget, spending on reliability measures is an inefficiency. They’re expenses that don’t generate revenue. They feel wasteful until something bad happens.
But, if you’re lucky, you can push the costs of failure on to society, further increasing profits. So, we get stranded in airports when planes don’t fly, or have to deal with the fallout when we can’t withdraw money from our bank accounts.
The effects of those economic pressures are brittle systems, with single points of failure and cascading failure modes. They’re more susceptible to cyberattack—both human and AI-fueled—and more susceptible to random mistakes.
The more we allow unregulated capitalism to privatize gains while socializing risks, the more we should expect these sorts of failures.
Upgrading our computerized systems, increasing their reliability, removing single points of failure, and making them more resilient are all technical problems. But they won’t happen until we fix the political and economic ones.
We need to change the economic incentives that make it profitable to operate these systems just at the edge of functionality, and to push the costs of failure on to all of us. Fixing that requires regulation—with enforcement and meaningful fines—and stringent antitrust enforcement.
But while that’s all easy to say, it’s much harder to implement—especially in the US where monied interests have such a tight hold on the legislative agenda. Until this changes, we should expect lots more of these sorts of failures in the future.
