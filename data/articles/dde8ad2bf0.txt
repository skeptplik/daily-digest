A burst of violence in Mexico's Sinaloa state after authorities captured cartel boss Fausto Isidro Meza Flores, known as "El Chapo Isidro", left at least 11 people dead, authorities have said.
Meza Flores, one of the FBI's 10 most wanted fugitives, was captured and flown to Mexico City on Saturday (November 21, 2026) after a heavy firefight between Mexican forces and cartel gunmen overnight left residents of the city of Guasave terrorised and trucks and businesses burning.
Mexico's Defense Cabinet on Saturday afternoon (November 21) said seven alleged cartel gunmen and four Mexican soldiers were killed.
Flores climbed the cartel ranks to lead the Beltrán-Leyva cartel and later became the leader of his own group, the Meza-Flores Transnational Criminal Organisation. The United States had a $5 million reward offer for information leading to his arrest, and he was wanted for trafficking drugs to the U.S. and illegally carrying firearms.
Mexico's government said in a report early Saturday (November 21) that intelligence from the Trump administration helped authorities capture him, but provided few details about how authorities in Mexico and the United States coordinated to detain Meza Flores.
He was also a key ally to "Los Mayos", a faction of the Sinaloa Cartel, which was designated as a foreign terrorist organisation by the Trump administration last year. His capture represents a blow to the cartel as it fights with another faction for control of the northern Mexican state.
Authorities in the United States and Mexico celebrated the arrest as a security win. U.S. President Donald Trump has pressured Mexico's government to go after criminal groups.
As a fierce firefight settled into a tense calm Saturday, experts warned that picking off capos could set off another wave of violence in a region already caught up in a power struggle between warring cartel factions.
Mexican forces’ move against cartel fuels violence
The capture of Meza Flores and his number 2 in the city of Guasave, Sinaloa, set off a wave of violence when a firefight broke out around 7 p.m. on Friday (November 20), according to residents of the 290,000-person municipality. Soon, military helicopters and drones were flying overhead and opened fire on key locations across the city.
Videos show screaming civilians fleeing gunfire at a baseball game, burning cars and businesses, and circling helicopters shooting at people below. Such shows of force by cartels are common following the capture or killing of leaders.
"The fighting was happening all over Guasave," said Alma Camacho, a Guasave resident and local journalist. "It spread throughout the city."
Mexican authorities said they used "stealth and surprise" to arrest Meza Flores, establishing a security perimeter around the building where he was located. After coming under attack with guns and explosives from suspected cartel operatives, armed forces personnel entered a home and detained him. He was later taken by aircraft to Mexico City to appear before prosecutors.
Video shared by authorities shows heavily armed men in camouflage escorting a man through a dimly lit house and putting him in the back of a truck. No casualties have been reported so far as a result of the operation.
Because of the violence, the Mexican army and National Guard special forces were unable to extract Meza Flores from Guasave until 4 a.m. on Saturday (November 21), said a federal official who spoke on condition of anonymity because he wasn't authorised to discuss the case.
"Getting him [Meza Flores] out was extraordinary," the official said, describing an intense response by members of the criminal group who maintained at least "10 roadblocks" with burning vehicles on Guasave's streets.
Terror in Sinaloa
Guasave resident and firefighter Paola Sánchez said thousands of people were terrorised late Friday as they took shelter from intense gunfire in grocery stores and other locations.
"Last night, many businesses were burned ... along with cars and trucks," Mr. Sánchez told The Associated Press.
She said the city's firefighters had to take shelter at their station and wait until Saturday morning (November 21) for orders from their superiors to dispatch the first crews to check whether any businesses or vehicles were still burning.
An uneasy calm seemed to settle over the city on Saturday (November 21). Mexican Security Secretary Omar García Harfuch wrote on X on Saturday morning (November 21) that armed forces would remain deployed in Sinaloa state to protect civilians. Authorities didn't provide details on how many people were wounded or killed in the clashes.
Burned out trucks continued to line the freeway leading into the town, blocking roadways on Saturday (November 21). "The aftermath of last night's war still lingers. Burned cars in the streets, several Oxxo stores completely burned down," Mr. Camacho said, referring to a chain of convenience stores in Mexico.
Guasave residents remained "very afraid" because security forces were absent from the streets, Mr. Camacho said.
Capture prompts celebration and warnings
Meza Flores' detention marks another high-profile capture of a cartel leader as Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum faces pressure from Trump to crack down on cartels. Sheinbaum has gone after illegal groups more aggressively than her predecessors, and homicides have dipped significantly in her two years in office.
The U.S. and Mexican governments celebrated the capture on Saturday (November 21), with the DEA commending Mexican authorities.
"Results like these don't happen overnight. They are built on sustained cooperation and trust. This is what partners can accomplish together," U.S. Ambassador Ronald Johnson said in an X post. "We will continue the hunt."
Mexican Security Secretary García Harfuch called the detention "an important step in pacifying Sinaloa". Sheinbaum's government has targeted a number of cartel kingpins, including Jalisco New Generation Cartel leader Nemesio Oseguera Cervantes, known as "El Mencho", who was killed by the military in February.
Mexican security analyst David Saucedo said the capture of Meza Flores marked another case of the governments of Mexico and the U.S. increasingly falling back on a longstanding security strategy that critics warn could fuel more violence in the long run.
The so-called kingpin strategy relies on zeroing in on capos like Meza Flores as a way to combat cartels. But experts say it often triggers violent power disputes among groups, like the one under way in Sinaloa.
"What is evident is this is a product of pressure from the United States," Mr. Saucedo said. "What they do is capture high-profile kingpins — the leaders — but they don't dismantle the entire organisation. They arrested the number 1 and number 2, but the entire network of hit men, street-level drug dealers, businesses and political and police connections remains intact."
