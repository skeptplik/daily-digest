“If the problem looks bad, trust me, we haven’t seen the worst of it yet.”
That was Patrik Frisk, CEO of French “materials regeneration” company Reju. Holding court at a reception at the Vancouver Art Gallery during Textile Exchange’s conference last month, Frisk spoke of a problem the fashion industry hasn’t quite grappled with yet, namely, where does all the stuff it creates go?
Add to that the fact that textile collectors, sorters and recyclers in North America and Europe are struggling with the exponential rise of cheap, low-quality garments—to the point where selling prices no longer cover operational costs—and the entire system is on the verge of a complete and irrevocable breakdown, making what Frisk describes as the “waste gap” an ever-widening chasm.
“The tide gets bigger every year,” he said. “If we wait for it before we start to build some sort of a seawall, we’ll probably be too late.”
On Tuesday, Reju revealed it has been laying the first stones of its own seawall. Behind announcements of full-scale textile-to-textile recycling facilities in Sittard-Geleen, Netherlands; Rochester, N.Y.; and Lacq, France, the company has been working with six mills to develop fabrics that will accelerate the integration of its regenerated polyester into the global textile supply chain: A. Sampaio, RDD Textiles, Swisstex Direct, Tintex Textiles, FOV Fabrics and Utenos Trikotazas.
Each mill has its own specialty. A. Sampaio, based in Portugal, specializes in circular knitted fabrics. Swisstex Direct, which has facilities in Los Angeles and El Salvador, offers an array of sustainable, natural and synthetic fibers. FOV Fabrics, in Sweden, provides full vertical integration for its high-tech woven factories. Utenos Trikotazas, in Lithuania, has more than 50 years of textile experience. Tintex Textiles and RDD Textiles, both in Portugal, hold certifications for recycled-content traceability and environmental compliance.
This, Frisk said, gives Reju access to the manufacturing capabilities necessary to move regenerated polyester from raw material to finished, market-ready fabric at the desired scale. To extend the seawall metaphor, every selected mill Reju is working with adds another stone to its circular textile bulwark. And as plans for Reju’s future Regeneration Hub network grow—it plans roughly 20 “megafactories” worldwide by 2034—so too, Frisk said, will the company’s ability to deliver consistent, high-quality regenerated polyester that “manufacturers and brands can then build on.”
“The gap isn’t technology, and it certainly isn’t good intention,” Frisk said. “It’s a system: collection, sorting, recycling and demand, built at scale with economics that work for everyone in it.”
But Reju can’t be the only one building, he noted. The “pull,” Frisk said, must come from everyone along the supply chain—and brands most of all.
“We’ve lined up everything else: the engineering, the capital, the sites, the partners,” he said, making his pitch to the audience. “There is one piece missing, and that’s most of you. So I’m not here asking for a purchase order. Absolutely not. What I’m asking you to do is just simply sit down and listen, and take a seat in a system that’s being built for you. That’s all I’m asking, because you’re the ones held responsible for the end of life of products you can’t control on your own.”
He paused, then added: “How ready are we to serve the tsunami?”
