Scientists sequenced a whale's entire genome using drones and whale snot
Scientists were able to sequence the entire genome of a whale using samples of its snot. They used sharp flying skills and commercial drones affixed with petri dishes to collect the samples.
SCOTT SIMON, HOST:
We can learn a lot from whale snot, as NPR's Nate Rott reports.
NATE ROTT, BYLINE: Take a deep breath in.
(SOUNDBITE OF INHALING)
ROTT: Hold it. Now, push it out as hard as you can through your nose.
(SOUNDBITE OF WHALE EXHALING)
ROTT: That's essentially what whales do every time they breathe, only they exhale a little more forcefully...
(SOUNDBITE OF WHALE EXHALING)
ROTT: ...Blasting a plume of mist into the air. That spout of water, that poof, scientists call that blow.
ÉADIN O'MAHONY: And that blow is essentially snot.
ROTT: Éadin O'Mahony is a marine mammal ecologist. She's studied a lot of whale snot, and she says it's really not all that unfamiliar.
O'MAHONY: Yeah. It is like your snot and your kids' snot and my snot.
ROTT: A viscous...
O'MAHONY: Clear-colored gooeyness.
ROTT: That's packed with information.
O'MAHONY: You've got things like the bacteria, the viruses, the pathogens.
ROTT: All the little critters that live in the whale's respiratory tract - you've got chemicals and cells, fragments of DNA, a trove of biological data O'Mahony and other scientists collect by using sharp flying skills and small commercial drones.
O'MAHONY: The drones have got sterile petri dishes attached to them, literally Velcroed on. And then as that whale comes up and breathes out...
(SOUNDBITE OF WHALE EXHALING)
O'MAHONY: ...The idea is to swoop through one whale and one whale only's breath, so we're just collecting a representative snot sample from one individual.
ROTT: A sample that they can then analyze to better understand the health of that individual whale and the broader population. This method is not new, but what O'Mahony and colleagues were able to do with it is. By collecting multiple samples from individual whales, they were able to sequence...
O'MAHONY: Millions and millions of genetic markers of that one whale and giving us the whole genome of the whale from a blow sample.
ROTT: It's the first time a whole whale genome has ever been sequenced this way. The findings were detailed in a study published in Molecular Ecology Resources.
Amy Apprill, a scientist at the Woods Hole Oceanographic Institution, was not involved in this study, but she does blow sampling, too, mostly to study bacteria.
AMY APPRILL: Yes. I am very intimately familiar with whale snot.
ROTT: And she says it's exciting to see that scientists were able to use this method to get such a close look at an animal, to look at its genetic health without having to be invasive.
APPRILL: The prior method was basically dart biopsies of animals, and they did a little bit of that in this study to ground truth it, but that is highly invasive.
ROTT: Involving a crossbow to remove a tiny piece of skin and blubber, which can obviously distress the whales, which are already grappling with a lot of stressors.
APPRILL: So this just opens the door.
ROTT: And shows how much information can be gathered from these animals in a safer - albeit snottier - way.
Nate Rott, NPR News.
