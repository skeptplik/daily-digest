Ryan Calais Cameron has proved himself a playwright of stratospheric talent. His previous plays For Black Boys Who Have Considered Suicide When the Hue Gets Too Heavy and Retrograde both streaked their way into the West End. This new one is filled with mighty ambition but does not lift off in the same way.
It is based on what he calls a “true(ish)” story. The figure of Edward Mukuka Nkoloso (Lucian Msamati) is taken from Zambian history. A freedom fighter for the nation’s independence in the 1960s, he set up a national academy for science and trained a group of “afronauts” in the hope of beating the US and the Soviet Union in the race to reach the moon. Nkoloso, a kind of Dead Poets Society inspiration figure here, picks 17-year-old Martha (Ama Qamata) for the mission.
Co-directed by Cameron and Monique Touko, the play introduces us to the characters and sets the scene in a largely comic first half, which is too soft and slow, dissipating tension or intrigue. Soutra Gilmour’s set is beautiful though, with its old-school Star Trek aesthetics: a circular ascending stone ramp with a giant Spaceship Enterprise-like portal, looking out to planetary matter.
Nkoloso is trying to “teach the young to look at the sky”. This is a lovely phrase and idea, which ties his space programme to the story of rebuilding Zambian selfhood, post-independence, especially in the face of derision at this apparently tin-pot project.
But if the play wants to use the metaphor of finding freedom on Earth by taking flight, it does not go far enough in filling in the political detail and the combined concepts of liberation remain vague.
The telling of the story takes precedence over its drama, while character building is also subsumed by it. Grace (Pamela Nomvete) represents elder wisdom and acts as a foil to Simon (Ivanno Jeremiah), who has been shaped by western science (he was sacked from Nasa).
The centre of the drama is unfocused: is it ultimately about Martha who has a romance with fellow afronaut David (Prince Kundai)? Those elements do not develop beyond the generic. Or is it about Nkoloso, who might be a visionary but is also an immoral figure and fantasist? The play can’t, or won’t, resolve the colliding versions of him. Msamati is brilliant as ever, full of charm and conviction, but his complex character is not given enough material in the script to come alive.
It’s an interesting story that heads in many directions but ends up lost and atmosphere-less.
- At Royal Court theatre, London, until 19 December
