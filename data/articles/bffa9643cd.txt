As trains carrying thousands of tonnes of copper from the Democratic Republic of Congo roar through her community, 20-year-old Manuela Julia is not thinking about critical minerals or billion-dollar investments.
She is thinking about getting around more easily.
The railway runs through Catumbela, near the Angolan city of Lobito, on the Atlantic coast.
For Julia, it has made travelling cheaper and more convenient.
"Before, I had to use a taxi, which is much more expensive. Now it is cheaper and easier to travel," she says.
But the railway has ambitions far bigger than improving local transport.
It is part of the Lobito Corridor, a US-backed effort to secure access to Africa's critical minerals and promote regional economic cooperation, which Washington hopes will help bring peace to conflict-torn eastern DR Congo.
Under agreements brokered by US President Donald Trump, DR Congo and neighbouring Rwanda have committed to greater economic cooperation, including more transparent mineral supply chains and regional infrastructure development.
The wider plan envisages linking these initiatives to the railway, which transports minerals from Central Africa to Western markets via Angola, rather than east to China as most do currently.
The hope is that formalising the mineral trade and encouraging investment could help curb illicit markets that have helped finance armed groups and fuel instability.
But can a railway built to move minerals to global markets also deliver peace and prosperity?
The Lobito Corridor links DR Congo's mineral-rich south to Angola's Atlantic coast through the Benguela Railway and the Port of Lobito.
Its importance has grown as demand for copper and cobalt rises, driven partly by the global transition to cleaner energy, as both are critical ingredients for all kinds of electrical components.
"The timing matters," says Johannesburg-based political risk analyst Wellington Muzengeza
"Electrification and the energy transition have increased the strategic importance of copper, cobalt and reliable mineral supply chains."
DR Congo is the world's largest producer of cobalt and one of its biggest copper producers, along with neighbouring Zambia. The US sees the corridor as a means to diversify mineral supplies and reduce reliance on China, which dominates much of the world's processing capacity.
A US-DR Congo strategic partnership identifies the route from the Congolese town of Sakania to Lobito as a priority for mineral exports, while the economic framework agreed by DR Congo and Rwanda envisages connecting regional infrastructure and trade initiatives to the corridor.
The project has attracted over $750m (Â£570m), including funding backed by the US International Development Finance Corporation and the Development Bank of Southern Africa to upgrade railway tracks, signalling systems and trains.
The railway, built in 1902 under Portuguese colonial rule, was originally designed to transport goods and minerals from Central Africa to the Atlantic coast. After years of disruption during Angola's civil war, it was rebuilt and fully reopened in 2015.
The US first got involved in the project under former President Joe Biden but backing has continued under Trump.
At Lobito's mineral terminal, the renewed ambitions are already visible.
When the BBC visited, a train carrying about 12,000 tonnes of copper cathodes from DR Congo had arrived. Workers moved the refined copper sheets into a warehouse before they were loaded on to ships bound for international markets, mostly the US.
The cargo is handled by Lobito Atlantic Railway (LAR), which has a 30-year concession to operate freight services along the corridor and manage the mineral terminal.
Chief operating officer Nicholas Gregoir says freight volumes have more than doubled since the company took over in January 2024.
LAR expects to transport about 200,000 tonnes of copper through the corridor in 2026.
For mining companies, the railway offers a shorter route to international markets.
"The Lobito Corridor is the shortest route for both Zambia and the DRC," to the Atlantic, says KÃ¡tila Vanessa Monteiro da Silva Neto, executive director of the Lobito Port Authority.
Zambia, Africa's second-largest copper producer, also plans to connect its Copperbelt to Lobito through a proposed 830km (515 mile)-railway.
But a more efficient route to market does not automatically translate into economic transformation.
"Joining a corridor is not the same as industrialising," Muzengeza says. "The test is not simply whether Zambia gains another route to the sea. It is whether that route helps build a stronger economy."
Some Angolans believe the railway's greatest value could lie in what it offers businesses and communities beyond mining.
"I call it a corridor of development," says businessman AdÃ©rito Areias.
The railway passes through some of Angola's most productive agricultural regions.
Better transport links could help farmers reach larger markets and reduce losses.
"If there isn't an artery carrying products from the interior to where there is demand, those products simply rot where they are produced," Areias says.
But the challenge is turning that ambition into reality.
The Port of Lobito handled about 1.8 million tonnes of cargo in 2025, of which around 158,000 tonnes was minerals. Its mineral terminal can handle up to 3.6 million tonnes annually.
"The port already has the capacity," says Neto. "What we need now is sufficient railway capacity and rolling stock."
For residents along the route, the benefits have been immediate.
State-owned railway operator Caminho de Ferro de Benguela (CFB), which runs passenger services, carried a record 1.6 million passengers in 2025.
LAR says it directly employs nearly 1,000 people, 98% of them Angolan.
"This job allows me to support my family," says Domingos Canhama, a father of four and a forklift operator at CFB.
"I can put food on the table, maintain a home and help support my father," he says.
Muzengeza argues that the bigger opportunity is developing local businesses in engineering, maintenance, warehousing and agricultural processing.
"Businesses in Angola need access to the railway, not just proximity to it. That means feeder roads, electricity, storage and predictable services," he says.
But can minerals and their movement along the railways line help deliver peace to eastern DR Congo, where the battle for control of mineral resources has fuelled decades of conflict?
Most recently, this has seen the M23 rebel group seize control of many mineral-rich areas.
The Congolese government says neighbouring Rwanda is backing the M23 in order to gain access to DR Congo's mineral wealth. Despite overwhelming evidence, Rwanda denies backing the M23.
The Trump-backed agreements between DR Congo and Rwanda seek to promote transparent mineral supply chains, cross-border investment and regional infrastructure, with the aim of ensuring mineral wealth does not finance armed groups.
"The US wants reliable access to minerals essential to defence, advanced technology and energy systems, while reducing dependence on supply chains in which China has substantial influence," says Muzengeza, the political risk analyst
"Lobito is therefore more than a railway but an infrastructure that can reshape trade routes and commercial power."
At a meeting in Paris in November, DR Congo urged the US to turn its strategic partnership into tangible investments, arguing that success should be measured by completed projects rather than signed agreements.
"We want the partnership to support â local mineral processing, electricity access for households and businesses and peace in the east," said DR Congo's Economy Minister Daniel Mukoko Samba.
Even if more minerals move through formal channels, better infrastructure alone would not resolve the political and security tensions driving the conflict.
There is also the question of who captures the wealth. Much of the copper and cobalt transported from DR Congo through Lobito is destined for processing and manufacturing outside Africa.
While Angola can earn transit fees and create logistics jobs, much of the higher-value activity takes place elsewhere.
"When you export raw materials, you are also exporting jobs and knowledge," Angola's Foreign Affairs Minister TÃ©te AntÃ³nio tells the BBC.
Areias shares that concern.
"We should not export lithium but export batteries," he says, arguing that the corridor should help develop local processing and manufacturing rather than simply accelerate raw material exports.
Back in Catumbela, such debates can feel distant from everyday life.
For Julia, the railway already means cheaper journeys and easier movement.
But its ultimate success will depend on whether it can deliver more than faster mineral exports.
Whether it can also help secure lasting peace in eastern DR Congo, where the minerals are mined, remains the bigger test.
