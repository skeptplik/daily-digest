Since New York City officials postponed the Macy's Thanksgiving Day Parade, a precaution ahead of a storm that brought heavy rain, wind and flooding, hundreds of touring performers who traveled from across the US to perform were thrust into chaos.
"It was jarring," said Ryan Demkovich, director of a Texas high school marching band. After learning the news, he had to scramble to make new arrangements for over 500 people.
Demkovich and his Pearland High School band made the quick decision to launch a fundraiser, reaching $350,000 (Â£267,000) in just a few hours.
Still, some performers reportedly had to skip the parade, a televised, traditional Thanksgiving event - now rescheduled for Saturday.
On Wednesday afternoon, a day before the event, organisers announced the postponement, in part due to concerns around the parade's giant balloons in strong winds.
The balloons, inflated on Wednesday, were then temporarily deflated - along with the spirits of those who journeyed to the city to watch the action.
The move represented the first time the event had been postponed in its 100-year history, although it was suspended altogether during World War II.
Each year, a number of marching bands travel from across the country, from high schools and colleges, to perform at the event. This is considered a huge opportunity, as the parade is watched by millions in person and on TV.
Demkovich described an emotional and exhausting experience, first learning that the parade would be postponed while watching a Broadway performance of Wicked with his band.
"My initial thought was like, 'Oh, crap. We're not going to be able to go'," he said.
"Where are we going to be able to get all the money for this?"
The delay meant unexpected expenses of additional nights in a hotel - and meals - for hundreds of people in the middle of New York City during the holidays.
There were no vacancies to extend their stay at the New York Marriott Marquis in Times Square, so they booked the nearest hotel with availability - across the Hudson River - in New Jersey.
Demkovich said the Pride of Pearland Marching Band posted a fundraiser online and within hours met its fundraising goal.
"The word just spread like wildfire, truly like wildfire," Demkovich said, adding that donations came from over 2,000 people and local businesses.
He called the outpouring of support "very, very overwhelming to think about and comprehend".
"We have a lot to be thankful for this Thanksgiving."
Some groups were not able to maneuver as easily. The University of Alabama's Million Dollar Band is one of those missing out, due to a schedule conflict on Saturday.

Speaking to the BBC on Thursday, the group's director Rickey H Badua described the time pressures. "We are racing to get this done," he explained.
The band is made up of hundreds of musicians from dozens of schools across the state. It travelled almost 5,000 miles (8,000km) to get to New York.
"Getting the news that we got the parade postponed was not easy, and emotional for our students," Badua said.
But the musicians sprang into action quickly, he said. "We come from a very far place," Badua said, adding that "to get so close to the finish line and not show up for it, we couldn't give up that easily".
The response from friends and family had been "incredible", he said.
Badua was hopeful his group would have the same experience he had when he first performed at the parade in 2003, as student drum major.
"The energy is palpable, people are just excited to see a band that is great."
Another musical act, from Illinois, told the BBC on Thursday it needed to raise $175,000 to cover another night in a hotel, additional food, and bus costs back to the city of Champaign.
"Things are going well due to the very generous support of so many amazing human beings," said Professor Barry Houser, director of the University of Illinois Marching Illini.
By Friday, 3,500 donors had stepped up to help the 410 students and their travel party. "This is truly phenomenal," said Houser.
While awaiting the parade, musicians and punters alike have sheltered from the heavy wind and rain brought by the storm to eastern areas of the US.
Hundreds of thousands of homes were without power in the storm's aftermath Friday, and coastal areas had flooded. The National Weather Service (NWS) expects conditions to improve gradually on Friday.
