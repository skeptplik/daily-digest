The small town of Twillford is abuzz with intrigue and excitement. A local duke has died and ladies of a certain standing are determined to make an impression at the funeral. As a newly arrived dressmaker with the only clothing shop in the area, you’re about to get plenty of work.
Thus begins this utterly delightful sim game, in which you talk to customers, gather their style preferences and tailor specific outfits just for them. It could be a suitably respectful funeral frock, with just a hint of style to catch the eye of the duke’s wealthy relatives, or perhaps a work outfit for a farmer who loves bright colours. Once you have your brief, you open your sketchbook and draft a pattern using fabric swatches and a pencil to colour in your design. Need something elegant? How about a strapless bodice. An outfit for the office? A pencil skirt is a good call.
Once you’ve decided on the style, you buy the necessary fabrics, keeping an eye on costs. If someone wants a practical outdoor outfit, you don’t want to be spending 80 credits a metre on ivory velvet, but if they’re hoping to impress a gentleman caller, it’s definitely time to bring out the sunset hibiscus silk. This is the fun part – translating each customer’s personality and demands into colours, shapes and textures. You get keywords to match your outfit against (and you can search your fabrics and accessories to match them), but much of the actual styling is down to you.
With your design sketched out and your customer measured up, you can add a few accessories – diamonds, buttons, a novelty carrot badge. The next stage is choosing the components from a selection of bodices, skirts, sleeves and colours, then you cut out the sections, using as many different materials as you like (though some customers will insist they only want one colour or fabric – the spoilsports).
The hard part is the sewing, and there is a lot of it. Using an old-fashioned machine you must follow the dotted guide line across each section of your dress material, guiding it smoothly and accurately to be as neat as possible. It’s easy on straight edges, but when you have to produce tight curves – with a cuff or elaborate bodice, say – things get tense, even if you turn the sewing machine speed right down. The mermaid skirt is my nemesis – with its long graceful arcs, it is the end-of-level boss of needlework.
Once it’s all done, you can photograph your dress, perhaps make a few alterations and present it to your client. It is devastating – devastating – to get to the end of the process only for the customer to reject your work. An opera singer named Edith Swan asked for a risque outfit suitable for day wear and I opted for a plum silk dress with plunging neckline and pencil skirt, but it failed because I forgot to add a matching brooch. On the other hand, my slightly experimental blue gingham dress with green cotton pleats and undershirt went down a storm with Fiona the framer.
As the game continues, the clients get more interesting and demanding (just wait until you meet Alice), and when you do a good job, you unlock new styles and accessories. When I unlocked tiaras, believe me, it was just as exciting as when I got the Blasphemous Blade in Elden Ring.
Dressmaker was co-designed by an experienced dressmaker, and you get the sense that so much love and experience went into the game: the way the sketchbook is designed, the sounds of scissors snipping through material, the strangely specific and sometimes seemingly contradictory requests from clients. It is certainly a cosy game, set in a comfortably non-specific time period, but there is a sense of commercial realism about it that ensures it works as a business sim; you’re working to order with fussy customers and any slip-up can seriously affect your income. But you can also make your own prêt-à-porter dresses to exercise your creative vision.
I arrived fashionably late to Dressmaker but it is now a game I want to show to non-gamers, and one that has made me feel sartorially capable in ways I haven’t before. As you’re designing, the hours fly by and your blood pressure drops, even when you’re having to cut out your 18th juliet sleeve of the day. Also, the fact that I can now reliably identify a juliet sleeve gives me great pleasure. The next time I watch a costume drama and spot one, I’ll be like that Leonardo DiCaprio meme, pointing wildly at the TV, shouting, “Juliet sleeve! Juliet sleeve!” Dressmaker, then, exhibits style and substance and is easily worth its price.
