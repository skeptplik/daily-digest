British women convicted of illegally terminating their pregnancies under a Victorian-era law will be automatically pardoned on Monday, the government said.
The action follows Parliament's passage last year of a landmark vote to decriminalise abortion by repealing a section from the 1861 Offences Against the Person Act.
Women who have received a caution from police will also be pardoned and women arrested or investigated for ending their own pregnancy will have those details removed from their criminal records.
They will not have to apply for pardons and some will be issued posthumously.
“For too long, women have faced the devastating consequences of outdated abortion laws," said Heidi Stewart, chief executive of the British Pregnancy Advisory Service.
“While no pardon can undo the distress and trauma these women have experienced, this historic change in the law recognises the profound harm caused by their criminalisation and represents a meaningful step towards justice.”
The government did not provide the number of women affected. But Tonia Antoniazzi, the Labour member of Parliament who introduced the decriminalisation amendment, said police had investigated more than 100 women suspected of illegal abortions in the five years preceding last year's vote.
That included some who suffered natural miscarriages and stillbirths.
Antoniazzi described how women suspected of illegally aborting a foetus were once “dragged from hospital beds to police cells” and interrogated when they should have been cared for.
“This trauma cannot be erased, but I hope that this offers some alleviation of the pain these women have endured, enabling them to participate fully in society again, living their lives to the full, working the jobs they’ve always wanted to," she said.
Under current law, doctors can legally carry out abortions in England, Scotland and Wales up to 24 weeks, and beyond that under special circumstances, such as when the life of the mother is in danger. Abortion in Northern Ireland was decriminalised in 2019.
Under the Crime and Policing Act that took effect this spring, women who terminate a pregnancy at any stage will not face criminal charges.
The measure was opposed by anti-abortion groups that argued it would open the door to abortion on demand at any stage of pregnancy.
The law was changed after high-profile prosecutions, including the jailing of a mother of three that caused an outcry from abortion rights supporters.
Carla Foster was sentenced to more than two years in prison in 2023 for medically inducing an abortion about eight months into her pregnancy.
An appeals court freed her a month later, saying the case called for “compassion, not punishment.”
Another woman, Nicola Packer, was acquitted on a charge of unlawfully self-administering poison or a noxious substance with intent to procure a miscarriage.
Packer, who took abortion medicine when she was about 26 weeks pregnant, testified that she did not know she had been pregnant for more than 10 weeks.
