Eight opposition parties submitted a nonbinding censure motion to the Senate on Thursday against education minister Yoshihiro Seki over his involvement in a political funds scandal that has shaken Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi’s administration.
But while Seki will not be required to resign if the censure is approved Friday, concerns are growing among the ruling parties that its passage could delay Senate discussion and passage of key bills, including one to cut the food consumption tax from 8% to 1% for a two year period beginning April 1.
The eight parties that jointly submitted the motion include the Constitutional Democratic Party, the Democratic Party for the People, Komeito, Sanseito, and the Japanese Communist Party.
Their motion says that Seki gave false answers during parliamentary testimony about fundraising parties he held where civil servants gave speeches. He told parliament there had been only two such events. But it emerged he held three more than he reported, all in 2024.
While the upper chamber is controlled by the opposition, there are two other small opposition parties and seven independents who didn’t back Thursday’s motion, and it was unclear if there were enough votes to get it passed.
In the meantime, Seki faced pressure to resign before the vote, including from members of the ruling coalition partner, the Japan Innovation Party (JIP), though if the motion is defeated Friday, it might give him confidence that he will not be replaced.
“Seki should carefully consider the current state of affairs in parliament, and then make his own decision regarding his future. If a censure motion is issued, parliament will come to a complete standstill until the results of the censure motion are announced,” said former JIP leader and Lower House member Nobuyuki Baba on Wednesday.
The government aims to have the tax bill and others, including one to reduce the number of Lower House seats, enacted by parliament’s scheduled end on Dec. 12.
Speaking to reporters following the opposition parties’ submission, Seki, who has said he wanted to continue his post, deflected questions about whether he would resign.
“I’m aware that a censure motion has been submitted to the Senate, and I intend to continue to provide sincere, thorough explanations so that everyone can understand what happened,” he said.
