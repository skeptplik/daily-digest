Compromised passwords and gaps in multi-factor authentication (MFA) remain common ways attackers gain access to sensitive systems. For law enforcement agencies handling criminal justice information (CJI), protecting against these threats is also a matter of CJIS compliance.
The FBI's latest CJIS Security Policy sets clear requirements for password management and MFA. these are Priority 1 (P1) controls, so must be implemented immediately to comply. But this can be challenging, particularly across the complex IT environments that are common in law enforcement.
This guide contains practical tips to help agencies meet the latest requirements, strengthen their defenses against credential-based attacks, and make ongoing compliance easier to manage.
1. Enforce Stronger Minimum Password Lengths
CJIS requires user-selected passwords to be at least eight characters long, or six characters if generated by a credential service provider or verifier using an approved random number generator. However, we recommend going beyond these minimums and encouraging passphrases of at least 15 characters.
Longer passwords are generally harder to crack, and passphrases can be easier to remember than shorter passwords with complex combinations of symbols and numbers.
CJIS doesn't require traditional complexity rules, so focus on length and strength rather than arbitrary character requirements.
2. Block Weak and Compromised Passwords
Agencies must maintain a list of commonly used, predictable, and compromised passwords. This should include passwords exposed in previous breaches, dictionary words, sequential characters, and context-specific terms such as usernames or agency names.
CJIS requires agencies to update this list at least quarterly, and whenever organizational passwords are suspected of being compromised.

3. Reset Passwords Quickly and Give Users Clear Feedback
Agencies must force a password change whenever there's evidence that a credential has been compromised. Don't rely solely on scheduled password changes to address exposed credentials.
You must also check existing passwords against the breach list at least quarterly. More regular checks can help identify compromised credentials earlier, giving IT teams an opportunity to respond before attackers exploit them.
CJIS also requires users to select a new password immediately whenever compromise is suspected.
When a user does need to create or reset a password, rather than displaying a generic error message, provide clear, real-time feedback that helps users understand what makes a password weak and how to improve it.

4. Protect Passwords in Transit and At Rest
Password security doesn't end with enforcing strong credentials. CJIS also requires passwords to be transmitted over encrypted, authenticated channels to prevent interception and man-in-the-middle attacks.
When stored, passwords must be salted and hashed using a suitable one-way key derivation function, making them more resistant to offline cracking if an authentication database is compromised.
Agencies should review how their applications and authentication systems handle credentials to ensure these protections are in place.
5. Require MFA For All Privileged and Non-Privileged Accounts
CJIS requires MFA for both privileged and non-privileged organizational accounts. Don't focus solely on administrator accounts. Standard user accounts can also give attackers access to sensitive data or provide a foothold for further attacks.
Review your accounts and access policies to identify where MFA is missing, including accounts belonging to employees and contractors.
6. Extend MFA Across All Relevant Access Methods
CJIS requires MFA for local, network, and remote access. Agencies should review authentication across their environment, ensuring that all systems that touch CJI are adequately protected.

7. Make Sure Your MFA Methods Meet CJIS Requirements
CJIS specifies Authenticator Assurance Level 2 (AAL2), which requires two different authentication factors. This is a combination of something you know (like a password), something you have (like a smart card), or something you are (like a fingerprint).
Review your authentication methods to ensure they meet these requirements. Where possible, consider phishing-resistant MFA methods to provide stronger protection against credential phishing and adversary-in-the-middle attacks.
