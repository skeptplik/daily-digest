A grieving family in Lebanon returns to their village
In Southern Lebanon, the Army is taking over after Israeli forces withdraw. One grieving family returns to their village to find almost everything in their house destroyed.
ROB SCHMITZ, HOST:
In Southern Lebanon, the U.S. brokered a deal for the Lebanese Army to move into areas after the Israeli military withdrew. So far the deal, aimed at pushing out Hezbollah, has gone ahead in only one village. NPR's Jane Arraf and Jawad Rizkallah traveled there and met one family coping with a trail of death and devastation.
ATEF KADOUH: (Speaking Arabic).
JANE ARRAF, BYLINE: After years of working as an electrician in Saudi Arabia, Atef Kadouh built his house in the village of Zawtar al-Gharbiyah. He and his wife, Somiya Yaghi, raised five children, all born in the house. Now there are four.
UNIDENTIFIED PERSON: (Speaking Arabic).
ARRAF: A photo of the youngest, Adel, is propped up in a basket of dried flowers near the doorway.
KADOUH: My son who drive a ambulance (ph), 27 years, 27.
ARRAF: Adel, clean-cut with a trimmed beard, was a sports teacher and a basketball champion. He was about to travel to France for his doctorate when the war with Israel began. Instead, he volunteered as an ambulance driver. The Israeli military killed him and four other medics on March 28 in a drone strike as they helped bury a 75-year-old man who had died in hospital.
KADOUH: (Crying, speaking Arabic).
ARRAF: "This one broke me," his father says. "He was my youngest son, 27 years old. You attack a paramedic rescuing wounded people, children? He was not a fighter at the front."
Lebanon's health ministry says Israel has killed more than 135 first responders and medical staff in the past two years. Asked about this air strike, the Israeli military replied, without providing proof, that militants routinely pose as paramedics and transport weapons in ambulances. It did not respond to follow-up questions pointing out the five were not killed in an ambulance.
The Kadouh family returned to the village in July when Zawtar al-Gharbiyah became the first, until now, only test of a U.S.-brokered plan for Israeli forces to withdraw and the Lebanese army to take over. The couple found that Israeli soldiers had occupied their house.
And right next to this completely blackened door with a huge chunk taken out of it, there are names written in blue pen, blue marker on the walls that seem to be the names of Israeli soldiers.
Almost everything in the house had been destroyed, every window shattered, furniture broken. The fridge, freezer and washing machine had bullet holes in them.
KADOUH: (Non-English language spoken).
ARRAF: Kadouh points out shattered floor tiles, kitchen cupboards wrenched from the walls. "They left nothing," he says. The Israeli soldiers set fire to the house before they left, the family says.
SOMIYA YAGHI: (Non-English language spoken).
ARRAF: His wife shows me their 18-month-old grandson's room, where trails of soot from a scorched ceiling drip down on walls decorated with images of teddy bears and hot air balloons.
All of the things were moved out of this room and thrown either on the balcony or thrown over the balcony. It's just a mess of baby shoes and clothing and books.
In some rooms, there are piles of half-burned trash - rotting sandwiches along with fruit boxes and cans of energy drinks, hundreds of mustard and ketchup packages, all labeled in Hebrew. There's an engine casing marked in English, propulsion unit, tactical - the kind used in missiles or drones - and bullets throughout the house.
She's just handed me a bullet, and she says there are a lot of them. It looks like a rifle bullet.
They have no running water or electricity. Kadouh says the Israeli military destroyed their solar panels. Among all the damage, the destruction of their trees cuts the deepest.
KADOUH: (Non-English language spoken).
ARRAF: "I tended to them as if they were small children. They're all dear to me," Kadouh says. "The olive tree, that was 60 years old. I planted it when I first built the house."
She's saying the bulldozer came through here and uprooted the trees, the huge pine trees, where they used to get pine nuts from, which are really valuable, a delicacy, as well as the olive trees. And then they carried on through and destroyed the garage.
"Now we're back to square one," Kadouh says, "starting from scratch." But it's worse than square one. Despite a ceasefire, there are Israeli airstrikes in the adjoining village. We see smoke rising just a few hundred yards away. "Please record this," Yaghi says.
YAGHI: (Non-English language spoken).
ARRAF: "Our land is a paradise with everything in it," she tells me. "Our life is this land. Our life is our homes. Even if we were to die in our homes, it would be much better than living outside."
The Lebanese Army told them that when it reentered the village, it dismantled explosives left by Israeli forces in their house and others. The Israeli army did not reply to NPR queries about why the house was damaged and set on fire. It said military policy bars burning homes or leaving explosives.
KADOUH: (Non-English language spoken).
ARRAF: Kadouh says, "after all the killing and destruction, maybe someday there will be peace with Israel." But not in his lifetime and not with Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu. "Netanyahu is the one who killed my son," he says. "How could I put my hand in his? If they want to be our neighbors, do good. Instead, they burn us."
Jane Arraf, NPR News, Zawtar al-Gharbiyah, Lebanon.
