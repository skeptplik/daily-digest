Rockstar Games boss Rob Nelson has explained why the studio added the divisive criminal profile system to GTA 6.
In an interview with Eurogamer, Nelson was asked about Rockstar's approach to the systems and themes in GTA 6. He said that a lot of it was born out of all of the work they put into Red Dead Redemption 2, specifically having a reactive world that responds to the player's behavior and choices. He explained that there was a desire to evolve the GTA experience and to "give players choices to interact with the world beyond just violence, beyond just 'down the barrel of a gun.'"
That means players can now point a gun at someone and threaten them, rather than having to beat or kill them to steal their money. The threat of violence is just as prevalent as the violence itself in GTA 6, and it also plays into the morality-adjacent system that is your criminal profile. The game doesn't explicitly punish you for doing bad things, because it's GTA, and you're bound to do bad things. However, it judges you on how harsh you are. If you rob a store with minimal force or only attack people if they draw on you first, attempting to minimize casualties, your profile won't change. If you go in and kill everyone, the level may decrease.
This prompted Rockstar to expand NPC interactions even further. If you drew a gun in GTA 5, no one cared until it was actively pointed at them. Now, people will begin to quietly scurry away if they see you pull a rifle out of your trunk. Nelson explained that while the powerful rampages were fun for a while in previous games, they lacked substance and would get old quickly. In GTA 6, the process to start mowing people down takes a bit more thought because you don't have an endless pocket of weapons anymore.
"You have to call your car," says Nelson. "You have to pop a car trunk and get [a weapon], and now I'm walking, and now people are noticing, but they're sort of sidling away, and it's more believable. And then they're still fleeing in terror, but then there's people who also have guns, and they're shooting at you, or you try to hold somebody up, and they pull a gun, or they grab it, and then you have to deal with them.
"And then, you choose. You're trying to jack somebody off a motorcycle, and they pull a gun on you, and you have to blow their head off, and it's like, 'You shouldn't have made me do that!' And so then it has more meaning, and it's more funny in a way when it's a little bit more pointed. And you can still go on those rampages and basically smash your criminal profile until it breaks, and then you enter this irredeemable state, which basically says, 'I reject your criminal profile system! I don't want it.'"
The more realistic approach with GTA 6 has been a bit divisive for purists. Some don't want consequences; they want a power fantasy, and GTA 6 is decidely not that. If you want a supercar, it's going to take you dozens of hours before you can steal one. There's a progression to the chaos. For some, that's more rewarding. For others who want that quick fix, it's cumbersome. It'll be interesting to see how Rockstar looks at all of the responses to the game and how that informs the next GTA.
Will they go further down this path or will they try to find a greater middle ground? The game certainly isn't hurting despite some vocal critics. GTA 6 is the highest rated game in history on Metacritic and generated nearly $4 billion in revenue in its first 24 hours, making it the biggest entertainment launch in history. IGN gave the game a 10 out of 10, praising it for its depth and detail.
"Exquisitely detailed, endlessly ripe for experimentation, and uncompromisingly engineered, GTA 6 has not stopped astonishing me since I started it," reads an excerpt from our review. "I regularly find myself in love with video games, but it’s extraordinarily rare that I find myself in complete awe of them. Put simply, Grand Theft Auto 6 is the single most impressive video game I have ever played."
