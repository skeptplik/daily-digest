When Heather Champion raised a council motion in March opposing a massive new gas plant to power a large datacentre in the southern highlands of New South Wales, it failed to pass.
Five months later, when the local councillor for Wingecaribee raised a new motion calling for the federal government to implement a moratorium on new datacentres, it passed unanimously.
“I think the council’s change in perspective is a direct result of the change in sentiment in the community,” Champion says.
The shift highlights grassroots successes in pushing back on datacentre construction and how those wins are inspiring other communities to take similar action.
In May, Guardian Australia reported on three datacentre projects across the country – Hazelmere in Western Australia, West Footscray in Victoria and Lane Cove in New South Wales.
Since the report, the project proposals have been withdrawn in WA and NSW, and NextDC’s M3 expansion in West Footscray has been stalled after Jemena, the power provider, delayed the installation of taller power poles amid community outcry.
A spokesperson for Lane Cove Responsible Planning, Sasha Titchkosky, says after the success of having the Goodman Group’s Mars datacentre in Lane Cove withdrawn, she is working with communities in western Sydney and the southern highlands on their own campaigns.
Titchkosky says by working together, the groups send a strong signal to industry and politicians that they need to do better.
As it stands, councils play a role in approving datacentres in some states but in others, including NSW and Victoria, fast-tracked approvals skip the local council stage. The federal government has proposed rules that would from next year set mandatory minimum requirements for large datacentres, including how projects manage energy, water, costs, community engagement, location and workforce impacts.
“Just saying they’re going to put in place regulations but still allowing datacentres to be approved … isn’t good enough.
“They [need to] either put a pause on approvals or basically say that everyone who gets approved now will be subject to the regulations when they come in force.”
Campaigners across Australia are sharing notes and tips through WhatsApp, using the platform to organise rallies and discuss new proposals.
In Victoria, a community wiki site displays a map of locations of datacentre proposals, the community groups organising around them, and links to campaign tips from US groups that successfully lobbied against the sector in the midterm elections.
Titchkosky has been in contact with campaigners in the southern highlands, where an application refusal for one of three gas plants capable of producing 673MW of power in total is being challenged in the NSW land and environment court. The applicant, Cloud Carrier, wants to use the gas plant to power a “data campus” in Moss Vale with AI “at its core”.
“We’ve been in touch with Lane Cove for quite a long time … We are sharing skills, messages – it’s very lateral and it’s very informal,” Champion says of the community networks’ organic growth.
They share what works and what doesn’t work – and what might work in one area may not be suitable in another, she says.
“[Lane Cove’s] circumstances are just so completely different but in some ways that’s been really helpful … having [our] voices combined does provide some breadth.”
The groups are also working with communities in western Sydney to build their capacity to take on new centres planned for the area.
Mandy Ottaviano, a master’s of sustainability student at the University of Sydney who has been researching community action against datacentres in Australia says there is a “real desire” for the groups to connect with each other but there is no formal organisation.
“What we’re not seeing a lot of yet is [non-government organisations] stepping in and bridging that gap in terms of coordinating those groups in a meaningful way together,” she says.
“We haven’t yet seen any emergence of a national kind of coalition.”
The chief executive of Data Centres Australia, Belinda Dennett, acknowledges there is “more interest from the community” in datacentre development than ever before, and datacentre companies are stepping up community consultation.
“This ranges from community information sessions, door knocking, newsletters, websites, hotline phone numbers and social media updates,” Dennett says.
“Third parties delivering energy, broadband and water infrastructure are also an important part of building community support so we also encourage more coordination and information sharing between parties.”
Mixed messages
While state and federal governments appear to be reacting to the growing community concerns about datacentres – some states are bringing in new rules and the federal government is set to follow suit early next year – campaigners are concerned the changes don’t go far enough.
The NSW parliament’s long-running inquiry into datacentres released its report this month, with the committee voting to remove two contentious recommendations: that the NSW government advocates for a moratorium on new datacentre developments until new obligations are in place; and to advocate for the new federal rules to be applied retrospectively.
The chair, the Greens MP Abigail Boyd, was the only committee member to vote in support of the recommendations.
“It appears that both the Labor and Liberal parties remain in hock to the industry talking points,” Boyd said in the chair’s foreword in the report.
“I was particularly disappointed that both Labor and Liberal parties voted together against a pause on data centre approvals until sufficiently robust and legally binding obligations are in place, and similarly voted against advocating for data centres currently in the planning pipeline to have to comply with any rules that are currently being contemplated.”
Boyd said the delay to the rules coming in federally “makes clear the coordinated effort by governments and industry to avoid having to comply with even the most moderate of environmental and social obligations”.
Champion says she was “really frustrated” the recommendations were removed.
“We agreed between the group of us we would call for a moratorium, and all the community groups know that’s what’s needed.
“It feels like a real cop-out, to be honest.”
Titchkosky says it is “clear as day where the two major parties” sit on datacentres – and questions whether the move may prove politically destructive, given that datacentre disquiet played a pivotal role in voter sentiment in the US midterms.
“They’re ultimately prepared to sell out communities for multinational corporate interests,” she says. “I think we are going to see the same issues play out in Victoria and NSW in our upcoming elections as they were in the US midterms.
“Communities are really angry about this.”
