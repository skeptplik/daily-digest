Caitlin Thomas recalls knowing that something terrible had happened but not fully understanding what.
She and her partner had arrived at a music festival some 270km north of Melbourne in the early afternoon and began setting up their tents and a gazebo for shade when the sky suddenly turned dark and the rain became incredibly heavy. A huge gust of wind picked up and Thomas asked her partner to hold on to the gazebo so it wouldn’t fly away. “He then asked me to hold on to it also so he could crack open his beer.”
“We heard this huge crack of thunder which felt like it shook the ground underneath it,” she says. “It almost felt like we were in a dome of thunder. Within three seconds everything was in a whiteout, and I felt a zap race through my body.”
She saw her partner, Harry Gordon, lying on the ground in the foetal position, holding his left arm, not moving.
“I remember the fear of not knowing if he was okay. I recall the second my body and brain linked as I looked down at my left leg and left arm and could not feel anything … As soon as I saw Harry move, I began sprinting for my life, searching for help. I remember looking down at my body running but not being able to feel anything.”
Thomas and Gordon had been struck by lightning in a freak storm at the three-day Strawberry Fields music festival in Tocumwal, on the Victoria-New South Wales border, on 20 November.
Grant Kirkby, a lightning risk mitigation expert from Western Australia, believes they were affected by an “upward streamer leader”.
“As lightning begins to travel downwards from a thundercloud, it creates an extremely strong electrical field at ground level,” he says. “In response to this electrical field, small channels of electricity can start travelling upwards from the ground and [through] objects such as trees, buildings, metal structures and even people.
“In effect, these upward electrical currents are competing to connect with the approaching lightning. When one successfully connects, it completes the electrical pathway between the cloud and the ground, resulting in the bright lightning strike that we see.”
The gazebo’s metal frame – an excellent conductor – would have allowed the electrical current to travel through it easily, he explains. So, what Thomas and Gordon experienced would have been an electrical discharge rather than a direct strike, similar to what happened to a Chinese weather presenter on live TV who was holding an umbrella.
“A person does not necessarily have to be standing at the point where lightning strikes the ground to be injured,” Kirkby says.
Lightning (which contains a current five times hotter than the surface of the sun) can injure or kill someone indirectly by the electricity that spreads through the ground, jumps from a nearby object or travels along metal, as well as upward streamers. About 95% of people are struck indirectly, and both direct and indirect strikes can be fatal.
Thomas says her experience was “extremely painful, almost like every muscle had contracted at the same time”. It left her feeling weak and dizzy, with an instant headache.
But she and Gordon were extremely lucky. After being taken to hospital by ambulance and monitored for several hours, the 23-year-olds were given the all-clear.
Globally, deaths from lightning have been estimated at between 6,000 and 24,000 and injuries at 240,000 a year. In Australia, one to two people have been killed by lightning each year since 2023, according to Kirkby, and more than 100 have been injured. Many experience lasting effects – and not via burn injuries, as commonly thought.
“The burns are usually very minor,” says Prof Mary Ann Cooper, retired from the University of Illinois, Chicago, who has conducted medical studies of lightning victims and released a free book on reducing lightning injury.
The damage is neurological, she says. In the short term it can cause kerauno paralysis (kerauno being Greek for lightning) in the legs or arms. It may cause unconsciousness or confusion akin to concussion. Longer term injuries include learning problems, amnesia, headaches, personality changes, irritability, chronic pain, sleeping problems and dizziness. Many survivors suffer from post-traumatic stress disorder.
“One of my survivors and good friends used to call it black holes that he had in his brain, and he never knew where the little black holes were going to be,” Cooper tells the Guardian.
‘When thunder roars, go indoors’
How can we protect ourselves? First, by understanding the risk.
You don’t have to be in the middle of a storm to be struck, as discovered this month by a man in his 20s who was hit by lightning under a clear blue sky while swimming on the Central Coast of NSW. He was last reported to be in a critical but stable condition.
Use the “flash-to-bang” rule to calculate how far away a pending storm is. “Light travels almost instantly,” Kirkby explains, “but sounds take about three seconds to travel one kilometre.” If you count 30 seconds or less between seeing a flash and hearing the thunder, the storm is up to 10km away and you should head for safety.
The single most important way to protect yourself is to go into a substantial building, say Cooper and Kirkby. “When thunder roars, go indoors.” The next best option is a hard-topped metal vehicle. This isn’t due to the rubber tires but because the current flows over the metal, creating a faraday cage.
After the lightning stops, you should stay sheltered for 30 minutes.
The Australian Bureau of Meteorology warns that there is no safe place outdoors during a storm. But if seeking shelter is not possible, keep your exposure low, Kirkby says. “Keep your feet together and try to find some sort of depressed area so you are not the highest object.”
But don’t seek refuge next to something that is taller than you. If you stand under a tree, for instance, you can be struck by a side flash: if the electrical current goes through the tree it could jump to you. “This happens quite regularly,” Kirkby warns.
Be mindful that lightning deposits up to 1bn volts of electricity at its impact point, which dissipate the further you get from the point of impact.
Kirkby stresses the importance of keeping your feet together – and, whatever you do, don’t lie down. It’s all about equalising the voltage. “Think about a bird sitting on a high-voltage power line with 110,000 to 130,000 volts, and he’s fine, flapping his wings and singing and having a great time,” he says.
But if his wing touches some other object, like a nearby wire, it causes a voltage differential, which causes current to flow. “And it’s the same with people.”
If your feet are apart, it causes a voltage differential that allows ground current to flow through the body, meaning cows can be struck down in whole herds.
Thomas hasn’t noticed any after-effects since the music festival. “Although it would be cool if I had superpowers,” she says.
