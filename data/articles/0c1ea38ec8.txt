Saičić was selected for his academic achievements, personal integrity and reserved public profile, which set him apart from Serbia’s established political figures.
The student movement in Serbia has decided to put forward Professor Radomir Saičić, a distinguished chemistry professor and academician, as its candidate for the upcoming presidential election, scheduled for December 27. His candidacy is hardly a surprise. Saičić had been mentioned from the very beginning as one of the leading figures who could represent the students in the presidential race.
He is, however, a rather different kind of political figure from those who have dominated Serbia’s political scene for years. A deeply religious, exceptionally quiet and reserved man, Saičić is widely respected in academic and professional circles. He is not a conventional politician, nor someone known for seeking public attention or political confrontation. His reputation rests primarily on his academic achievements, personal integrity and the respect he commands among his colleagues. That is precisely what makes him an appealing choice for a movement that has repeatedly presented itself as an alternative to the country’s established political elite.
“I entered the movement believing that a change of government makes sense only if it also brings a change in the system of values, including the way those in power treat citizens. That is why I will advocate a sovereign Serbia in which the Constitution is respected and every citizen can live with dignity” Saičić said and added:
“As President, I will respect the limits of my constitutional powers, publicly explain my decisions, and demand the same accountability from other holders of public office. It is time to restore dignity to the office of the President of Serbia. Faith gives me the moral strength and the obligation to keep my word. Science teaches me to ask questions and seek evidence, while life has taught me to take responsibility when things are most difficult. I am ready to take that responsibility now.”
The political significance of his nomination goes beyond the choice of an individual candidate. The students appear to be deliberately seeking someone who can embody a break with the political culture of the past 14 years: a figure associated with expertise, personal modesty and moral authority rather than political ambition or a cult of personality.
The presidential election will take place on December 27, while December 6 is the final deadline for submitting presidential candidacies, according to the official electoral timetable.
Vučić considers boycotting the elections
One of the biggest uncertainties surrounding the election is whether the Serbian Progressive Party (SNS), which dominated Serbian politics under Aleksandar Vučić for more than a decade, will even participate. The party has yet to make a final decision, while Vučić himself has openly expressed his preference for staying out of the presidential race. Such a boycott could reflect the party’s reluctance to risk another potentially humiliating defeat following its loss in the October parliamentary elections. It would also raise questions about the legitimacy of an election in which the country’s former ruling party might refuse to take part.
Vučić said that opinions within the SNS were divided, but made clear that he personally favoured ignoring the presidential election.
“There are different views: some want to support an independent candidate, others want the SNS to put forward its own candidate, while some, like me, want us simply to ignore the elections and let them boast that they have taken everything and won 94 per cent of the vote.”
He also described the forthcoming presidential election as: “The most meaningless elections in history.”
Daily arrests and review of contracts signed by the SNS Government
Meanwhile, Serbia remains in a highly tense and politically uncertain atmosphere. Although the previous government is formally still in office, pending the formation of a new administration, arrests and investigations involving senior figures from the former Vučić administration are continuing. The developments have further polarised an already divided society, with the former president accusing the new parliamentary majority of political persecution, while the student movement insists that accountability and the rule of law must finally apply to everyone, regardless of political connections.
For the student movement, the presidential campaign is expected to focus on the rule of law, the fight against corruption and the restoration of public confidence in state institutions. One of its central promises will be a comprehensive review of contracts and agreements concluded by Vučić and his governments, particularly those involving major public investments and the use of public resources. The students are expected to argue that the country needs transparency, institutional accountability and independent oversight, rather than decisions made behind closed doors.
Perhaps the most important political message of Saičić’s candidacy is the attempt to redefine the very nature of political power in Serbia. For the past 14 years, the country’s political system has increasingly revolved around one man, with institutions often reduced to instruments of presidential authority. The students are promising a different model: a functioning state in which institutions serve citizens, public officials are accountable and political authority is exercised within constitutional limits.
Saičić’s challenge, therefore, will be to turn the moral credibility of the student movement into a credible political alternative. His quiet personality and academic background may be precisely what the movement believes Serbia needs at this moment. But the presidential election will also test whether the students’ unprecedented parliamentary victory can be translated into a broader mandate for institutional and political change.
