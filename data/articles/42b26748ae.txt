Lucy Letby’s lawyer has said the court of appeal must urgently review her convictions, after it was revealed that a key medical witness in the prosecution’s case was barred from treating patients because of safety concerns.
The Guardian revealed this week that Prof Peter Hindmarsh – a crucial expert witness who provided an opinion at Letby’s trial that two babies had been poisoned with insulin – was excluded from clinical practice in March 2023 because his hospital considered him unsafe to treat children in relation to insulin.
The jury in the case were never told of the restriction placed on Hindmarsh, who was described by the prosecution in court as “the pre-eminent expert” on insulin.
Letby’s lawyer Mark McDonald, who has applied to the miscarriages of justice watchdog, the Criminal Cases Review Commission, for Letby’s convictions to be referred back to the court of appeal, said: “These revelations about Prof Hindmarsh, and the failure to disclose material information about him to the defence at trial, raise fundamental questions, and it is more urgent than ever that the court of appeal re-examines the safety of all Lucy Letby’s convictions.
“She is an innocent woman in prison, serving whole-life sentences, yet material information that could have been of critical importance to her defence was not disclosed. Did the police know? How did this information remain undisclosed?
“We need answers. Lucy deserves answers. She should not have to spend another day behind bars while these fundamental questions remain unanswered.”
Two senior lawyers said serious concerns about an expert’s professional abilities, and non-disclosure to the defence, could be grounds for an appeal against convictions.
Letby’s defence lawyers were never told that Hindmarsh had been excluded from clinical practice, nor of an April 2023 tribunal ruling imposing a restriction. The Crown Prosecution Service (CPS) has also said it was not informed.
Letby was convicted of murdering seven babies and attempting to murder seven others, and sentenced to 15 whole-life terms in prison. Hindmarsh’s opinion that two babies were poisoned with insulin, for which Letby was found guilty of attempted murder, was considered crucial to Letby being convicted. Court of appeal judges turned down her applications to appeal.
Since the convictions, dozens of leading UK and international medical and scientific experts have contributed to reports submitted with her CCRC application, arguing that the prosecution’s medical case, including Hindmarsh’s evidence, was wrong.
This week a Guardian investigation revealed that while the trial was going on, Hindmarsh’s employer barred him from clinical practice, and reported to the General Medical Council (GMC), which was already investigating him, that it believed he posed a risk to the safety of child patients.
Hindmarsh was a consultant paediatric endocrinologist – a specialist in hormones, including insulin – at University College London hospitals trust (UCLH) when the trial began. He had also been an honorary consultant at Great Ormond Street hospital.
In March 2023, while the trial was continuing at Manchester crown court, Dr Tim Hodgson, a medical director at UCLH, wrote to the GMC: “On the grounds of safety UCLH are not presently in a position to allow Prof [Hindmarsh] to practise clinically even under supervision … I do not believe UCLH can accept the level of risk Prof Hindmarsh appears to pose to children and young people even with close clinical supervision.”
Hodgson set out safety concerns, including two cases in which he said Hindmarsh had advised child patients to stop taking insulin. The GMC then applied for Hindmarsh’s registration as a doctor to be suspended, which Hindmarsh opposed. His barrister argued it would be disproportionate, and would impede his ability to work as an expert witness in legal cases.
On 21 April 2023, a medical practitioners tribunal declined to suspend Hindmarsh but ordered that he must be directly supervised in his clinical work. That appears to have had no practical effect because UCLH had barred him from treating patients at all.
Hindmarsh did tell Cheshire constabulary, which instructed him in the case, about the GMC investigation in December 2022, and the CPS informed Letby’s defence team, but said it would oppose the jury being told. The jury was never informed that the GMC had launched an investigation into concerns about Hindmarsh, referred by UCLH and Great Ormond Street. His contract at Great Ormond Street had been terminated in July 2022, and the jury was not told about that either.
Nick Johnson KC, the lead prosecution barrister, described Hindmarsh to the jury on 23 February 2023 as “the pre-eminent expert in that narrow field” of insulin.
Letby’s defence team was not informed about the further developments of March and April 2023, including that Hindmarsh had been excluded from clinical practice; and the jury was never told.
McDonald said: “It is staggering that Hindmarsh was presented as the pre-eminent expert on insulin at the trial, gave crucial evidence alleging babies were poisoned with insulin – which many eminent experts say was wrong – and it was never disclosed that he was considered by his own hospital [to be] unsafe to treat children with insulin, and barred from clinical practice.”
A representative of Hindmarsh declined to answer questions from the Guardian, including whether he had failed to disclose the March and April developments to Cheshire constabulary. The police force declined to comment. The CPS confirmed it was never given the information.
The criminal procedure rules require expert witnesses to disclose to the party instructing them anything, including disciplinary proceedings, “that might reasonably be thought capable of undermining the expert’s opinion or detract from the credibility or impartiality of the expert”. The rules were introduced in 2014 after recommendations by the Law Commission to improve expert evidence and guard against miscarriages of justice.
The commission was set up after a series of notorious miscarriages of justice based partly on flawed evidence from medical expert witnesses. The cases included Sally Clark, a solicitor who was wrongly convicted in 1999 of killing her two infant sons.
Clark’s convictions were overturned in 2003 after a CCRC referral to the court of appeal, principally because key medical evidence had not been disclosed to the defence. A striking statistical opinion presented by an expert witness for the prosecution, about the likelihood of both sons dying, was also shown to be catastrophically wrong. Clark had spent more than three years in prison condemned as a child killer and never recovered from her ordeal; she died in 2007 of acute alcohol poisoning.
Tim Green KC, a specialist in disclosure, said of the rules: “If there was a failure to make relevant disclosure of material undermining an expert’s opinion evidence, and where that opinion evidence was relevant to the central issues before the jury, then that failure could form part of the grounds of appeal. It all depends upon the facts of a particular case and potential weight of the non-disclosure upon the jury’s verdict.”
Pete Weatherby KC, a barrister with extensive experience of criminal trials, said: “The expert witness disclosure rules are there to protect the integrity of the trial process and avoid miscarriages of justice. Expert evidence was pivotal in the Lucy Letby prosecution because there was no direct or forensic evidence that she committed any of the criminal acts of which she was accused.
“Serious concerns about an expert’s conduct or judgment may be highly relevant to assessing their expertise, credibility, and the reliability of their evidence. If those concerns were not before the jury, that could be very significant for an appeal. If the full details were not obtained by the prosecution, and disclosed to the defence at the time, that would be even more significant.”
