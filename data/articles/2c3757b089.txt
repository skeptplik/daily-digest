California has served an investigative subpoena on Nvidia after a scandal that prompted the departure of co-founder and longtime chief executive Jensen Huang.
Rob Bonta, the US state’s attorney general, issued the subpoena as part of an investigation into whether exports of the California-based company’s advanced semiconductor chips ended up in China, contravening US restrictions.
“The conduct of Nvidia and its former chief executive raises bright red flags, including about the ways in which the company may have misled investors,” said Bonta.
“Nvidia is in a messy, transnational situation but the underlying principle of our corporate securities law is clear: companies cannot mislead their investors.”
The subpoena, issued on Thursday, followed Huang’s resignation a week ago after it emerged he had multiple affairs, including one with Alice Huang, a suspected Chinese spy.
Alice Huang, who is not related, is associated with Singapore-based Megaspeed, which has bought nearly $5bn (£3.8bn) of Nvidia chips and hardware in recent years, and is being investigated for possibly diverting advanced restricted chips to China.
Megaspeed has said it is a “legitimate and lawful” business, and that Huang left the company late last year.
Bonta said the investigation would also look at whether the former Nvidia chief’s actions resulted in confidential information being passed to China.
“My office is investigating whether Nvidia or Jensen Huang’s actions resulted in China obtaining advanced chips or confidential information,” he said. “And if Nvidia misled investors and the public about this risk, in violation of the law.”
Nvidia’s shares are down by a fifth since Huang admitted the extramarital affair, hitting a one-year low at the close of trading on Thursday, and wiping almost $1tn off its market value.
Earlier this week, Harvey Jones, Nvidia’s chair, said the company had no intention of reinstating Huang after the former CEO said he would be “honoured” to return. Elon Musk and a host of tech bosses had campaigned for Nvidia to bring him back.
Huang is due to testify before several congressional committees over the potential breach of national security on 1 December.
Shares in rival chipmaker AMD closed down almost 8% on Thursday after the publication of reports connected to short seller Hunterbrook warning about its links to China and the “circular financing” of customers.
The company, which has been targeted amid the Nvidia scandal, is looking at a further fall of more than 5% in pre-market trading.
Hunterbrook Media published allegations on the use of AMD’s software by a Chinese server maker blacklisted by the US. Sister publication, The Bear Cave newsletter, alleged that AMD’s use of datacentre lease guarantees and backstops for unsold cloud computing capacity could leave it exposed to a pullback in AI spending.
AMD said the reports’ claims “misrepresent AMD’s business practices and relationships”.
“AMD complies with applicable US export control laws and regulations and maintains rigorous processes to ensure compliance across our global operations,” the company said. “We remain focused on serving our customers and upholding the highest standards of integrity.”
AMD’s share price has more than doubled so far this year, giving the company a market capitalisation of more than $800m.
