Grand Theft Auto VI is now the biggest video game launch of all time, and is likely to become the biggest video game of all time. It’s a once-in-a-lifetime event, a phenomenon that will shape games as a medium and pop culture at large for years to come, set against the backdrop of its developers fighting for their rights at studio Rockstar Games. It has snatched sales and streaming records and become the best-reviewed game ever (pulling ahead of 1998’s The Legend of Zelda: Ocarina of Time). It’s also the first GTA game with a female protagonist, who also happens to be a woman of colour. Yet very few women (and seemingly no women of colour) played and reviewed it before release.
When GTA IV’s review embargo lifted on 18 November, the gender imbalance was immediately obvious. Out of 156 reviewers, only six appeared to be women. As journalist Lex Luddy noted in a BlueSky post, that is 3.8%.
The lack of diversity of voice is depressing. The unfortunate reality is that there aren’t that many women writing about video games in 2026, thanks to a mix of media consolidation, firings, hostile takeovers and burnout. Public ire – something, sadly, that all public-facing critics and journalists have to deal with – is also especially intense for female, queer and non-white writers. Though an Entertainment Software Association report this year suggests that women make up 46% of the gamer population in the US, they do not make up half of the people writing about video games. A glance at the editorial teams for some of the top video game sites in the US and UK – GamesRadar, PC Gamer, Polygon, Kotaku, Eurogamer and Game Informer – reveals 100 writers, of whom 27 are women. That’s just over a quarter. The only GTA VI reviews on those sites were from men.
As it was such a big launch, many outlets will have assigned GTA VI reviews to the most “senior” games writers. But that is precisely the problem: there aren’t more women at this senior level, more female household names, because the games industry chews them up and spits them out. Given that this is the first GTA game in 30 years with a female protagonist – apart from the mute characters Candy and Gretchen in the Game Boy Color versions of GTA 2 – the lack of female perspectives on the game is even more glaring.
GTA VI stars Lucia Caminos, the Bonnie to Jason Duval’s Clyde. She is a Latina woman played by a Latina woman, struggling to thrive in a dark-mirror version of modern America, where the crooks are in charge and the only way to get by is to break the rules. We’ve known about Lucia since the first trailer debuted in 2023, and with three different delays, you would think the enterprising leaders at the biggest video game websites or verticals would have allocated a review or two to one of the dozens of talented women working this beat. Wouldn’t female game journalists, who have lived through years of male-centric GTA discourse, have a take on the series’ shifting perspective? Wouldn’t it be interesting to hear from American women about what it’s like to try to make ends meet or simply exist in a country that has overturned abortion access and locked up and brutalised brown people en masse, and how those real-world experiences intersect with the experience of the game?
But alas, here we are. Six women have reviewed GTA VI in 2026.
Maybe there’s hope. Manni L Perez, who plays Lucia, was nominated for best performance at the forthcoming Game Awards 2026, while Jason was not. Reviews, livestreams and reactions thus far suggest this is a GTA game that, while still steeped in satire, biting critiques of modern American society, and absurdist violence, is a love story. And Lucia is at its centre. Perhaps her character and her journey, coupled with a gameplay loop that models a functioning and considerate relationship, could mean the GTA player base shifts to reflect the gamer population at large. More women may buy and play this game than any other Rockstar game ever. Maybe 13 years from now, GTA VII will have more women reviewing it than men.
