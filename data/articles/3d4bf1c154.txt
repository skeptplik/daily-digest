"I think the biggest talking point over the next half year is going to be the time to market, and how devs are now afraid of how AI copies their games," says Alex Nichiporchik, the head of Tinybuild.
He's not wrong. Recent improvements in generative AI technology mean it's now easier than ever for unscrupulous developers to make rapid copies of in-development titles by, for example, reverse engineering code from demos. One headline victim is Needle in a Haystack Simulator – a video of the unreleased game got more than 50 million views on X this summer, leading to the rapid emergence of multiple copycats with similar gameplay on Steam. There are even accusations flying around that one of the copycats allegedly copied code from another Needle in a Haystack imitator.
During a publisher and investor panel at the recent AI and Games Conference in London, Raw Fury's James Barnes described the rise of AI clones as a "wild situation", while Stefan Metaxa, commercial director at Spiderling Studios, said it has led to an abrupt change in thinking from studios and publishers. "The traditional wisdom previously was to announce early, build up as many wishlists as possible over time, and then you'll have a good basis to launch your game eventually," he said. "And that seems to be changing fundamentally."
The new wisdom is to keep the gap between announcement and launch to a minimum – which seems to have worked for Tinybuild. At DevGAMM Lisbon, Nichiporchik tells GamesIndustry.biz that the company has been experimenting with shorter gaps. ReStory: Chill Electronic Repairs was announced in late 2025 and launched in August this year, while for Probably Stolen, the interval was even shorter – it was announced on August 3 and released on October 28.
Importantly, the move doesn't seem to have had a negative effect on sales. If anything, it's quite the opposite: Nichiporchik says Probably Stolen, a cyberpunk shop simulator, has already sold more than a million units. Tinybuild has a tradition of doing a shot for every 1,000 concurrent users (CCU) on launch day, he says, but that tradition had to be abandoned when the CCU for Probably Stolen rapidly climbed above 80,000 at launch, and just kept on climbing in the days that followed. "I stopped at like four," he says.
Go for launch
The Tinybuild head says that one advantage of minimising the gap between announcement and launch is to keep titles fresh in players' minds, instead of them being left to fossilise and be forgotten about on wishlists. "People have short memories today, right?"
He adds that the minimal interval worked well for Probably Stolen because the game landed extremely well with playtesters, so the publisher was confident it would immediately resonate with audiences rather than facing a long battle to build attention. Although originally, the game was set to be revealed months earlier.
"We were ready to announce Probably Stolen I believe in May, and then we got the first test results internally, and we're like, 'Okay, these scores are a little bit low, let's push it'. We identified what didn't work, and we pushed the announcement. And then on shot number two, well, technically number three or four, we were able to get where we wanted."
It's likely that the short gap between reveal and launch helped to nix any would-be copycats. Nichiporchik says the problem of unauthorised clones has been made much worse in recent months by the advent of the tool REA (Reverse Engineer Anything), which "allows you to reverse engineer any executable down to its source code," he says. "And especially for Unity games, you can pretty much one shot the source code."
"Just last week, someone made a port of Graveyard Keeper for Android," he continues. "They reverse engineered the code, compiled it for Android – it barely fits into memory – and put a plugin for touchscreen controls. We already took it down, but we were like, 'Huh, it's that easy?'"
"As long as you have great game feel and a great game loop, I think you're going to be OK"
But he argues that more generally, developers shouldn't worry too much about competition from rapidly developed AI-made games. "There's a reason that every kind of AI slop game feels the same," he says. "It's because when you try to make something from zero, AI will multiply your skills, and zero times a thousand is still zero."
"What I'm saying is that devs shouldn't be afraid of it," he continues. "Devs should just think about 'How do you make [your game] more difficult to copy?' Because game feel is impossible to copy. You can have all the references and you can have a lot of outsourcing companies trying to copy your game with proper designers, and they still fail. Why? Because of the game feel.
"As long as you have great game feel and a great game loop, I think you're going to be OK. And the other theory that keeps on confirming itself is time, right? The more time people spend in your game, the more sales you're going to get. So look at your time spent and create great game feel that people love, and everything else is secondary, I think."
Added value
Probably Stolen originated as a game called Deepspace Emporium from Questing Goose Studio, which has been reworked substantially under Tinybuild's watch.
Nichiporchik says that the demo for Deepspace had a tiny number of players, but all of them were highly engaged on Discord, so the publisher could see the potential.
The first step was reworking the original's simple graphics by bringing in a team of artists. Then the marketing team got to work on some videos. "It's a difficult one to market, because the screen is quite static," says Nichiporchik. "So if you look at the trailers, we do a lot of work there to make it look interesting. It's like zoom here, fade there, just so it feels a little bit more dynamic."
On top of that, Nichiporchik says the game's surprising depth is what has really helped to make it a hit. Beyond simply selling things, there's a host of things for players to get their teeth into. "There's a whole system of breeding fish and selling them. You don't expect an aquarium sim."
In an age where self-publishing is on the rise and people are questioning the need for traditional publishers, Probably Stolen is an example of how a publisher's input can transform a title with a tiny audience into a million-selling game. But Nichiporchik sees that times have changed.
"I agree with most developers out there that you don't need a publisher in its traditional sense"
"Well, you see, I actually don't think that publishers are needed," he says. "I agree with most developers out there that you don't need a publisher in its traditional sense. You need a partner, because it's not like the dev submits a build and then we evaluate. We spend hours together just brainstorming, figuring out what works, looking at trends.
"A lot of stuff that you do in your research, that's a lot of stuff that we do. And when that partnership works, and when we can point to something [and say], 'Hey, this is working for that other project that we have, the algorithm is picking up this kind of stuff', and the developer gets that feedback and implements it, it's a partnership. A lot of what we do I think is actually co-development if you really think about it, even though we don't touch the code – the devs do."
It's a change from the top-down approach of publishers in the past. "When publishers were the gatekeepers, it felt like they had all the power," says Nichiporchik. Nowadays, when self-publishing always remains on the table as an option, that power dynamic has shifted irrevocably.
China
One big reason behind the success of Probably Stolen is how it has taken off in China. As reported by GameDiscoverCo, the game gained momentum via Bilibili, and 51% of the 246,000 playtest activations were from China.
Nichiporchik says Questing Goose Studio found something that "culturally appeals to Chinese players" with Probably Stolen's simulation-style gameplay and cyberpunk theme, and adds that the audience reception in China went beyond Tinybuild's expectations, with Chinese players making up around three-quarters of the total at launch. More Western players have come in since then, he says. "But by going mainstream in China, you get eyeballs globally on your game, which is exactly what happened here."
Tinybuild did a lot of marketing in the country. "We have Chinese marketing people in-house, and very close to launch, we partnered up with a Chinese co-publisher, XD Games," says Nichiporchik. "They did a phenomenal job, and they were also able to help us manage critical situations." One such situation involved backlash over certain issues with the game's Chinese localization. "If it wasn't for them, we wouldn't be able to manage that situation well."
"If you make it more convenient and affordable to get your game legally, they will get it legally"
Pricing was also a big factor in Probably Stolen's success. Nichiporchik says Steam's recommendations for regional pricing are often a little too high, and Tinybuild went lower for Probably Stolen, pricing it at 55 yuan ($8.25) in China – a steep drop from the US price of $19.99. "We just look at similar games that have been hits in specific regions and adjust based on that," he says. "We're not always right, unfortunately. It's kind of like an educated guess."
He thinks that setting prices lower to reflect the cost of living in different regions can help to offset piracy. "If you make it more convenient and affordable to get your game legally, they will get it legally," he says. "I grew up pirating games myself, I'm not ashamed of it. When a game cost like 50 bucks and my mom's salary was a hundred bucks a month, it's just physically unaffordable, right?"
The stellar launch of Probably Stolen caps off a bonanza year for Tinybuild, which has seen hits in the form of ReStory, Hozy, All Will Fall, and Sand: Raiders of Sophie, as well as the hugely successful Graveyard Keeper 2, which Nichiporchik says has made nearly $10 million in gross revenue on Steam alone. "And Graveyard Keeper, the original, has one of the best long tails in our portfolio," he adds. That's key: Tinybuild titles like The King is Watching, Hello Neighbor, and SpeedRunners just keep on selling as the publisher attempts to turn its games into long-running franchises.
Fast launch, long tail. The new wisdom?
