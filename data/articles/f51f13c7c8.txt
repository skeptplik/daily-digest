A foraging vampire bat slices into sleeping prey and laps up about half of its own body weight in blood, causing its belly to balloon. Now, an analysis of the bats’ genomes and organs offers clues to how they stomach this gory diet.
The study1, posted on the preprint server bioRxiv on 7 November before being peer reviewed, provides a “high-resolution look into what it takes to build a vampire bat”, says Jasmin Camacho, an evolutionary developmental biologist at the Stowers Institute for Medical Research in Kansas City, Missouri, who was not involved in the research. “It’s one of the first times we can really see what those molecular signatures of being a blood specialist look like.”
On blood alone
Blood is a challenging meal: it contains mostly water, little sugar and fat, and toxic amounts of iron. Yet all three species of vampire bat survive exclusively on blood after reaching adulthood.
To understand how they pull this off, Michael Hiller, an evolutionary genomicist at the Senckenberg Research Institute in Frankfurt, Germany, and his colleagues generated genomes for all three vampire-bat species (Desmodus rotundus, Diaemus youngii and Diphylla ecaudata)and three other species of bat. They then compared the genomes. They also analysed gene activity in six organs for two of the vampire species and four species of non-vampiric bats.
The stomach stood out. In most mammals, the stomach bathes food in acid to aid digestion. In vampire bats, however, the researchers found that the organ has been repurposed into a stretchy, pH-neutral storage sac lined with thick layers of connective tissue that help it to expand after a feast.
To shed excess water quickly so that the bats can fly home, their stomachs ramp up activity of a fluid-transporting gene that is typically active in salivary and sweat glands by more than 900-fold, the analysis found. The function of these vampire-bat stomachs has changed so much from those of other mammals that the organs perhaps “deserve a different name”, Hiller says.
Despite the lack of fat in blood, the bats still retain some genes for digesting lipids. The activity of these genes is dialled down in adulthood but might enable nursing pups to digest their mother’s fat-rich milk before switching to blood after weaning, says Ken Welch Jr, a comparative physiologist at the University of Toronto in Canada. But the bats have lost some genes altogether, such as ones known to help animals to distinguish between sweet and bitter tastes2.
