Nato chief Mark Rutte has said the alliance will defend against any threats in space after German reports that Russia is preparing to launch a nuclear-armed satellite.
Intelligence reports about a possible launch in the next 60 days had been shared among Nato countries this month, according to German media. It was not clear which country had authored the reports.
German Chancellor Friedrich Merz said such reports were "not new, yet they remain no less shocking", and a government spokesman said the reports were being taken "very seriously".
Kremlin spokesman Dmitry Peskov said he had not seen the reports but dismissed stories citing "unknown sources" as "hardly worth taking seriously".
The Russian embassy in Berlin told German media it rejected what it called "unfounded allegations" and said the reports were part of a "propaganda campaign".
Citing anonymous German military and intelligence sources, public broadcasters NDR and WDR and newspaper SÃ¼ddeutscher Zeitung, said the Russian satellite was dubbed "Sputnik-S" and was equipped with a nuclear warhead. It could be used to disrupt or destroy other satellites, including important communication and observation satellites.
Asked about the reports, the Nato secretary general told a press conference in Berlin alongside Merz: "We will always make sure we defend every inch of Nato territory, and that includes making sure we are safe when it comes to the space element."
The Western defensive alliance had a variety of response options, said Rutte.
"When it comes to the military domain, we will always make assessments of our deterrence and defence and make updates where necessary."
Merz did not confirm or deny the reports of Russia's plans, but said they "reveal the level of escalation Russia is evidently prepared to pursue".
German government spokesman Stefan Kornelius warned against causing "excessive nervousness regarding hypothetical dangers".
Josef Aschbacher, the head of the European Space Agency, told the Handeslblatt newspaper that having nuclear weapons in space was "the ultimate nightmare scenario".
Russia is a signatory to the 1967 Outer Space Treaty, which bans weapons of mass destruction from orbit.
Bleddyn Bowen, co-director of the Space Research Centre at Durham University, said that exploding a nuclear weapon in space would have "massive infrastructural effects" as there were now more than 15,000 active satellites. But he told the BBC's Today programme that "the effect of these weapons would be extremely indiscriminate and also take out a lot of Russia's own space infrastructure".
Other countries, including the US and China, have deployed or plan to deploy other weapons in space, exploring other capabilities outside of the treaty, such as targeting rivals' satellites - which are key for military communications, surveillance and navigation.
In 2024 the US accused Russia of developing a "space-based" anti-satellite weapon armed with a nuclear warhead. Russia denied the accusation.
"Our position is clear and transparent. We have always been categorically against the deployment of nuclear weapons in space and we are still against it," President Vladimir Putin said at the time.
In September the US Secretary of the Air Force Troy Meink said the US had "on-orbit, space-control weapons" circling the planet, but did not provide specifics. In response to the announcement, the Kremlin's Peskov told Russian media that space "must be free of any weapon".
