Long-chain omega-3s are important for our heart and brain health, but most of us aren’t eating anything like the recommended amount. In fact, we couldn’t all eat enough even if we tried – the global supply of long-chain omega-3s is too low. Now, the super El Niño is making the problem even worse.
The issue is that, whether you get your long-chain omega-3s from supplements or by eating farmed fish, those almost all derive from wild fish harvested from the oceans. The mega-El Niño that developed this year is hitting key fish stocks by reducing the upwelling of nutrient-rich waters off South America. This not only reduces catches, it also lessens the amount of omega-3s in the fish that are caught. In October, Peru banned any further anchovy catches this year – and Peru alone supplies around a fifth of the omega-3-rich fishmeal and fish oil used worldwide. This will lead to a huge shortfall and soaring prices.
While this El Niño will end, the long-term outlook is dire. The world’s population is still growing, but the fish oil supply cannot be increased. What’s more, the long-chain omega-3s in marine fish ultimately derive from algae, and the amount of omega-3s that algae produce seems to depend on water temperatures. As the world’s oceans warm, the supply of omega-3s may decline. Now for the good news: we have already developed solutions. We’ve just got to use them.
The size of the problem
At this point, some of you will be thinking: “What are you talking about? I get all the omega-3s I need from eating foods such as walnuts, flaxseeds and chia seeds. Who needs fish oil?”
Well, you do. You’ll have noticed I have been talking about long-chain omega-3s. Some plants are rich in short-chain omega-3s such as ALA, but they don’t contain the two key long-chain omega-3s EPA and DHA. Our bodies can convert a little ALA into EPA and DHA, but we need to eat foods containing EPA and DHA to get enough.
We know that EPA and DHA are important for the heart and immune system, among other things, and for brain and eye development during pregnancy and childhood. But there is still a lot of uncertainty about just how much we need. That said, many organisations recommend adults get at least 250 milligrams of EPA and DHA combined per day through their diet.
For instance, the UK’s National Health Service recommends people eat two portions of fish a week, including at least one oily fish. But most people around the world get less than 250 mg of EPA and DHA in their diets, a 2014 study found. The situation more than a decade later is almost certainly worse, because over half of the fish we now eat comes from fish farms, and levels of long-chain omega-3s in farmed fish have plummeted. For instance, a 2016 study found that levels of EPA and DHA in farmed salmon from Scotland halved from 2006 to 2015.
“People are buying salmon because it’s got a health benefit,” says Johnathan Napier at Rothamsted Research in the UK. “There was no label saying ‘50 per cent less EPA and DHA’, so I think the consumer was badly misled in that respect.”
We now need to eat three portions of fish per week to get enough, he says. The reason why is that farmed salmon used to be fed entirely on fishmeal and fish oil, but, as prices have risen because of the limited supply, a growing portion of their feed has come from crop plants instead (and sometimes even chickens).
This reduces the nutritional value of our food, and it is also probably bad for the fish. “Because omega-3s are important for the immune response, they’re probably more susceptible to disease,” says Napier.
Eating more fish is also problematic. Pollutants such as dioxins and PCBs can accumulate in fish oil and then in the people eating it. The NHS advises girls and women not to eat more than two portions of oily fish a week because of the risk of these pollutants affecting fetuses.
Going straight to the source
There are two alternatives to fish oil as a source of long-chain omega-3s. One is to grow the marine algae that make it in the first place. This is already being done by a number of companies to produce vegan supplements, as well as additives for fish feeds. Algal EPA and DHA could replace a quarter of that obtained from fish oil within five years if production keeps expanding, according to a European Commission report out this year.
But Napier doubts that large-scale production will take off. “The thing that kills you in the end is the cost,” he says.
Algae don’t produce high levels of omega-3s. What they do make isn’t in an easily extractable form, and growing them requires expensive equipment, feedstocks and processing, he says. “It just costs too much money.”
The second alternative is to genetically modify (GM) plants to produce long-chain omega-3s by adding genes from algae. A GM canola (rapeseed) whose oil is around 10 per cent DHA is already being grown in the US to produce DHA for supplements and fish feed. The fish feed, produced by a Melbourne-based multinational called Nufarm, is being used in Canada and Chile as well as the US, and has also been approved in Norway.
The problem is that the GM canola produces very little EPA. Another company did manage to modify canola to produce EPA, but that produced no DHA, says Napier. His team, however, has created a GM camelina whose oil contains more than 10 per cent EPA and 10 per cent DHA. Last year, Nufarm acquired the rights to this technology.
“We can make the same level of EPA and DHA as you would find in a fish oil in our GM camelina,” says Napier, who is working with Nufarm to help get the camelina on the market.
His team has also modified the camelina to produce a pigment called astaxanthin, which gives salmon its colour. Like long-chain omega-3s, the astaxanthin in salmon ultimately derives from marine algae, so, as fish feeds have changed, producers have had to start feeding them synthetic astaxanthin made from petrochemicals, because consumers won’t buy grey salmon. In other words, the GM camelina could provide at least three of the key compounds found in fish meal.
Will this approach take off? Crop production can be scaled up more easily than algal production. In places such as Europe, there are a lot of regulatory barriers to growing and selling GM crops directly, but GM crops are already imported for animal feed.
So we will have to wait and see, but certainly in technological terms, we already have two solutions to the shortage of fish-oil-derived long-chain omega-3s. And getting them from algae or GM crops instead of fish will avoid the issues with dioxins and PCBs.
There is also the question of sustainability, but it’s hard to compare the different approaches. Catching wild fish, growing crops and cultivating algae can all be very destructive, but often in different ways. There are also many environmental issues with farming fish.
Would the best solution sustainability-wise be to cut out fish altogether and get our long-chain omega-3s directly from GM plants instead? Napier says eating fish does have benefits besides providing omega-3s. However, together with Cathie Martin at the John Innes Centre in the UK, he has created a tomato that is rich in EPA. The team is now working to boost the DHA levels.
Omega-3-rich GM tomatoes won’t reach supermarket shelves in Europe any time soon, but could do so in countries such as the US, where getting GM crops approved is much easier. I, for one, think they are a brilliant idea, and can’t wait to try them.
