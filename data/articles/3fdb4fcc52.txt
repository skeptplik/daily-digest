Abstract
In 2025, a Lancet Commission proposed distinguishing clinical obesity, defined by adiposity-related organ dysfunction, from preclinical obesity, defined by excess adiposity with preserved function, and recommended using anthropometric measures beyond body mass index (BMI) to confirm obesity status1. Here we report the comparative performance of this framework versus conventional BMI-based classification using prevalence, incidence and mortality data from 844,108 adults across four cohorts in China, the UK, the USA and Spain. Complementing BMI with waist circumference and waist-to-height ratio data showed that BMI alone overestimated obesity in 1.2–3.1% and underestimated it in 5.2–8.6% of participants across cohorts. The combined anthropometric definition increased overall obesity prevalence by 3.3–7.4% relative to BMI-based classification. Clinical obesity accounted for 22.7–31.3% of total obesity, with an overall population prevalence of 5.6–10.9%. A large proportion of individuals (between 77.4% and 91.3%) with preclinical obesity at baseline showed no progression to clinical obesity over 10 years. Increased mortality risk was associated with the combined anthropometric definition of obesity, but not with BMI-only obesity. Clinical obesity was associated with substantially higher all-cause mortality (hazard ratio 1.69, 95% confidence interval 1.64–1.73), whereas preclinical obesity conferred only modest risk (1.19, 1.14–1.24). These findings support the global adoption of the Lancet Commission diagnostic framework to improve obesity care and policy.

Main
Medical diagnoses typically define disease on the basis of objective signs of organ dysfunction2 (see https://www.merriam-webster.com/dictionary/diagnosis). By contrast, the health impact of obesity has historically been assessed using body mass index (BMI)—a proxy for body size—alongside obesity-related comorbidities3,4,5. This approach provides an aggregate measure of ill health rather than a disease-specific diagnosis.
The absence of a clinically grounded definition has impeded recognition of obesity as a distinct disease, contributing to gaps in care, inconsistent prevention, misallocated resources and persistent stigma6,7.
To address this, in 2025, a Lancet Commission from The Lancet Diabetes & Endocrinology proposed a new diagnostic framework that distinguishes between two categories of obesity: clinical obesity, defined by excess adiposity impairing organ function or daily activities; and preclinical obesity, characterized by increased adiposity and increased health risks but with preserved organ and whole-body function1. The framework also recommends supplementing BMI with other anthropometric measures, such as waist circumference (WC), waist-to-height ratio (WHtR) or fat quantification with dual-energy X-ray absorptiometry, when available, to improve the detection of excess adiposity. Although consistent conceptually with diagnostic principles in medicine, the clinical implications of this framework remain uncertain. It is unknown how substantially the recommended use of additional anthropometric measures changes the classification of obesity compared with BMI alone, or whether the distinction between clinical and preclinical obesity captures meaningfully different health states. The natural history of preclinical obesity is also poorly characterized, including the extent to which it remains stable or progresses to clinical obesity over time. More broadly, whether these classifications show consistent associations with prognosis across populations and healthcare settings has yet to be established. We examined these questions using data from 844,108 adults from four large cohorts in China, the UK, the USA and Spain, aiming to evaluate the Lancet Commission’s proposed diagnostic framework in terms of obesity classification, the distribution and longitudinal course of clinical and preclinical obesity and their associations with mortality.
Multi-anthropometric definition of obesity
To assess whether incorporating other anthropometric measures (WC and WHtR) improves obesity detection beyond the use of BMI alone, we analysed data from four cohorts: the Kailuan Study (China, n = 137,795), the UK Biobank (UKB, n = 436,203), the All of Us Research Program (AoU; USA, n = 218,841) and the SIDIAP database (Spain, n = 51,269). The characteristics of the study populations are shown in Supplementary Table 1, selection of study participants from each cohort is shown in Supplementary Figs. 1–4 and the details of study design of each cohort are presented in the Methods and Supplementary Methods. Following the Lancet Commission’s proposed diagnostic criteria1, obesity confirmation required any of the following three components: (1) BMI ≥ 40.0 kg m−2; (2) BMI ≥ 30.0 kg m−2 (≥28.0 kg m−2 for Chinese individuals) plus elevated WC or elevated WHtR; or (3) BMI 25.0–29.9 kg m−2 (24.0–27.9 kg m−2 for Chinese individuals) plus both elevated WC and elevated WHtR. Elevated WC was defined as ≥102 cm for male and ≥88 cm for female individuals (≥90/85 cm (male/female) for Chinese individuals)8,9, and WHtR thresholds varied by ethnicity (non-Hispanic white: male/female ≥0.57/0.58; non-Hispanic Black: ≥0.55/0.57; Hispanic: ≥0.57/0.59; Asian: ≥0.56/0.59; others: ≥0.50)10.
Classification based on BMI alone resulted in both over- and under-identification of obesity across all four cohorts (Fig. 1 and Extended Data Table 1). The proportions classified as obese by BMI but without elevated WC or WHtR were 3.1% in the Kailuan Study, 1.9% in the UKB, 1.9% in the AoU and 1.2% in the SIDIAP, whereas 6.6%, 5.2%, 7.5% and 8.6%, respectively, of individuals with BMI-defined overweight met both WC and WHtR criteria for obesity-level adiposity. With the Lancet Commission’s recommended multi-criteria definition, hereafter referred to as the combined anthropometric definition of obesity (CAD-obesity), overall obesity prevalence increased from 17.8% to 21.3% in the Kailuan Study, 24.0% to 27.3% in the UKB, 42.5% to 48.1% in the AoU and 18.7% to 26.1% in the SIDIAP. Overall, CAD-obesity yielded a modest net increase in obesity prevalence of 3.5% in China, 3.3% in the UK, 5.6% in the USA and 7.4% in Spain.
Clinical and preclinical categories
Clinical obesity was defined as CAD-obesity accompanied by at least one of the 18 adult diagnostic criteria specified by the Lancet Commission. These criteria represent obesity-related organ dysfunction or functional impairment across several systems, including cardiovascular (for example, heart failure, atrial fibrillation, thromboembolic disease and hypertension), respiratory (obstructive sleep apnoea and chronic obstructive pulmonary disease), metabolic (combined dysglycaemia and dyslipidaemia), hepatic (MASLD with fibrosis), renal (chronic kidney disease), neurological (intracranial hypertension), musculoskeletal (hip or knee osteoarthritis), reproductive (infertility), urological (urinary incontinence) and lymphatic dysfunctions, together with limitations in daily activities. Criteria for clinical obesity were operationalized using predefined clinical, laboratory, imaging or diagnostic criteria adapted to the variables available in each cohort. Detailed definitions and cohort-specific assessment methods are provided in the Supplementary Methods and Supplementary Table 2.
However, not all organ dysfunction that coexists with CAD-obesity can be assumed to be attributable to obesity. We therefore applied an attributable fraction (AF) approach to provide a population-level estimate of attribution and to adjust subsequent estimates accordingly11. The AF was calculated as: \({\rm{AF}}=\frac{{\rm{HR}}-1}{{\rm{HR}}}\), where HR denotes the hazard ratio for the association between CAD-obesity and risk of incident organ dysfunction with adjustment for relevant confounders using Cox proportional hazards models. The cohort-specific HRs used to derive the AF estimates were 1.62 (95% confidence interval (CI), 1.58–1.66) in the Kailuan Study, 1.68 (1.64–1.72) in the UKB, 1.67 (1.61–1.73) in the AoU and 2.07 (1.97–2.18) in the SIDIAP (Extended Data Table 2), corresponding to AF values of 0.383, 0.405, 0.401 and 0.517, respectively.
Because the distinction between clinical and preclinical obesity depends on the presence of organ dysfunction among individuals with CAD-obesity, we first quantified the observed crude prevalence of organ dysfunction and then applied the cohort-specific AF estimates to account for the proportion plausibly attributable to obesity at the population level. The crude prevalence of organ dysfunction among people with CAD-obesity ranged from 56.7% in the AoU to 75.3% in the UKB (Fig. 2; Extended Data Table 3). AF-adjusted clinical obesity accounted for 26.3% (95% CI: 25.2–27.3) of total obesity in the Kailuan Study, 30.5% (29.4–31.6) in the UKB, 22.7% (21.5–24.0) in the AoU, and 31.3% (30.9–31.8) in the SIDIAP (Fig. 2; Extended Data Table 3). Accordingly, the proportion of individuals with CAD-obesity but without any detectable organ dysfunction—representing the minimum estimate of preclinical obesity—ranged from 24.7% in the UKB to 43.3% in the AoU, and the maximum estimate of preclinical obesity (1 minus the AF-adjusted prevalence of clinical obesity) ranged from 68.7% in the SIDIAP to 77.3% in the AoU (Extended Data Table 3).
The overall prevalence of AF-adjusted clinical obesity in the full population was 5.6% (95% CI: 5.4–5.8) in the Kailuan Study, 8.3% (8.0–8.6) in the UKB, 10.9% (10.4–11.5) in the AoU and 8.2% (8.1–8.3) in the SIDIAP (Fig. 2 and Extended Data Table 3).
Sensitivity analyses restricting to the nine organ dysfunction indicators available across all datasets yielded results that were highly consistent with the primary analyses. Prevalence estimates changed minimally under the restricted definition, indicating that additional cohort-specific indicators contributed little to classification (Supplementary Tables 3 and 4). Indicators that were not universally available were individually rare, and most participants presenting them also had at least one of the nine common indicators, supporting the robustness and comparability of the classification across cohorts (Supplementary Table 5).
Ten-year stability of preclinical obesity
To assess the progression from preclinical to clinical obesity, we performed an analysis among individuals with CAD-obesity but without any organ dysfunction at baseline. The AF-adjusted 10-year incidence of clinical obesity was 21.2% in the Kailuan Study, 8.7% in the UKB and 22.6% in the SIDIAP; because of a shorter follow-up in the AoU cohort, only an AF-adjusted 4-year incidence was calculated and an incidence of 8.5% was found (Fig. 3). Thus, approximately 70–90% of individuals with preclinical obesity remained stable over a decade. The incidence rates were similar between male and female individuals (Supplementary Fig. 5), and sensitivity analysis restricting to the nine organ dysfunction indicators yielded similar results (Kailuan Study: 21.4%; UK Biobank: 8.8%).
No excess mortality with BMI alone
Cox proportional hazards models were used to assess associations of BMI-based obesity and CAD-obesity with all-cause mortality, adjusting for sociodemographic covariates. The AoU cohort was excluded because of limited follow-up. Median follow-up was 15.3 years in the Kailuan Study (17,642 deaths), 13.8 years in the UKB (31,812 deaths) and 11.2 years in the SIDIAP (2,338 deaths).
Compared with individuals with no overweight or obesity, participants with BMI-defined overweight or obesity but without CAD-obesity had a lower all-cause mortality risk (pooled HR 0.88 [95% CI: 0.86–0.90] and 0.93 [0.86–0.99], respectively). By contrast, individuals with CAD-obesity, regardless of whether their BMI was in the overweight or obese range, had a significantly higher, albeit modest, mortality risk (pooled HR 1.06 [1.03–1.10] and 1.07 [1.05–1.10], respectively; Fig. 4a and Extended Data Table 4).
Further cause-specific mortality analyses were done in the UK Biobank (the only cohort with available cause-specific mortality data), including cardiovascular mortality, obesity-related cancer mortality and their combination (Supplementary Table 6). After multivariable adjustment, BMI-defined overweight or obesity without CAD-obesity was not associated with increased risks of these cause-specific mortality outcomes. By contrast, CAD-obesity was associated with higher risks, including among individuals in the BMI-defined overweight range.
In further sensitivity analyses, exclusion of deaths occurring within the first 5 years of follow-up did not materially alter the associations, arguing against reverse causation as a major explanation for the findings (Supplementary Table 7). The proportion of missingness was low across all cohorts, with no variable exceeding 20% (Supplementary Table 8), and a sensitivity analysis restricted to participants without missing variables also yielded robust results (Supplementary Table 9). Stratified analyses by smoking status confirmed that smoking was a strong effect modifier; however, among never-smokers, CAD-obesity remained consistently associated with higher mortality risk, whereas BMI-defined overweight and obesity in the absence of CAD-obesity showed no or modest mortality risk after multivariable adjustment (Supplementary Table 10).
Higher mortality in clinical obesity
We evaluated the association between clinical obesity and mortality risk. Participants were first categorized into four baseline groups: (1) no CAD-obesity and no organ dysfunction (reference group); (2) no CAD-obesity with organ dysfunction; (3) CAD-obesity without organ dysfunction (preclinical obesity); and (4) CAD-obesity with organ dysfunction (clinical obesity). Clinical obesity was associated with a substantially higher mortality risk (pooled HR, 1.69; 95% CI, 1.64–1.73), whereas preclinical obesity showed a modest association (pooled HR, 1.19; 95% CI, 1.14–1.24; Fig. 4b and Extended Data Table 5). Cohort-specific estimates were directionally consistent.
Sensitivity analyses by excluding deaths occurring within the first 2 or 5 years of follow-up or by incorporating waist-to-hip ratio into the diagnostic indicators did not materially alter the associations (Extended Data Table 6). The association between clinical obesity and mortality was stronger in female individuals and in those aged younger than 60 years (P < 0.001 for interactions; Extended Data Fig. 1 and Supplementary Table 11). In the age-stratified analysis, sensitivity analyses by further excluding deaths occurring within the first 5 years or restricting participants without missing variables yielded similar results to those of the primary analysis (Supplementary Tables 12 and 13), providing further reassurance against reverse causation and selection biases.
To disentangle the relative contributions of BMI-defined obesity, CAD-obesity and organ dysfunction to mortality risk, participants were further classified into eight groups according to the presence or absence of each component. In groups defined by organ dysfunction and CAD-obesity, BMI-defined obesity conferred little additional risk, when compared with the BMI-defined non-obese status. On the other hand, CAD-obesity showed a consistently higher mortality risk, and the highest mortality risks were observed among participants with both CAD-obesity and organ dysfunction (that is, clinical obesity) (Extended Data Table 7).
Separate cause-specific mortality analyses of clinical and preclinical obesity in the UK Biobank dataset showed that clinical obesity was consistently associated with substantially higher risks, with effect sizes generally stronger than those observed for all-cause mortality, whereas preclinical obesity showed elevated but consistently lower risks (Supplementary Table 14).
Internal evaluation of WHtR cut-offs
We performed further complementary analyses to examine the appropriateness of the ethnicity-specific WHtR thresholds used to confirm obesity status. First, the relationship between BMI and WHtR was broadly consistent across cohorts (Pearson r ≥ 0.64; Extended Data Fig. 2). On the basis of fitted linear regression models, the conventional WHtR cut-off of 0.50 corresponded approximately to the BMI-defined overweight threshold, whereas WHtR values corresponding to the BMI-defined obesity threshold were generally higher than 0.55 (from 0.55 to 0.60 across the four cohorts).
Mortality analyses showed a similar pattern. Restricted cubic spline models indicated that mortality risk began to increase at WHtR values around 0.55 (Extended Data Fig. 3). Consistently, participants with a WHtR between 0.50 and the ethnicity-specific thresholds had no excess mortality risk (pooled HR, 0.99; 95% CI, 0.97–1.02), whereas those with a WHtR above the ethnicity-specific threshold had a higher risk (pooled HR, 1.21; 95% CI, 1.18–1.24; Supplementary Table 15).
Using 0.50 instead of the ethnicity-specific thresholds substantially increased the proportion of participants defined as having obesity by meeting the WHtR criterion across all four cohorts (Kailuan, 59.2% versus 18.3%; UKB, 66.7% versus 27.2%; AoU, 74.3% versus 48.8%; SIDIAP, 71.1% versus 33.3%). When these thresholds were incorporated into the definition of confirmed obesity, the same risk pattern was observed: participants classified only by the 0.50 threshold showed no excess mortality (pooled HR, 1.00; 95% CI, 0.96–1.03), whereas those meeting the ethnicity-specific thresholds had a higher mortality risk (pooled HR, 1.20; 95% CI, 1.18–1.22; Supplementary Table 16).
Discussion
To our knowledge, this is the first large-scale, multinational study to comprehensively evaluate the Lancet Commission diagnostic framework1 against the widely used BMI-based classification. Previous studies have examined individual components of the framework—such as expanded anthropometric criteria or prevalence estimates of clinical obesity—but have lacked integrated assessment or longitudinal follow-up12,13,14,15,16. This study evaluated several interrelated elements of the new diagnostic framework: the implications of using combined anthropometric criteria to confirm obesity; their effects on prevalence and risk stratification compared with BMI alone; the importance of selecting appropriate anthropometric cut-offs; and the prognostic implications of distinguishing clinical from preclinical obesity.
With regard to anthropometric classification, our findings show that BMI alone both over- and under-identifies obesity and does not reliably distinguish individuals who have an increased mortality risk. These results support the Commission framework’s use of complementary anthropometric measures alongside BMI to improve obesity classification and risk stratification. Although WC and WHtR are, like BMI, indirect measures of adiposity, increased values for these measures are (for anatomical and physiological reasons) more likely to reflect fat-mass expansion, whereas a higher BMI might also reflect greater lean mass. Their correlation with direct body-composition measurements10 supports their use as a pragmatic means of detecting excess adiposity where direct measurement is impractical. Future studies should determine whether direct measurement of fat adds meaningful precision and could ultimately replace combined anthropometry if reliable bedside technologies become widely available.
