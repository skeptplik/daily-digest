In 2010, the UK experienced one of its coldest ever winters, with heavy snowfall bringing major roads to a standstill. Brian, an engineer in central Scotland, had little food in the house. He donned a backpack, took one of his children’s sledges, and trudged a mile and half to queue outside the nearest supermarket. Shelves were emptying, and tempers were fraying.
Next time, Brian decided, he would be prepared. “It’s very hard to look your kids in the eye when you’re looking at a relatively empty cupboard,” he says. Brian started building up a store of non-perishable food and other supplies, ready for unexpected shortages. He’s now part of the UK’s prepper community—a group of survivalists who plan proactively for emergency situations such as natural disasters or infrastructure breakdowns. He shares his tips on YouTube as Scottishprepper73; he reckons his family could live a full year off his stash.
Preppers generally operate on the fringe. They’re used to people considering them crazy and paranoid. When Brian tells friends about his plans, they often respond with jokes. Or worse, they tell him “I’ll just come to your house.” Then, last month, the UK government launched its “Be Ready” campaign, which advised households to stock up on three days’ worth of bottled water, tinned food, and other essentials. The advice, the Cabinet Office said, was “the first stage of a three-year campaign to help households across the UK feel better prepared for events such as severe weather, which can occasionally affect services including power, water, phones and the internet.”
Prepping was flung into the mainstream, and British preppers—a minority in a largely US-dominated scene—have found themselves drawn out of their (metaphorical) bunkers and into the limelight.
For the most part, preppers I spoke to welcomed the government advice, though they unanimously felt it didn't go far enough (“What’s your plan for day four?” Mike Chappell, a Greater Manchester-based prepper, says.) But public reactions, they say, have been mixed.
Dan Goss, who is based in Liverpool and runs the Start Prepping UK website, says traffic to his site “went bananas,” reaching around 50,000 visitors at one point. The news, he says, has “broken people’s normalcy bias,” legitimising the idea of prepping in a way that no amount of bleak climate news can. He’s observed a rise in what he calls the “normal person prepper”—people who aren’t interested in planning for an apocalypse or learning advanced survival skills, but want to build basic household resilience. Glyn Smith, founder of the prepper community UK Survival Network, reports a similar spike in interest. “It’s not lone survivalists; it’s mums, dads, couples, and retirees,” he says. “Normal people who want practical peace of mind.”
There’s also been pushback. Every time prepping hits the news, Brian says, some people respond with aggression. When he’s spoken to media before, multiple people have made death threats. “‘I’m not going to waste money on preps, I’ll just buy a shotgun and come to your house,’ yada yada,” he says. For this reason, he, and several other preppers I spoke to, asked to be identified only by their first name. As well as hostility, Goss says, some people complain that they can’t afford to prep, or that the government should provide. A common sentiment, Chappell says, is that “nothing ever happens anyway.” Some accuse preppers of fearmongering.
The general image of the prepping community probably doesn’t help. Think “prepper” and you might imagine a caricature of a man in camo and armed to the teeth, either hunkering down in a nuclear bunker or camping out in the wilderness. But the preppers I speak to present a much more mundane, inclusive, and, well, British approach to prepping. They are keen to distance themselves from the idea of the “doomsday prepper.” Instead, they prep for less extreme but more likely incidents. As the UK anticipates winter weather buffeted by an El Niño climate pattern, they worry about storms, floods, and crop failures. Something as simple as a burst water pipe or power cut could cause localized chaos. Then there’s the threat of cyberattacks: Just this month, supermarket fridges and freezers across Europe were hacked in an attack France believes was conducted by Iran.
Regardless, says Ana, a prepper based in south Wales, the impacts are likely to be the same. “It’s going to be affecting potentially your food supply in the supermarkets, your water, your electricity,” she says. Ana has a food channel on YouTube, but pivoted to prepper content when she realized most people had no idea how to preserve food for long-term storage. She grew up growing food, cooking from scratch, and keeping a full pantry, and has maintained these habits. The Covid-19 pandemic reminded her just how vulnerable the UK supply chain is. She was pregnant at the time and noticed a shortage of baby formula; a supermarket worker told her people had been buying it to put in their coffee and tea.
It’s always a good idea to keep a store of basics like bottled water and long-life food. True preppers often invest in more specialized items. Brian gives me a virtual tour of the shed where he stores his supplies, neatly arranged in floor-to-ceiling shelving units. He points to different types of water filtration kits, including a LifeSaver Jerrycan capable of purifying up to 20,000 liters, and rattles off medical supplies (“Antibiotics, tourniquets, chest seals, splints”). He has a few different cooking setups, such as foldable stoves that use gas canisters, and keeps buckets of freeze-dried food that will last for 25 years. Next door, he tells me, there are 165 gallons of fresh water that he refreshes every six months.
Many preppers prepare to go off-grid if the power and internet fail, but Brian has staked his bets on a tech-heavy setup. He has a generator, his house is fitted out with solar panels, and he uses an air source heat pump for heating. He has a backup heater that runs on diesel, and several extremely powerful portable power stations (think a rechargeable power bank, but with enough capacity to keep appliances like a microwave going). He uses Starlink for internet access and runs two different mesh networking systems, with high-frequency radio acting as his ultimate fallback. In his shed, he has plenty of torches, lanterns, candles, radios, walkie-talkies, and a night-vision headset. The way he sees it, you might be able to survive without power, but it wouldn’t be much fun. “We’ve got enough tech these days that life doesn’t have to be uncomfortable,” Brian says.
This is a key difference between the US and UK prepper scenes: While many US preppers plan to “bug out” and live alone off-grid, British preppers mainly plan to stay at home. “We don’t have that vast wilderness to escape to,” says Chappell, who makes content as the UK Urban Prepper and keeps most of his at-home supplies stacked in what used to be the dining room. When we speak, Chappell has a four-week-old daughter, so his stocks include enough formula milk to get her to solid foods. Nappies are too bulky to stockpile en masse, but he has enough for a short-term issue and a fallback of old-fashioned terry towel nappies.
While prepping is often depicted as a lone wolf activity, British preppers are, on the whole, more community-minded. The scene in general has a degree of secrecy around it; some preppers keep their preps tightly under wraps out of fears that, if people know what they have, they could become a target.
Goss dismisses fears of people turning on each other in a crisis. He evokes a sense of Blitz spirit: when times get tough, he suggests, people tend to come together rather than pull apart. In any case, he doesn’t really have a choice. Like Chappell, he lives in a terraced house in an urban area—a far shout from a private bunker. “Like it or not, you’re going to wind up working with your neighbors during a big giant blackout event or something,” he says. “And it would be a really good idea for you to kind of plan for that ahead of time.” He suggests gaining trust by setting up a street WhatsApp group and doing small favors for neighbors. In an emergency scenario, pooling resources and knowledge may be more effective than going it alone. And if things did descend into looting and fighting–well, wouldn’t you want your neighbors on side?
Others thought this view might be too optimistic. “When you’re bombarded with information 24/7 and you’re deprived of any information in a general power cut, it wouldn’t take long before people get nervous and start to do stupid things because they panic,” Brian says. “You get a fear response. Fear leads to poor choices, potentially fatal ones.” He’s thought about what he’d do if friends really did turn up on his doorstep like they joke about. “It’s a difficult one,” he says. “I don’t have an answer.”
In that kind of scenario, Chappell has a plan to bug out, but just to a family member’s home. His house is surrounded by people he doesn’t know, and he can’t predict how they might react. It’s not that he doesn’t want to help people, he says, but he’s not willing to risk his family’s safety.
Of course, these plans go well beyond a three-day water-and-beans buffer. For many of the people I speak to, prepping is also a hobby. There are meet-ups and workshops. Goss says he enjoys camping and tending his allotment—activities that fit neatly into the prepper skillset (he’s also into archery, just to complete the image). But still, it’s nice to be ready, just in case. “This is not a way of life where I sit in a bunker hoping for the sky to fall. I want nothing ever to happen,” Chappell says. “But it’s insurance, right?”
