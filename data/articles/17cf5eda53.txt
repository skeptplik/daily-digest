A Bandidos gang member says it is possible he used a fake $20,000 invoice for souvlaki to pocket thousands of dollars while employed on a Big Build site.
The former CFMEU member and bikie Joel Leavitt faced a second day of examination by the royal commission into alleged corruption across Victoria’s construction sector on Wednesday.
In 2023, while employed as site health and safety representatives, Leavitt and another man, Charles Farrugia, exchanged messages which were shown to the commission.
“Just make up a quote that hits 20K and we will get the other nine off the subbies,” Leavitt wrote. “Which will mean five each.”
The commission viewed a genuine receipt for roughly $9,000 for souvlaki catering, and another allegedly fake invoice for $20,000 which was sent by Leavitt to the site superintendent, Tim Alford, from the firm Acciona.
The commissioner, Chris Kourakis AC, said the evidence strongly suggested the two CFMEU health and safety representatives had organised a meal for workers to get a few souvlaki and had pocketed $5,000 each.
“I’ll accept that,” Leavitt said. “I’m not surprised that I could have.”
The commission also viewed messages between Leavitt and Alford in which the bikie appeared to direct who could and who couldn’t be hired to work on the site.
In one message, Alford appeared to address the arrangement, saying: “I don’t care bringing your mates on and putting them in spotting roles and finding homes. But they all need to do there jobs.”
The counsel assisting the commission Chris Carr SC asked Leavitt if he, on behalf of the CFMEU, was dictating who could work on the site, to which he replied he couldn’t recall.
“I had my own demons at the time, so I may have dropped the ball on a few things,” Leavitt said. “That’s my answer to that question, counsellor.”
Earlier on Wednesday, questioning returned to a $165,000 invoice paid to Leavitt’s now defunct company, JVL Consulting, by the union-aligned subcontractor BK Labour Hire in July 2025.
When shown a copy of the invoice Leavitt was unable to recall anything about it, or even give a clear answer as to what services his former company had provided.
Later on Wednesday, the BK Labour Hire director Bernard Kearney admitted he paid the invoice despite Leavitt not having done any work for the firm.
“I just decided to pay it and move on,” he said.
“I can’t answer why.”
Carr said there were likely only a couple of reasons why BK Labour would have paid Leavitt, including a fear of violence or to suppress some information he had on them.
“Was it one or both of those two?” Carr asked.
“Neither,” Kearney said.
BK Labour also received a succession of invoices from the senior CFMEU organiser Joe Myles totalling $256,000 over about a year.
Carr had earlier asked why Leavitt appeared to become uncomfortable when asked about the invoice.
Leavitt blamed his poor memory on having ADHD, as well as PTSD from being shot in May 2023.
“Around that time I actually got told by gang squad that there was a contract on my life,” he said. “So it brought back some uncomfortable memories.
“That’s why I blocked out a lot of that time.”
Kourakis warned Leavitt it was an offence to fail to answer questions put to him by the commission, something he said could ultimately be tested in a courtroom.
“I’ve been shot … I’ve had drug issues … I am answering these to the best of my recollection,” Leavitt replied.
“How do you think you would act if you’d ever been shot?”
After the commission briefly adjourned for Leavitt to speak to his lawyers, the bikie returned to the stand and said JVL Consulting had provided safety advice to companies. “Maybe helping them out with their procedures and policies and stuff like that,” he said.
The commission viewed a copy of Leavitt’s bank statement at the time which fluctuated between $0 and minus $20, before the $165,000 landed in his account.
“Everyone likes money – I’m sure all youse are on good money for this,” said Leavitt, addressing the bar table.
Kourakis informed Leavitt that the commission had evidence he did not do any work for BK Labour.
He said an inference that could be made was that Leavitt was “standing over” BK Labour for the payment because he needed money.
“If that’s the way you want to look at it, commissioner,” Leavitt replied.
Carr asked if Leavitt knew something about BK Labour that the company would have wanted to keep quiet. “Is the conclusion the commission should draw is that you just stood over them with some sort of explicit or implied threat of violence?” he asked.
Leavitt replied: “I’d like to talk to my counsel before I answer that.”
The inquiry continues.
