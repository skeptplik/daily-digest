Researchers have used an artificial-intelligence model to read and tinker with the internal workings of another AI network — offering a new way to peek into the ‘black box’ of AI.
Computer scientists usually improve models by training them in tiny increments or by building them from scratch. A team at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) in Cambridge has demonstrated how an AI model called GPT-6 Astra can interpret the raw internal values of some other models well enough to predict what different parts of the systems do. It can then edit the models to make improvements — including boosting their mathematical abilities. The authors reported their findings in a preprint1 on 2 November.
Frontier AI models are “showing early sparks” of an ability to treat neural networks like files that they can read, reason about and change, says co-author Rikiya Takehi, a machine-learning researcher at MIT.
The results do not mean that Astra, developed by OpenAI in San Francisco, California, can explain or understand how a given AI network works. But the findings suggest that general-purpose AI models — such as ChatGPT or Claude — might be able to modify a network in useful ways, like a mechanic who can identify and fix issues in an engine that they neither designed nor fully understand.
Interpreting AI
Researchers already try to interpret how different components of a network create its outputs. The preprint shows that it is possible to automate some of these ‘interpretability’ methods, says Fazl Barez, an AI-safety researcher at the University of Oxford, UK.
Researchers hope that better understanding models in this way will help to create safer AI systems, although Barez cautions that this assumption is “largely untested”.
It remains unclear if Astra is going beyond applying information and techniques learned in its training. Barez says he would be surprised if Astra is able to read and interpret models in ways that go beyond existing human abilities.
Still, if developed further, such ability to reprogram models might be an asset in developing AI for science, says Marinka Zitnik, a computer scientist and bioinformatician at Harvard University in Cambridge, Massachusetts. For example, it could help to spot errors in a model ahead of real-world testing, she says.
Neuron editing
Frontier AI systems are based on massive neural networks — brain-inspired architectures in which layers of processing units, or neurons, are linked by billions of ‘weights’, values that represent the strength of connections inside a model. The strength of each weight is slowly nudged during training to make the model better at, for example, predicting a protein structure or the next word in a sentence.
The complex way in which a model’s features link to its outputs means they are often described as black boxes. Interpretability researchers run experiments to work out, for example, which weights are activated under specific conditions. Researchers have also previously developed custom models that can learn to edit other models2.
