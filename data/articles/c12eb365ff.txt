It’s an all-too-familiar paradox: a closet full of clothes and nothing to wear. In 2009, Rent the Runway offered a flexible, affordable solution with no storage required, and for a while, rental felt like the future of fashion. By the late 2010s, though, resale took the lead in the circular marketplace. The rise of thrifting and marketplaces like The RealReal and Depop made shopping a stranger’s castoffs feel normal, pushing secondhand retail into the mainstream.
Cut to 2026, a world where consumers are acutely aware of how polluting the fashion industry can be; many of them also lack the space to store all seasons of clothing, and prioritize affordability when it comes to their buying decisions (see: this month’s midterm blue wave). The stage is set for a rental renaissance, with Nuuly filling the gap that Rent the Runway has failed to step back into in recent years. In just the last quarter, URBN’s rental subscription service reported a 28.6% increase in net sales to $179 million, driven by a 30.4% increase in active subscribers. Once synonymous with the category, Rent the Runway began facing its own challenges, including a 3.8% decline in active subscribers and an accumulated deficit of $1.1 billion in its recent Q2 results.
Meanwhile, a new class of rental companies has arrived, each one carving out its own niche. Hauteline, Pickle, Louvelle and Isle of Monday are among the most innovative, and while each operates a bit differently, these startups share a mission of making rental feel more playful and fresh. And none rely on the subscription model that long defined rental services. Instead, all four operate through a rent-by-the-piece structure, giving shoppers more autonomy to borrow, lend and even buy.
“Our generation grew up thrifting and on Depop, and doesn’t care if something’s been worn before,” Hauteline Co-Founder Kristie Chow tells Fashionista. “It’s part of how we curate our own closets, so rental feels very natural for our demographic.”
Chow and her business partner Michelle Li founded the New York-based rental-retail hybrid with a goal to curate a closet for the ‘cool girl’ — an undeniable description, as their platform has sparked the attention of PinkPantheress, Alex Consani, Iris Law and just recently, Daniela Avanzini and Megan Skiendiel from Katseye.
Pricing varies from item to item across accessories (a Collina Strada belt at $12/day), black-tie dresses (Grace Gui’s Black Lightning dress for $33/day) and other womenswear items. Customers choose from four rental periods: four, eight, 16 or 30 days, with the total price rising as the rental period progresses. Most inventory is available to buy as well, and customers are only be charged the difference between the item’s retail price and the rental amount already paid.
When choosing what to stock, the founders consider designers’ unique perspectives. Indie brands, like the London-based contemporary label Niihai and Vietnamese designer Duy Trans’ Fanci Club, are among Hauteline’s current offerings. Because they work with smaller designers, Chow and Li are able to source inventory directly from the brands themselves; by doing so, Hauteline stocks a full size range across cuts and colors.
Hauteline also has a secret weapon: a flagship store on the ever-busy Orchard Street in New York City’s Lower East Side. “People kept talking about how difficult women’s sizing is when buying online, so it gave us confidence that people wanted to try things on,” Li says. Plus, she adds, “We have data on what everyone rents and buys in our store, so we can curate which brands’ pieces we carry and help them succeed.”
Pickle, co-founded by Julia O’Mara and Brian McMahon, took a different route to physical retail. With a flagship in New York’s West Village and a pop-up in Wynwood, Miami, the self-proclaimed “Airbnb of clothing rental” runs on a peer-to-peer network online as well, and currently has more than 600,000 items listed across 2,000 brands nationwide.
Pickle tailors its app to each market, personalizing the experience based on how locals interact with products. Established networks help rental businesses thrive in cities. “When you have that built-in network of friends, trust compounds as more people hear about Pickle from a trusted source,” O’Mara says. “It’s like ‘The Sisterhood of the Traveling Pants,’ or what we did as kids sharing with siblings, high school friends or sorority sisters.” Influencers are getting in on the sharing, too, as Lauren Wolfe and Acquired Style’s Brigette McHale have both put their closets up for rent.
The app centers on women’s clothing and accessories, with users lending and selling pieces from their own closets for anywhere from a day to four weeks. Each member has a profile that offers a window into their habits on the platform and their general location. O’Mara shares that 67% of Pickle’s sellers also rent on the platform, so the ecosystem feeds itself.
Listing an item is simple: Users add it to their closet with photos, a short description and a competitive price, then share it to curated lists.
Pickle’s curated lists also help shoppers browse by theme: “Suede Season” features a Norma Kamali fringe dress available to rent for $95 (originally retailed at $264), while “Animal Print” includes a Sandy Liang x Gap deer-print pullover to rent for $30 (originally retailed at $140). Once a request comes in, the mechanics work much like Depop’s, with the lender able to Accept, Counter-offer or Decline.
Pickle isn’t the only platform betting on the peer-to-peer format. Louvelle takes the same model and aims it squarely at lovers of designer brands. A caveat is that Louvelle is invitation-only, so you’ll either need to get a nod from an existing member or join the waitlist before you can browse or list your favorite high-end labels. Once you’re in, you’re able to borrow the likes of Acne Studios, Bottega Veneta, Elie Saab, Issey Miyake, Loewe and more.
What sets Louvelle apart for lenders is “the ability to monetize your closet and see your closet as an asset rather than a liability,” says CEO and Founder Tanya Tamer. For borrowers, it’s the “access to high-end luxury without the clutter or the financial commitment.”
But how can you trust a stranger with your $5,000 Bulgari Tubugos Clutch? Louvelle abides by a strict vetting process so that both ends are secure. After ID verification, every member also earns a trust score based on specific factors, which sets how much they can rent at one time. The more they rent, the higher the score climbs, and “then they have more flexibility,” Tamer explains.
Louvelle offers two options depending on how involved a lender wants to be: Self-Managed and Louvelle-Managed. Self-Managed works like any other peer-to-peer model, with lenders listing, shipping and cleaning on their own terms through the app. With Louvelle-Managed, a concierge team takes care of everything from listings and shipping to renter communication, returns and cleaning.
Either way, the platform accepts clothing, jewelry, handbags and accessories from its approved designers, including vintage pieces, which Tamer says are especially popular among renters.
The vintage boom isn’t limited to peer-to-peer closets, as Isle of Monday, which launched in May this year, has made it the foundation of its rent-by-piece model. Co-Founder Janelle Gray says the mission is “to democratize access to rare designer and archival pieces that have long sat offline.” Previously, renting vintage was only reserved for celebrity stylists; Isle of Monday allows the everyday consumer to participate.
When the company introduced its beta site in late 2025, the concept generated significant interest, resulting in a 30,000-person waitlist for the platform, and its following has grown since. The young company has also already had a pop-culture moment: It was featured in Tate McRae’s “Housewife” music video, where a vintage Spring ‘95 Gianni Versace dress was sourced from the New York-founded company.
Both Gray and Gabriella Carota were fixtures within the vintage world prior to creating Isle of Monday, so acquiring inventory was a smooth process. “We already worked with wholesale dealers, and we also had partners interested in being on the site on a consignment basis. To match the demand we saw from the waitlist, we aggregated our wholesale dealers and partners onto one platform,” Gray adds.
However, they were less familiar with the rental format. To test what would be most successful in unknown territory, the platform launched with only 150 products. That’s not to say that the company lacks in its product variation, though. “A big misconception in the industry is that there isn’t enough supply. For us, there’s too much,” Carota states.
A Gucci Fall ’08 fur bolero and a Moschino Cheap & Chic Spring ’02 doll dress are just a couple of the golden finds in Isle of Monday’s archive, each available for less than $500 for the shortest rental period of four days. Per the company’s website, pricing is determined by weighing “market value, condition, history and prominence,” with maintenance and care factored into the final number. Transparency extends to quality as well: A condition score is placed alongside the product listing so that customers know what to expect before renting.
The founders also offer a physical space, an appointment-only showroom in New York, where customers have full access to their inventory. “The biggest value is fostering a loyal community of customers who keep coming back,” Carota states. That human touch is central to the model. “This industry is built on trust, and it’s a very human component,” Gray adds. “We’re a fashion tech platform with a lot of technology, but our network is relationship-driven and could never be replaced by AI.”
