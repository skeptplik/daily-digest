Holidaymaker Harriet Davies was left fighting for her life after suffering side effects from drugs she believed would help her.
The 37-year-old fell ill with appendicitis while skiing in France in January.
After having surgery and being given medication there Davies, from Cardiff, returned home. But the pain persisted.
On returning to hospital, a scan revealed an abscess in her abdomen and she was given antibiotics.
But Davies became sicker. She vomited and had a fever and was given different antibiotics.
Davies said while the drugs worked she was left with sore lips and a rash.
Antibiotic resistance like this happens when bacteria do not respond to medicines designed to treat them, making infections harder to treat.
"I got a fever, and a blood test showed that my immune system had completely crashed," she said.
"The type of white blood cell that protects against infection was basically gone, I didn't have any of that which was the first line of defence.
"So, I had to go back to hospital then and have emergency antibiotics to basically stop me from getting sepsis."
Davies believed the antibiotics made her ill.
She said: "I started realising that it wasn't going the way they had expected and seeing the reaction of the clinical team around me, that was really scary for me.
"I started to realise that this is really serious and my life is potentially at risk here if I don't get better from this infection."
It was weeks before Davies got better. She urged others to be aware of drugs' side effects.
"[Antibiotics] saved my life ultimately but they also made me quite ill and I now see them as something that should only be used when they're really, really needed," she said.
"My experience highlights how quickly bacterial infections can become complicated and potentially dangerous, and how hard they can be to treat when antibiotic options are limited, particularly if someone develops a severe reaction to a treatment."
Davies and Public Health Wales (PHW) now want people to be aware of the dangers of antimicrobial resistance, or resistance to antibiotics.
The matter is being highlighted by World Antimicrobial Resistance Awareness Week, which runs until 24 November.
PHW's consultant antimicrobial pharmacist Dr Nicholas Reid said it was a misunderstanding that antibiotic resistance only affected people who took antibiotics often.
Anyone, he said, could pick up an antibiotics-resistant infection.
"When antibiotics don't work as expected, people may need alternative treatments or longer hospital care," he said.
"Protecting their effectiveness is vital, not just for treating infections but also for procedures such as surgery and cancer care."
Swansea University's Prof Thomas Wilkinson called antibiotic resistance a burden on every hospital in the world.
There were, he said, other "strategies" but he warned: "They cost money."
