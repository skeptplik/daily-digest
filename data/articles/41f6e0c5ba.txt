What does a billion dollars look like on screen? Or $1.5bn, or perhaps $2bn; estimates swing around a little for just how much money has been spent creating GTA 6. The one thing that’s certain is that it’s the most expensive game ever made – in fact, by far the most expensive piece of media ever made.
The other thing that seems certain is that it was a good investment nonetheless. It had probably grossed billions in preorders alone. News reports are dusting down a nostalgic classic format, “oh wow, videogames are really popular I guess”; this time wide-eyed at the statistic suggesting that GTA 6 alone could outgross every major Hollywood studio combined this year.
Can you see all of that on screen? All those dollars, all those thirteen years of work? Honestly, yes – the sheer scale of GTA 6 is a monumental achievement, a level of richness and detail that players will likely be exploring for months if not years to come before it yields up all its secrets. I’m only a few hours in, but already somewhat in awe of what Rockstar has created.
I also find myself wondering if it’s the last time we’ll ever see anything quite like this.
On one level, GTA 6 was the closest thing to a surefire hit the games industry could ever offer. That’s what justified spending billions in the first place; nobody ever doubted the commercial success of the final game would repay that investment handsomely. Yet in another sense, GTA 6 is an enormous, gigantically risky experiment – one whose results will create ripples across the industry for years if not decades to come.
The climate in which the game is arriving, after all, is a very different one from the world in which it started development. When the ambition of this project was being scaled out, PlayStation 4 was taking the world by storm and nascent live service titles – including GTA Online – offered the promise of a massive new wave of growth for gaming. Budgets were soaring, but budgets had always soared, and besides, money was cheap; low interest rates and strong market fundamentals justified huge investments across the board.
The climate in which the game is arriving is a very different one from the world in which it started development
More than a decade later, the world has shifted underfoot. Interest rates are high enough to make investors wary of risky projects like videogames, and the market fundamentals are frankly rancid. Audience growth has mostly stalled out, and price sensitivity has made it nigh-on impossible for revenues to keep up with inflation. Hardware costs have skyrocketed, making what should have been cheap, accessible console platforms in the sunset days of a generation into expensive systems well beyond the impulse purchase levels of most consumers.
In this climate, it’s no wonder that AAA budgets have come under the microscope more than ever before. Those budgets have climbed to the point where $200 to $300 million dollars is no longer unusual – still vastly lower than GTA 6’s cost, but even at those levels, only the biggest blockbusters can make a profit, and the costs of missing your estimates are eyewatering.
Consequently, where not so long ago the incredible impact of something like GTA 6 would have had other publishers scrambling to follow in its wake with hugely ambitious projects of their own, now I suspect that few if any of them will have the appetite for that kind of risk. The most expensive game in history also seems set to be the most commercially successful entertainment product in history, but the table stakes have almost certainly become too high for even the most ambitious, or arrogant, of Take Two’s peers.
This game doesn’t just need to be a blockbuster; it needs to be the blockbuster
Their instinct to steer clear of this behemoth and not try to follow in its footsteps will only be strengthened if GTA 6 misses any of the gigantic expectations that have attached themselves to it. This game doesn’t just need to be a blockbuster; it needs to be the blockbuster. It has to live up to an incredibly tough comparison with its predecessor, which was one of the best-selling games in the world month after month for many years, and influenced cultural conversations far outside the gaming world for the best part of a decade. GTA 6 will be measured not just on commercial figures, but on the altogether more ephemeral and tricky scale, relevance – and if it fails to live up to expectations on any of those measures, that will change the whole perception of risk around projects of this scale for decades.
Despite the game's incredible launch – both critically and commercially – there are some very apparent headwinds. Console sales figures, dampened by the hardware prices, have not been pulled out of their plunge by the imminent arrival of GTA 6; next year’s launch on PC, likely pulled forward to try to make up for the softness of the console market, will also probably be dampened by the insane prices of PC gaming hardware right now.
The bigger issue, however, is that GTA 6's launch timing was quite obviously designed to take advantage of a cross-generational sales bump, just like GTA 5 did two generations ago – effectively double-dipping across console generations. The timing may have been coincidental, but it looks careful; if the market was working in an ordinary, healthy way, GTA 6 would be arriving just far enough from the launch of PS6 to ensure that most people wouldn’t be willing to wait, but just close enough to ensure that the game was still very relevant and in-demand on the new hardware. That plan has been turned upside down, with the arrival timeline and launch plan for the next-gen hardware itself now looking distinctly iffy; it won’t hurt GTA 6’s sales this year or next, but comparisons with GTA 5’s sales curve may get tougher past that point.
There’s almost no degree of success for GTA 6 that could turn around the structural cracks in the broader industry
This is not remotely to say that GTA 6 is struggling, or will struggle – again, this game is probably going to outgross every Hollywood studio combined – but rather that the weight of expectations resting on its shoulders is immense and may be unrealistic. The nature of those expectations is also important. Ten years ago, executives across the industry might have looked at the performance of a game like this in search of an excuse to greenlight their own monstrously ambitious project. Today, they’re more likely to pick apart its performance looking for any excuse to scale back budgets instead.
That’s because there’s almost no degree of success for GTA 6 that could turn around the structural cracks in the broader industry, or undo the damage done to big-budget development by massively expensive, high-profile failures like Concord, Suicide Squad, or Skull and Bones, to name but three. GTA might be the only brand that could justify a budget in the billion-dollar scale, and no other company seems likely to try to test that theorem; one has to wonder if even Take Two itself would be willing to take a bet-it-all-on-red risk at this scale again with the franchise.
None of this detracts from what the developers have accomplished – but where GTA 5 felt like a taste of the future of gaming when it arrived, it's hard to escape the sense that GTA 6 might instead be a glimpse at a future that will never come to pass. It wouldn’t be entirely surprising if no game budget ever goes past $1bn in 2026 dollars ever again; making this unique and remarkable game into the first and last of its kind, and the swansong for a type of gigantically ambitious and monstrously expensive development project that is only becoming harder to justify over time.
