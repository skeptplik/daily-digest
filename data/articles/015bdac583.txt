A GP has warned that long waits for ADHD and autism assessments are creating a situation where the people most in need are not being seen.
According to experts, demand has risen so sharply in recent years that it now far exceeds what the mental health system can cope with.
New figures seen by BBC Scotland News suggest more than 38,000 adults are waiting for neurodevelopmental assessments, with at least 14,000 of them waiting longer than two years.
Dr Marianne McCallum, who represents the Deep End group of doctors working in Scotland's most deprived areas, said it was a "tragedy" that people who really needed a diagnosis because they were not coping could not be seen by a specialist.
She said those who had the most to gain from a diagnosis were currently the least likely to get it, with a huge rise in private referrals creating a two-tier system.
Dr McCallum, who works in the Ferguslie Park area of Paisley, said: "It's just become a bit of a mess, because there's a bottleneck and the secondary services are overwhelmed.
"In primary care we're trying to hold a lot of this without any extra resource."
Dr McCallum said some patients were influenced by social media.
"I've had people who've come in saying they think they need an ADHD assessment and actually when I've spoken to them, the problem is dyslexia," she said.
She said this meant some people who had a really significant lifelong neurodevelopmental condition were unable to access services.
"It is a tragic situation, particularly for people who really could do with the diagnosis," Dr McCallum said.
"Sometimes they lose their job because they're not coping and they snap and that's it. So I think it is a tragedy."
Autism is a lifelong developmental condition that affects the way a person communicates, interacts, and processes information.
There is no medical treatment for the condition.
Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder, external (ADHD) can affect different brain functions including communication, emotions and attention.
Drugs that can help symptoms can only be prescribed by the NHS if the patient has an NHS diagnosis, or under what is called a "shared care" agreement.
But most GPs will not offer this for a private diagnosis.

In recent years, Scotland has seen an unprecedented rise in the number of people seeking assessment and support for both ADHD and autism.
But a 2025 report by the Royal College of Psychiatrists in Scotland (RCPsychiS) said there was no national approach to meeting that demand.
It found the scale of need "far exceeded" what Scotland's mental health system could deliver.
The report found people with autism and ADHD were at higher risk of developing other mental health conditions and at a higher risk of suicide, which could be reduced with timely diagnosis and care.
It also said those with more severe mental illness faced longer delays accessing urgent care.
The data tracking website ADHD.Scot asked all of Scotland's 14 regional health boards how many people were waiting for an assessment, how long they were waiting, and how many assessments had been carried out in the past year.
Nine responded with full figures but five health boards could not provide comparable data.
Overall, the figures suggested that only 1,575 assessments were carried out on the NHS for neurodevelopmental conditions in the last year for which data was available.
NHS Lothian, which had the most people waiting (13,253), only saw 537 patients for their first appointment last year.
In Greater Glasgow and Clyde, more than 9,000 adults were waiting for an ADHD assessment, with about 559 seen in the year to March 2026.
For autism, Highland had 372 adults on the list for assessments, of which 290 had been waiting over two years and the longest wait was about seven years.
Only eight people in Highland received a first appointment with a specialist in the last year.
Last week, the Court of Session ruled against a man who had taken NHS Tayside to court, claiming it was impossible for him to get an ADHD assessment after being referred by his GP.
His case was that the health board's policy now excluded those who did not also have other mental health conditions.
The judge accepted the health board's position that there had been no change in policy and, in fact, it was the huge increase in demand for assessments that meant his case had not been a priority for those managing local waiting lists.
The Scottish government is expected to publish plans to improve neurodevelopmental support for adults in the coming weeks.
A spokesperson said: "Demand for adult neurodevelopmental diagnosis and support has risen significantly in recent years, placing considerable pressure on services across Scotland.
"We recognise that this can be a frustrating and uncertain time for people. However, support should be available based on people's individual needs, rather than having to wait for a diagnosis for help."
