Scientists exploring the Mariana trench with a remotely operated submarine rover have discovered the deepest evidence of explosive volcanic activity to date. Experts say that the nature of the activity is also unusual and ripe for further exploration.
Robert Stern, formerly at the University of Texas at Dallas, and his colleagues were collecting rock samples at the Mariana trench – the deepest valley in the world’s oceans, and home to the deepest known point on Earth, Challenger Deep – to understand the geology of the unique spot.
The Mariana trench began to form about 52 million years ago when one tectonic plate – the Pacific plate – slid or “subducted” under another called the Philippine Sea plate. At Challenger Deep, the trench is almost 11,000 metres below sea level.
Focusing on an area about 6100 metres deep, in the vicinity of Challenger Deep, the team found deposits containing volcanic ash and rock – evidence of the deepest volcanic eruptions ever reported. The location was also unusual, occurring in an area where the mantle is generally not thought to be hot enough to melt. The team made the discovery using a remotely operated submersible named Jason.
While the team hasn’t yet identified exactly where the eruptions occurred, only finding evidence of volcanic activity in samples, they believe that it can’t have occurred far above the collection site on the trench wall. This is because the volcanic ash and rock in the samples had welded onto rocks at the sample site, showing it was hot when it was deposited. If the volcanic activity had occurred at a much shallower depth, the volcanic ash would have cooled by the time it reached the site 6000 metres below sea level.
Peter Kokelaar at the University of Liverpool, UK, says that the site sits closer to the meeting point of tectonic plates than would usually be expected. “It’s probably the deepest product of an active volcanic system that’s been recorded,” says Kokelaar. “It’s particularly interesting because it’s a peculiar place for active volcanism, so close to the trench.”
Peter Rowley at the University of Bristol, UK, says that the site seems to show evidence that the Pacific plate is subducting deeper under the Philippine plate than expected, creating a stretching effect on the upper section of the Philippine Sea plate and thinning it out. This thinning might make it easier for mantle material to punch through the crust to trigger volcanic activity.
“Material that was originally quite deep down, stuff from the upper mantle, is actually quite accessible. So what they’re sampling is material which has some mantle origin to it. And getting to access mantle material is something we’ve been trying to do in various locations for a very long time,” says Rowley. “It’s an unusual tectonic setup.”
“So, certainly, [this is] explosive volcanic activity at greater depths than we’ve seen before,” says Rowley. “Possibly this is the deepest volcanism we’ve seen. Submarine volcanism is something we’ve got quite big knowledge gaps in. It’s that famous thing that we know less about the seafloor than we do about the moon.”
