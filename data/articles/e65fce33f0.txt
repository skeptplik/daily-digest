Melissa Shusterman was desperate for help with her menopausal symptoms: body aches, lethargy and brain fog, which she described as feeling “not myself”. The Pennsylvania state representative saw a gynecologist, a physical therapist, a chiropractor and a urologist. One doctor gave her lubricant, even though she’d already explained to him that sex was not the problem. (“Have you seen my husband?” she quipped.)
“I am more than lubrication,” Shusterman said. “I am a dynamic, smart, hardworking woman who wants to continue to feel good, so I can join my contemporaries, get back to work, and change things.”
In September, she was joined by dozens of women at a rally at the state capitol, as she announced a suite of bills aimed at helping menopausal people in Pennsylvania have a better experience than she had. The legislation, which would mandate insurance coverage for menopausal care, bolster public education around menopause and more, is one of the most ambitious in the country. But it’s also part of a larger movement.
Almost half of American women between the ages of 40 and 60 experienced menopausal symptoms last year, according to a recent federal study. These symptoms, like hot flashes and vaginal dryness, can interfere with people’s sex lives, moods and even their ability to work. Still, only about 16% of the women in the study took any prescription medication for their symptoms – a reflection, advocates say, of barriers to access and a lack of awareness around a period of life that’s too often been ignored or dismissed.
“It is still so taboo,” said Cheryl Bergman, executive director of the Michigan Women’s Commission, which released a report on menopause policy earlier this year. “Most of the time I bring the conversation up with men, even progressive men, they take a step back. They just literally step away from it.”
Now a group of lawmakers and activists, many of them women in their 40s and 50s, are working to break that taboo, opening up a new frontier in reproductive health policy. And they’re notching real victories, even in a time of dwindling access to reproductive health care around the country. In states from Rhode Island to Utah, lawmakers have passed measures to expand insurance coverage for hormone therapy, protect menopausal workers from discrimination on the job, and more.
In Pennsylvania, “we’re going to continue pressing until these bills become law,” Shusterman said. “This is about feeling yourself again.”
The many barriers to menopause care
It’s not the first time menopause care in the United States seemed poised for a renaissance.
In 1991, the National Institutes of Health launched the Women’s Health Initiative, a clinical trial designed to measure the health impact of hormone therapy during menopause. Model Lauren Hutton even did commercials for hormone therapy, becoming “the celebrity face of menopause”, said Jennifer Weiss-Wolf, author of the new book When in Menopause: A User’s Manual and Citizen’s Guide.
Then, in 2002, the Women’s Health Initiative abruptly shuttered part of its study, announcing that hormone therapy was associated with increased risks of breast cancer and heart disease. Doctors stopped prescribing the treatment, and patients stopped asking for it. The share of women prescribed hormones for their menopausal symptoms dropped from about 27% in 1999 to less than 2% in 2024, Weiss-Wolf writes in her book.
Today, most experts agree that the study design was flawed. Earlier this year, the American College of Obstetricians and Gynecologists recommended hormone therapy as the first-line treatment for menopausal symptoms like hot flashes, except in women with a history of certain conditions, like breast cancer. Meanwhile, newer non-hormonal options can offer relief from some symptoms.
But many patients aren’t being offered any of these options. Finding a doctor who’s actually trained in menopause management can be difficult or impossible, said Lauren Streicher, a clinical professor of obstetrics and gynecology at Northwestern University. In one 2022 survey, just 31% of OB-GYN residency program directors said menopause training was included in their residencies.
The problem is especially severe in red states where restrictive abortion laws have led to an exodus of OB-GYNs. “There’s no one to give menopause care or do anything else,” Streicher said.
Even if patients do find a doctor, only a few states have legislation mandating insurance coverage for hormone therapy, or for non-hormonal options, which can cost more than $600 per month. “The barriers go on and on and on,” Streicher said.
How advocates are fighting for access
In the last few years, legislators, advocates and public figures around the country have been working to break down those barriers. In 2020, Michelle Obama invited Sharon Malone, an obstetrician and menopause practitioner, on to her podcast to talk about menopause. In 2023, Oprah Winfrey hosted a panel on the issue with Drew Barrymore and Maria Shriver. In 2024, Halle Berry climbed the steps of the US Capitol with a group of senators to call for more federal funding of hormone-therapy research.
“I’m in menopause, OK?” she shouted.
And over the last two years, more than 60 bills aimed at improving menopause education, insurance coverage or workplace accommodations have been introduced at the state level, Weiss-Wolf said. At least 21 have passed.
Many bills have bipartisan support, and several have passed in red states. Louisiana, for instance, became one of the first states in the country to mandate insurance coverage for menopause care in 2024.
The movement’s energy comes from the women behind it, many say.
“Most of us are millennials and gen Xers, raising their voices because they’re experiencing something in their own lives,” said Pennsylvania state representative Morgan Cephas, who is sponsoring a bill, part of the larger package introduced in September, that expands state Medicaid coverage for menopause care, and one that provides workplace protections for people experiencing symptoms.
Women in midlife today have faced crisis after crisis, from the Great Recession to the wars after 9/11, Cephas said. “We’re also in the sandwich generation. We’re raising kids, but we’re also taking care of our parents,” she said. “I think we’re just in a moment that forces us to fight against the tide of us not being able to advance.”
Access to menopause care is an economic issue, advocates say. “Women get sidelined just as they’re reaching the pinnacle of their potential in their chosen work” because of misdiagnosed or untreated menopause symptoms, Shusterman said.
But for many, it’s also part of a larger conversation about reproductive health access in America. “The fall of Roe was a nail in the coffin for us because now we’re looking at our children and understanding that they have less reproductive rights than our parents had,” Cephas said. “While we’re having conversations about protecting our rights to abortion access, which is healthcare, we begin thinking of the entire continuum of healthcare for women.”
Democrats’ success in the midterm elections may help speed menopause legislation along. In Pennsylvania, Democrats flipped the state Senate and held the House and governorship. With such a trifecta, “I believe we’ll see a good number of these bills fly through,” Cephas predicted in an interview before election day.
Shusterman, meanwhile, sees the package as a model for state legislatures around the country. In addition to Cephas’s bills, the package also includes legislation requiring private insurance coverage for menopause care, as well as bills aimed at expanding coverage for specific conditions related to menopause, like osteoporosis and urinary tract infections. “I encourage other states to take every one of these bills and introduce them,” she said.
“This was the first package of bills I worked on where we heard from our community, our constituents, far and wide, across the aisle, political, non-political, independent, conservative,” Shusterman said. “People want to be seen. We need to be seen and cared for, because a lot of times women are the ones caring for everyone else.”
