Kate Bush, Grace Jones and Sir Ian Rankin have joined 7,000 artists and creatives around the world pleading for the Australian government not to weaken copyright law, urging Labor not to “not legalise the theft of the work of the world’s creators on their soil”.
The international push, the latest in a global pushback against artificial intelligence companies and their requests to water down copyright, comes the same day a long-awaited parliamentary committee handed down a mammoth report calling for copyright reform in Australia.
In an interview with Guardian Australia, Labor MP Jo Briskey, the committee chair, maintained that current copyright laws prevented frontier AI models from being trained on Australian content. Annabelle Herd, CEO of recording industry association Aria, said any move to weaken those protections was bad for creatives.
“Proposing to weaken the very right that gives creators commercial control and leverage and calling it additional protection is gaslighting,” Herd said.
While senior ministers in the Albanese Labor government have claimed the “status quo” on copyright is not viable, a growing global coalition is trying to change Australia’s course. Music icons Bush and Jones are among thousands of names who have signed up to the pledge, as musicians and artists fear AI models could train on content from anywhere in the world if Australia weakens copyright law.
“The Australian government must not legalise the theft of the work of the world’s creators on their soil. Our work must only be used to train AI models in Australia with our explicit permission,” the statement reads.
Organised by former tech executive Ed Newton-Rex, more than 7,000 artists have backed the campaign, including the likes of literary figures Joanne Harris, Bonnie Garmus and Andrew Motion; artists Tim Flach, Maia Kobabe and Woodrow Phoenix; and musicians Andreya Triana, Steve Jones and Anthony Phillips.
It comes after Elton John last week backed the Not AI’s To Take campaign, a coalition of Australian creatives pushing back on moves to change copyright law.
Although the prime minister, Anthony Albanese, has declared it would be “theft” if creatives weren’t paid for their work, some AI companies have argued it is too difficult to make individual licensing deals under existing copyright law. AI companies have said they need legal certainty on the copyright issue before investing and training models in Australia, with Anthropic putting forward an “opt-out” model to scrape creative works unless a rights holder objected.
Parliment’s joint standing committee on AI released its report on Tuesday, a 400-page report with dozens of recommendations on issues from datacentres to online safety rules.
The committee’s findings are not official government policy but will inform a major package of AI legislation expected to be unveiled by Labor early next year.
Copyright was a major piece of the report, with the majority view of the committee – with some exceptions, including independent senator David Pocock – recommending the government “strengthen Australia’s copyright protections”, including designing a “mechanism to make it easier and fairer for Australian rights holders and creators to be paid by AI companies training frontier and other large language models in Australia”.
This comes despite key rights holder agencies, including music publisher group Apra Amcos and Aria, disputing such rhetoric and saying copyright changes were not needed. Dean Ormston, the CEO of Apra Amcos, told Guardian Australia this month: “There’s nothing wrong with the copyright framework. The problem is getting somebody to sit at the table and have a discussion with us.”
News publishers also raised concerns about potential copyright changes. News Corp’s executive chair, Michael Miller, said “encouraging commercial deals sounds right” but wanted to see more detail.
“Any framework that lets trillion-dollar AI platforms scrape content without voluntary agreements isn’t innovation – it’s state-sanctioned theft,” he said.
Danika Johnston, interim managing director of Guardian Australia, welcomed the inquiry findings that the creative and news media sectors must retain control of their work and be paid fairly, but said “payment is not permission”.
“A strong copyright regime preserves the fundamental right of copyright holders to authorise use of their work and negotiate the terms and price, or refuse use altogether. Any scheme that removes that choice would weaken copyright,” she said.
“The Guardian supports responsible AI innovation and has been proactive in securing licensing deals, proving that a voluntary licensing market is already developing. Australia can support AI investment while also sustaining public-interest journalism and the creative sector.”
The inquiry report also called for “incentives” to be set for companies to make deals. Such a mechanism for deal-making could resemble the news bargaining incentive, under which large tech platforms are obliged to make commercial deals with news publishers or face a larger levy.
Briskey said the keenness of AI companies like Anthropic to set up operations in Australia gave the government “leverage” to seek stronger protections for Australians, including on safety, training and copyright.
“What we heard from the evidence was that under the current regime and the current framework, it does not enable or allow for training,” Briskey said, citing complaints from major AI companies.
“Arguably the only way we can make sure that we get those deals for the copyright holders, that we get a pathway for individual rights holders and individual creators, is if frontier is happening on our shores under our own laws.”
But that was strongly rejected by Aria. Herd is quoted in the report itself as saying “this line that no-one’s getting paid right now is actually not correct”. In comments on Tuesday, she claimed existing copyright laws already allowed rights holders to sue AI companies if they infringed copyright.
“Copyright owners have asked again and again for government to make AI companies come to the negotiating table. The parliament must not overturn centuries of copyright law on the fallacy that the law doesn’t work,” she said.
Pocock, who wrote a dissenting report to the main consensus findings, said he was strongly concerned about any copyright changes. He called for a three-year moratorium on copyright changes and urged against rushing to change laws for the benefit of AI companies.
