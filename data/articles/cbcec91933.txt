Certain types of hormone therapy for menopause are linked to better memory performance and a reduced risk of mild cognitive impairment, according to data presented at a neuroscience conference that began on 14 November. The findings suggest that certain therapies could be protective against the brain changes that often precede Alzheimer’s disease, say the authors, although more research is needed to confirm this effect.
The research is part of a series of studies presented at the Society for Neuroscience’s Neuroscience 2026 conference that reveal differences in cognitive decline and Alzheimer’s disease (AD) progression on the basis of biological sex or life stage. The studies have not been peer reviewed.
Women are twice as likely to develop AD than are men. But the reasons for these sex differences are poorly understood and poorly studied, say researchers in the field. “The women’s health gap is huge,” Liisa Galea, a neuroscientist at the University of Toronto in Canada, told reporters at the conference, which was held in Washington DC. Part of the problem is that studies often do not analyse sex differences, adds Galea.
Researchers think that hormonal changes that happen during menopause could partially explain why the disease affects women and men differently. For instance, going through menopause early, between 40 and 45 years old, is thought to increase a woman’s risk of cognitive decline and developing AD. Some studies have found that oestrogen — which decreases during menopause — has a protective effect on the brain.
Hormone therapy
Mateja Perović, a neuroscientist at the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health in Toronto, and her team wanted to know whether menopausal hormone therapy (MHT), which is used to supplement oestrogen levels and treat symptoms of menopause, can slow cognitive ageing and reduce the risk of developing AD. Perović says previous studies have showed mixed results, but these studies had not differentiated between the different types of MHT1.
The team analysed the cognitive function of around 14,000 women who received different types of MHT or none at all2. They also compared cognitive function of women using MHT who had a genetic risk of developing AD with those who didn’t.
They found that oestradiol-based treatments — which contain a compound that is chemically identical to the oestrogen produced by the body — were associated with better episodic memory in women without cognitive impairment than were treatments containing a mix of natural and synthetic oestrogens. Episodic memory refers to the ability to learn and recall information. A deficit in this can be an early symptom of AD. Women who did not receive any MHT also showed better episodic memory than those who received the mixed-oestrogen treatments.
Oestradiol-based MHT also showed benefits for women who were not genetically predisposed to AD and had mild cognitive impairment, whereas the mixed-oestrogen MHT did not.
Genetic risk
Both treatment types were beneficial for other types of long-term memory function compared with no MHT for women who had mild cognitive impairment and carried a gene called APOE4, which is linked to an increased risk of AD. But MHT use did not affect the risk of progressing to dementia, suggesting that the treatments might improve a person’s cognitive function without preventing the disease.
When the researchers looked at structural brain changes using imaging techniques, they found that women with the APOE4 gene who took oestradiol had higher cortical thickness and less white-matter damage than did women not receiving MHT. Decreased cortical thickness, caused by the loss of neurons, is associated with decreased brain function and can be an early indicator of cognitive impairment. The findings suggest that oestradiol-based MHT might be particularly beneficial for women at higher risk of developing AD, says Perović.
Mia Schaumberg, who studies healthy brain ageing at the University of the Sunshine Coast in Sippy Downs, Australia, says it is promising that research is considering individual risk factors, such as genetics, and differences in MHT composition when looking at women’s cognitive health. “It is starting to gain the attention that it deserves,” she adds.
Although Susan Davis, an endocrinologist and researcher at Monash University in Melbourne, Australia, says the effect of MHT on AD risk needs to be investigated in a randomized clinical trial to validate the findings of this study.
