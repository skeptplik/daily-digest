Philadelphia neonatologist Joanna Parga-Belinkie on keeping babies safe from measles
Amid ongoing measles outbreaks, Philadelphia neonatologist Joanna Parga-Belinkie talks with NPR's Scott Simon about how she keeps infants too young for the vaccine safe.
SCOTT SIMON, HOST:
The reemergence of measles in the United States has meant a whole new set of decisions for parents whose children are too young to get the vaccine. Kendra Doty's 2-month-old hasn't been out much, and that means she can't get out much.
KENDRA DOTY: We have asked our pediatrician about getting him vaccinated. He can't be vaccinated for about a year, and it's changing where we take him and whether we go see family. So it's been a little bit hard.
SIMON: Doty's a new mother in Washington, D.C. She says she'd love to see family in Idaho, but for now she's holding off.
DOTY: I don't really know anything about the measles - just that it, you know, used to not be a thing that we needed to worry about.
SIMON: Measles was effectively eliminated in the U.S. more than 25 years ago, but health officials revoked that status this week. More than 5,000 cases have been reported just this year.
Caitlin Jones (ph) lives in Chester County, Pennsylvania, a major center of the measles outbreak. She and her husband had planned to take their 4-month-old to a family reunion in the Caribbean this Thanksgiving. But the pediatrician discouraged their baby going through an airport.
CAITLIN JONES: The larger family is still going, but we are no longer going 'cause we can't go to the Philadelphia Airport.
SIMON: And Caitlin Jones' daily routines have also been changed.
JONES: I can't take my son to any, like, reading activities at the library. I can't take him into any stores. I can't go and get food. I can't go shopping. It kind of is very reminiscent of COVID times.
SIMON: The U.S. public has plenty of practice hunkering down to try to stay away from a pathogen. But with this reemergence of measles, it's mostly just the parents who weigh risks - like Kate Anderson, who dropped off her 5-month-old and 2-year-old at a daycare in Washington, D.C., when we caught up with her.
KATE ANDERSON: I feel nervous, and I know that our younger hasn't had the vaccine. But you can't really prevent your older child from bringing home things to your younger child.
SIMON: I'm joined now by Dr. Joanna Parga-Belinkie. She is the spokesperson for the American Academy of Pediatrics and a practicing neonatologist in Philadelphia. Dr. Parga-Belinkie, thanks so much for being here.
JOANNA PARGA-BELINKIE: Thank you so much for having me.
SIMON: How did we go from measles being labeled eliminated in the United States 25 years ago to now be an endemic?
PARGA-BELINKIE: You know, it has to do with declining vaccination rates on the whole. We need 95% of the community to be vaccinated to keep the spread of measles at bay, and we've seen an erosion in that vaccine rate.
SIMON: Fewer people getting the vaccine, which reduces the effectiveness of the vaccine.
PARGA-BELINKIE: Yes, and it's a very effective vaccine. It's 97% effective in preventing measles when you get the two doses.
SIMON: What do you tell families in Pennsylvania?
PARGA-BELINKIE: Yeah. It was hard to listen to that family that's out in Chester County because they're in the epicenter of our very large outbreak here in Pennsylvania. The first thing I say to families is that I am sorry that our community rates are so low that we're seeing a spread of this virus that people haven't seen. And it is something that we need to be aware of and be vigilant about. And so, you know, I talk about that vigilance when taking babies out.
You know, I also wanted to mention that you can get the vaccination as early as 6 months old. Now, it doesn't count toward those two vaccines to get that 97% effectiveness and immunity, which we usually recommend at, you know, around age 1. And then we used to say age 4, but we recommend it earlier when you're in an area with an active measles spread. So there is hope of getting an earlier vaccine if you talk to your pediatrician, and so that's something I'll chat with them about. But I also talk with them about how I'm sorry that this is happening and how we need to be talking about the vaccine, and we need to be talking about it in the community to get community rates of vaccination up.
SIMON: What do you tell those families who feel they're a long way from the outbreaks?
PARGA-BELINKIE: You know, especially with travel around the holidays, you know, it was really powerful to hear about those families and how they're rethinking their travel. When I think about vaccination, I say, make sure you get that vaccine two weeks before you travel so that you can make sure to have immunity if you do travel. And I say all this because while these outbreaks happen in certain areas, it's such a contagious virus. And it can travel and spread very easily. So even if it's not in your community, just the visit of one person with measles to your community can change all of that.
SIMON: Where do you see the situation going at the end of this fall and then into winter?
PARGA-BELINKIE: You know, I worry because measles is, and had been in the past, typically a winter virus. So I am worried that we may see more cases going through the winter. And I also worry, you know, about what this means for other vaccine-preventable diseases that we might see. In particular, as the - as a doctor that takes care of babies, I worry about things like pertussis, for example, which requires vaccination as early as 2 months of age.
SIMON: What would you tell parents who are reluctant to have their children vaccinated?
PARGA-BELINKIE: I would say to find a healthcare professional that they can really talk to about the vaccine, to look at resources from the American Academy of Pediatrics, because there's a lot of bad information or confusing information online about vaccination. And when you go to sources like the American Academy of Pediatrics, I think they do a good job of laying out in plain terms the importance of these vaccines and how they teach the immune system.
And I also say to look out for resources from the Children's Hospital of Philadelphia Vaccine Education Center. And through that, you can actually talk with doctors about vaccines, too, if you can't find a healthcare professional near you. So I really mention those resources and really want to encourage conversation and to answer their questions and hopefully allay their fears about vaccination.
SIMON: Dr. Joanna Parga-Belinkie is spokesperson for the American Academy of Pediatrics and a practicing neonatologist in Philadelphia. Thanks so much for joining us.
PARGA-BELINKIE: Thank you so much for having me.
