The paper fortunes of Anthropic’s seven co-founders reached a total of more than $180bn on Thursday as shares in their company started trading at a price ahead of expectations.
The opening price of $111.50 (£85) per share valued the entrepreneurs’ stakes – including the chief executive, Dario Amodei, and his sister Daniela – at between $26.5bn and $27bn each. The founders have pledged to donate 80% of their shareholdings in Anthropic to philanthropic causes, which would equate to about $150bn at the current share price.
Other investors generating considerable wealth from the float and the late share price surge include Madonna’s manager, Guy Oseary, whose venture capital firm, Sound Ventures, which he co-founded with Hollywood actor Ashton Kutcher, reportedly owns 0.5% as an early investor. That stake would be worth $10bn at the opening price.
At the closing price of $97.17, the combined co-founders’ stakes had fallen in value to just over $160bn at around $23bn each. The Sound Ventures stake was worth about $8.75bn.
Anthropic has also committed 5% of its shares to the Anthropic Public Benefit Fund, which will “explore large donations to traditional nonprofits or direct distributions to specific populations”. The company has said some of the proceeds could be used to address AI’s impact on the job market, though fears the technology will lead to significant job losses have yet to be realised.
Anthropic’s IPO is widely expected to channel vast sums into the coffers of a powerful social movement called effective altruism, via the many millions of dollars that its 5,300 staff will have made from the flotation. EA, as it is known, crystallised on university campuses in the US and UK in the early 2010s. It encourages its members to donate a substantial portion of their income to a set of specific causes that it deems high-priority. These include things like pandemic prevention, biorisk, nuclear safety and – overwhelmingly – the risks posed by advanced AI.
Anthropic’s co-founders, including Dario Amodei, are associated with EA. Amodei took a pledge to donate his income through the organisation Giving What We Can, which is associated with the movement. However, he now disavows his association and has said he does not consider himself an effective altruist.
In 2023, he told The New York Times’ Hard Fork podcast he was “sympathetic to a number of the ideas” promoted by EA, but against “dividing people up into tribes”.
Meet Anthropic’s co-founders
Dario Amodei, 43
Anthropic’s San Francisco-born chief executive was educated at Stanford and Princeton universities. He worked at the Chinese tech firm Baidu and then at Google before joining OpenAI, the developer of ChatGPT and now Anthropic’s arch-rival, where he had a key position as vice-president of research.
In 2020, Amodei left OpenAI, and it has been widely reported that his departure was due to conflicting views over safety. “It is incredibly unproductive to try and argue with someone else’s vision,” he said years later.
AI safety remains a major concern for Amodei. He called for a slowdown in AI development in September after the resignation of an employee who cited concerns AI could wipe out humanity and triggered a wave of concern about the existential threat posed by the technology.
Amodei is also blunt about the potential impact of AI on jobs, claiming that AI could eliminate half of all white-collar jobs by the end of the decade.
Daniela Amodei, 39
Amodei, Dario’s sister, began her career in politics, working in Congress and on a congressional campaign, before switching to tech and taking a job at Stripe, the payment processor. She moved to OpenAI before co-founding Anthropic with her brother. She leads teams on AI model safety and alignment, making sure AI systems adhere to human aims and values.
Like other Anthropic employees and co-founders, Amodei is tied to the EA movement. She is married to one of its major donors, Holden Karnofsky, who co-founded GiveWell and the major grant-giving organisation Open Philanthropy.
Jack Clark, 38
This Brighton-born former journalist has been catapulted into the list of richest Britons with the Anthropic flotation. His paper wealth of approximately $26.6bn (£20.3bn) compares with £38bn for the London-based Hinduja brothers and puts him above the £18.2bn fortune of the Reform UK donor Christopher Harborne and Manchester United’s co-owner Jim Ratcliffe, who is worth £15.2bn. Clark leads the Anthropic Institute, a thinktank devoted to researching the social implications of advanced AI.
In a lecture at Oxford University this year, Clark predicted that an AI system will work with humans to make a Nobel prize-winning discovery within 12 months, and that tradespeople will be helped by bipedal robots within two years. He added that it would be better if humans could slow the development of the technology “to give ourselves more time as a species” to deal with the implications of its powers.
Jared Kaplan, 42
Anthropic’s chief science officer received a PhD in theoretical particle physics from Harvard before working as a researcher at OpenAI, then co-founding Anthropic. He is known for his work on scaling laws – the idea that AI models grow smarter and more powerful in proportion with the resources that are spent on training them.
In an interview with the Guardian last year, Kaplan focused on the major AI safety concern that animates much of Anthropic’s work – the power and potential risk of AI systems that can improve themselves. Creating such systems would be “the ultimate risk,” he said.
Tom Brown, 39
Anthropic’s chief compute officer, who is in charge of the company’s use and procurement of datacentres, was a senior engineer at OpenAI. Given his job title, he is unsurprisingly supportive of a divisive topic in US politics: whether to build more datacentres. Speaking in September, Brown praised Donald Trump’s social media post stating “let data reign”.
“He was pointing out that datacentres are just an enormous source of prosperity,” Brown said. The midterm election results indicated that many voters disagree.
Chris Olah, 34
Chris Olah dropped out of a maths programme at the University of Toronto, then later took up a fellowship provided by tech billionaire Peter Thiel. This led to work at Google and OpenAI, where he studied neural networks, the technology that underpins large language models, which in turn power chatbots.
Olah made headlines this year for his close relationship with Pope Leo. He stood beside the pope this May, when the pontiff unveiled his encyclical on the social implications of AI. Olah gave his own remarks, saying he was “grateful to His Holiness and to the church for taking up this work of discernment”.
Behind the scenes, however, the two reportedly disagreed on whether AI could ever attain consciousness, with the pope saying that equating it with “human intelligence” was wrong.
Sam McCandlish, 36
Anthropic’s “chief architect” holds a PhD in theoretical and mathematical physics from Stanford, a university based in the heart of Silicon Valley, where he worked on quantum gravity, which is the study of how to unify Einstein’s theory of general relativity with quantum mechanics.
Before this, he worked as a researcher at OpenAI. Like Kaplan, he has published research on the field of AI scaling laws, and on other aspects of AI alignment and safety.
