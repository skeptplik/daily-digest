Journalists call for justice after reporter found dead in Mexican coastal city of Acapulco
Authorities probing death of Eduardo Flores, who reported on social issues, missing persons
Mexican journalist Eduardo Flores Salado was found dead over the weekend in Mexico's coastal city of Acapulco, intensifying concerns about reporters as the country remains among the world's deadliest for media workers.
His body was found Saturday night in the Canuto Nogueda neighbourhood, according to local authorities, in the outskirts of Acapulco, a popular resort city in southern Guerrero state.
On Sunday, the Guerrero state prosecutor's office said that the case was being investigated as an "aggravated homicide." It did not announce any arrests or provide a cause of death or motive.
Dozens of Flores's colleagues in Guerrero held a demonstration outside of the state attorney general's office on Monday, calling on Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum and local authorities to undergo a comprehensive investigation and implement stricter measures to keep journalists and their families safe.
"There is a lot of fear, of course. You have to be courageous to do journalism, to be a journalist in Guerrero," Cristian Zamora, a journalist and director at the Digital Agency, who had been friends with Flores since 2015 and was in attendance at the Monday demonstration.
"Every day, we go out and we don't know if we're going to return. Every day, we say something and we don't know who we're going to bother. We don't have that freedom."
Eduardo Flores was a well-known journalist in Acapulco. (Eduardo Flores/Facebook)
Over nearly two decades, Flores covered missing persons cases, security and social issues, working as a reporter for multiple local outlets.
Most recently, Flores worked with digital news outlet Enfoque Informativo and was well known in Acapulco for his activism within the 2SLGBTQ+ community. He also co-founded tabloid news outlet La Prensa Rosa, which translates to the Pink Press.
Mexico is consistently named one of the most dangerous and deadliest countries in the world for journalists, with Reporters Without Borders ranking the country as the second-most dangerous place for reporters last year, after Gaza.
Journalists indignant over targeted attacks in Mexico
Zamora described Flores as a "great friend, supportive, attentive and polite."
"He was murdered," he told CBC News. "All of us, his fellow journalists, it caused us indignation, anger at what is happening in the country."
Local 2SLGBTQ+ activist Alberto Serna Mogollon said Flores — whom he and other friends called by the common nicknames "Lalo" or "Lalito" — was a vocal member and supporter of the movement.
"That is why we are here: stand in solidarity, asking for justice from the competent authorities," he told CBC News.
A number of international human rights organizations — including London-based advocacy group Article 19 and Amnesty International — have documented that attacks, threats and murders of journalists frequently go unpunished in Mexico.
In a post on X on Sunday, Article 19 said it condemned the alleged murder of Flores, calling on the Guerrero state prosecutor's office to "conduct a prompt, thorough, and impartial investigation ... that considers their journalistic work as a possible motive for the murder and keeps other lines of investigation open."
Article 19 has documented 181 such cases since 2000 in the Latin American country, many of these concentrated in states with a strong presence of organized crime groups.
It also called on the Protection Mechanism for Human Rights Defenders and Journalists, a state-run program meant to help protect journalists and human rights defenders, to assess the risks facing reporters' families and colleagues and to ensure their protection.
The program declined the Associated Press' request for an interview and did not provide comment for this story.
98% of journalist homicides remain unpunished
Last Wednesday, the AP reported on "exposed shortcomings" in the 2012 program meant to safeguard to more than 2,000 individuals, including approximately 500 journalists.
Under the mechanism's framework, each case first undergoes a risk assessment by a governing board, which creates a security plan. It can then provide tailored physical and emergency measures, such as panic buttons, bodyguards, armoured vehicles and temporary relocation. The mechanism ultimately works alongside federal and state authorities to implement and monitor these protections over time.
Eight journalists were killed while enrolled in the program between 2017 and 2022, according to a joint investigation by Amnesty International and the Committee to Protect Journalists.
Propuesta Cívica, a Mexican civil society organization that provides legal support to journalists and their families, estimates that 98 per cent of journalist homicides remain unpunished.
"The deeper structural problem is that the mechanism has been given a responsibility that belongs to the state as a whole," Vanessa Carrillo, Propuesta Cívica's co-director, told the Associated Press last week.
Cameras, a video recorder, a microphone and placards are placed together on the ground during a protest in July 2023, by journalists to demand justice for the murder of a regional correspondent who was found slain two days prior in, Guadalajara, Mexico. (Ulises Ruiz/AFP/Getty Images)
The Inter-American Commission for Human Rights has emphasized that the state must prevent attacks and reduce risks, not simply investigate them.
According to Propuesta Cívica, Mexico's federal Protection Mechanism is hampered by bureaucratic delays, insufficient staffing and a reactive approach. Protection measures are often inadequate, risk assessments delayed and safeguards withdrawn despite ongoing threats.
In 2025, President Sheinbaum said the program could receive additional funding if needed, but Reporters Without Borders said the government has made little progress in fulfilling its press freedom commitments, including a promised review of the program.
Another reporter death
Flores's death follows that of Roxana Guzman, who directed a Veracruz crime news outlet, Pulso Informativo del Sureste.
A video she recorded of masked men breaking down her front door before her abduction on June 2 was widely shared on social media. Her remains were found a month later. Authorities said eight people had been arrested over Guzman's kidnapping and killing, including four municipal police officers from a small town 300 kilometres south.
Flores's employer Enfoque Informativo condemned his death and demanded a thorough and transparent investigation.
"This attack not only takes the life of a colleague, friend, and valuable human being; it constitutes a direct attack on freedom of expression," it said in a statement on Sunday.
"They will not silence us. We will continue to report with the same determination and professional ethics that characterized the work of Eduardo Flores Salado."
