Sportswear brand Nike has unveiled the 3D-printed air bags that will be used for its next generation of air-filled trainers. In this interview, the brand’s chief innovation, design & product officer Phil McCartney explains why it is a technological and performance advance.
“I see it personally as a revolution, not an evolution,” McCartney told Dezeen from the LeBron James Innovation Center in Oregon. “It’s a huge stride forward.”
Tuned Air was designed to give Nike much greater control of how air is used within the midsole to provide cushioning and support. Using a combination of proprietary 3D printing and “selective lamination” allows Nike to create midsoles that perform in different ways under different parts of the foot.
Informed by the raised vibrant colours on magazine covers, the airbag is built up from layers of ink.
“We can engineer the structure and tune the structure in the heel differently to the forefoot,” said McCartney.
“We can also tune the height, so you can have a variety of different heights, and we can tune the structure inside.”
Nike unveiled the technology and concept images for a running shoe today, and is planning for the first Nike Tuned Air trainer to be launched next year.
The trainers will be the first in what Nike sees as a third major generation of its air-filled shoes, since Nike CEO Phil Knight worked with inventor Frank Rudy to use a cushioning system filled with gas in the late 1970s.
Following the Nike Air Tailwind in 1978, Nike developed Air Max in the 1980s and Air Zoom in the 1990s.
“At its core, Air, for 50 years, has been this magical combination of lightweight, cushioned and resilient – it doesn’t break down over time,” said McCartney. “That’s why from day one when Phil [Knight] struck the deal with Frank Rudy, we’ve been so committed to it – it just delivers a really unique benefit.”
“If Max Air is about maximal cushioning and impact protection, Zoom Air is more about responsiveness and snappiness,” he continued. “Tuned Air, because of this proprietary ink and selective lamination process, allows us to literally tune the sensation within the airbag, which enables us to sort of move free from the constraints of typical geometry and shape.”
According to McCartney, this change in how the airbags are manufactured opens up possibilities, especially in customisation.
“It is the first step in exploring all the different places that this will go,” said McCartney. “The capability and what we’re able to do with this proprietary ink and the selective lamination allows us to really unlock the real potential of air.”
“It also creates this canvas for all the design explorations that we’ve always wanted to do, but the limitations of the manufacturing process didn’t allow,” he continued.
“So now the handcuffs are off in terms of it being a canvas – what expressions do we want to explore? What different visuals, geometries, and different ways to articulate the benefit can we explore? We’re looking at all of that. “
While the air-filled midsole has been manufactured, the trainer is still in development. However, McCartney expects the shoe to look close to the concept render that was revealed today.
“There’ll be a few modifications,” he said. “Knowing where the design is going to go. It’s pretty representative as an illustration, but it isn’t the final design.” He noted that the final air bag design will be close to the prototype.
“What we’re exploring now is how do we take the idea of lightweight cushioning and this idea of tuned [and find] what that looks like in an expression and an experience on the upper.”
One key design element that will feature in the commercial release next year will be the full-length, visible air bag, which McCartney said is an “important part of the design and important part of this platform”.
The midsoles are the latest innovation in Nike’s exploration of using air within footwear and apparel. While it has made millions of shoes containing air, it has also recently unveiled the inflating Air Milano Jacket at 2026 Winter Olympics and its Radical AirFlow t-shirts that counterintuitively use more material to make a wearer cooler.
In all the areas of innovation that McCartney oversees at Nike, air is where he sees the most potential.
“There’s a number of areas, but if I had to pick one, I think that [air] is the platform that intrigues us most, that drives our creativity the most, that also translates into the biggest athlete benefit. And I mean athlete in the broadest sense of the term – if you’ve got a body, you’re an athlete.”
“We’re really intrigued and fascinated by the notion of being able to harness what’s all around us to further performance,” said McCartney. “That started in footwear, obviously. This is the next revolution.”
