The UK government borrowed less than expected in October despite a rise in debt interest costs, in a boost for chancellor John Healey amid the pressure on Britain’s economy from the Middle East war.
The Office for National Statistics (ONS) said public sector net borrowing – the difference between government spending and income – was £17bn in October, about £800m higher than the same month a year ago. City economists had forecast borrowing would increase by more, to £18bn.
In the financial year so far, public sector net borrowing was £111.3bn, the ONS said – £7.3bn less than in the same period last year, helped by stronger tax receipts.
Grant Fitzner, the ONS chief economist, said: “Borrowing so far this financial year remained lower than in the same period last year with receipts growing faster than spending. Income-related taxes’ receipts grew particularly strongly.
“However, monthly borrowing was up on last October, partly down to higher debt interest.”
The latest figures showed rising borrowing costs on financial markets drove the UK’s debt interest payments to £11.2bn in October - £2.8bn more than in the same month last year, and the highest figure for any October since monthly records began in 1997.
Some government bonds are directly linked to the retail prices index measure of inflation, with payments rising in line with it. RPI has increased in recent months as the Iran war has pushed up energy costs.
Public sector net debt rose to £3.1tn, equivalent to 95.8% of GDP – 1.9 percentage points higher than last year and at levels last seen in the early 1960s.
Chief secretary to the Treasury, Emma Reynolds, said Britain was showing “standout resilience despite the global impact of the conflict in Iran.
“This year we’ve cut borrowing faster than any other G20 country. Now we’re going to cut waste and bureaucracy by reprioritising spending and making public services more efficient with AI,” Reynolds added.
Britain’s economy has continued to grow at the second-fastest rate in the G7 despite the fallout from the war in the Middle East, bolstered by AI investment and households continuing to spend.
In his budget last month, Healey raised taxes on banks to fund relief for households being squeezed by the energy shock from the Middle East war.
Against a highly uncertain global backdrop, Healey stuck to the government’s fiscal rules with £15bn of headroom – less than the £23.6bn buffer left by his predecessor, Rachel Reeves, as the conflict drove up the UK government’s borrowing costs.
After a low-key budget statement designed with febrile bond markets in mind, economists warned the chancellor had set himself up for a “brutal” spending review next year.
Avoiding a return to austerity for unprotected government departments could require a £17bn top-up to the spending plans for 2030-31, according to the Institute for Fiscal Studies. That is before taking into account an additional £11bn needed to fund a commitment to increase defence spending to 3% of GDP.
Official figures on Wednesday showed inflation in the UK rose in October to 3.6% amid the global surge in energy prices triggered by the Iran war. Economic forecasters predict a further rise in inflation, with the Bank of England anticipating a peak of close to 5% next year.
Threadneedle Street raised interest rates from 3.75% to 4% earlier this month and warned further increases might be required to combat inflationary pressures unleashed by the Iran war. Financial markets predict three quarter-point rate increases before the end of next year.
Rob Wood, the chief UK economist at the consultancy Pantheon Macroeconomics, said: “The October budget did little to address the more serious challenges facing the public finances, so we continue to have doubts about the government’s ability to follow through on its fiscal plans, suggesting upside risks to borrowing in the coming years.”
