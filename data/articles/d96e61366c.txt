Rarely does a filmmaker’s first movie also become their breakout hit. Before Jaws made Steven Spielberg a household name, he directed the little-remembered The Sugarland Express. The Coen Brothers started with the indie thriller Blood Simple, then enjoyed a handful of moderately successful films before Fargo exploded. But with Quentin Tarantino, this was not the case.
Tarantino worked as a video store clerk while writing Reservoir Dogs, which he planned to shoot independently with some friends on a meager $30,000 budget. That changed when his producer Lawrence Bender gave the script to his acting teacher, who then passed it along to actor Harvey Keitel. Keitel was impressed and agreed to help produce the film. He also agreed to star as Mr. White.
Keitel then helped Tarantino and Bender raise money for the film and hold casting sessions which got them the likes of Steve Buscemi, who played Mr. Pink; Michael Madsen, who played Mr. Blonde; and Tim Roth, who played Mr. Orange. The film was shot in Los Angeles in just 35 days. It then debuted at Sundance in January 1992, where it became a massive hit. Reservoir Dogs was released in theaters that October and grossed $22 million (an impressive haul for a film that cost about $1.5 million). It became an even bigger hit on home video.
Reservoir Dogs is about a crew of thieves pulling off a jewel heist organized by a crime boss named Joe Cabot (Lawrence Tierney). The six men in the crew don’t know one another, and each is given an alias (Mr. White, Mr. Pink, etc). The job, however, goes horribly wrong and results in a shootout with police, which also claims civilian lives. The surviving crew members take refuge at the prearranged warehouse where they were supposed to reconvene after the job. As Mr. Orange lay dying on the warehouse floor, Mr. Pink and Mr. White try to make sense of what happened. Mr. Pink is convinced there is a rat in the crew, which would explain how the police got there so quickly.
Why Reservoir Dogs is still so good
The funny thing about Reservoir Dogs is that you never see the heist itself, and that’s what’s so good about it. The movie jumps back and forth between the post-robbery setting and a series of flashbacks as the guys are hired by Joe and prepare for the job. It’s up to the viewer to put all the puzzle pieces together, and while we never get all the pieces, we get enough to assemble an entirely unpredictable narrative that includes bloody deaths that are impossible to see coming on a first viewing (and still shocking during repeat viewings).
In addition to the non-linear story structure of Reservoir Dogs, what made the movie so distinct was its characters. Keitel’s Mr. White is a surprisingly compassionate criminal, who shows his tender side with the dying Mr. Orange, played by Roth, who is equally compelling as a clearly doomed man. Steve Buscemi’s Mr. Pink is the funniest character in the film, as he whines about his assigned alias and the lack of professionalism among the other thieves. Michael Madsen’s Mr. Blonde is a downright frightening character who enjoys torturing people and is the reason why the whole heist goes wrong. Lawrence Tierney is also an effectively foreboding presence, which is offset by his onscreen son “Nice Guy Eddie” played by Chris Penn, who goes from funny to frightening and back again multiple times.
Reservoir Dogs set a high bar for all future Tarantino films, many of which had their own unique story structure and equally magnetic characters. Some would become bigger hits than Reservoir Dogs was, but Reservoir Dogs has never been overshadowed or forgotten about, no matter how impressive Tarantino’s body of work has become. The simple reason for that is that it’s just that good a film where it simply will never, ever go away.
