In 2024, AT&T lost sensitive communication logs of 109 million customers in a massive data breach. Call logs can reveal a lot about you, like your closest relationships, where you live and work, and your daily routine.
109 million people (nearly a third of Americans) suffered a massive data breach in April 2024 that exposed their AT&T communication logs.
AT&T lost sensitive information including phone numbers, call and text counts, call durations, and location data from May to October 2022, and January 2023, meaning the data was approximately two years old at the time of the breach, impacting both current and former customers.
AT&T said the records didn’t contain call content, customer names, or Social Security numbers. That’s less reassuring than it sounds, because even on their own, call logs reveal a lot about you.
What are call logs?
Every call, text, and data session generates records inside your carrier’s network. The two kinds that matter here are:
- SIP messages. Session Initiation Protocol (SIP) manages calls and texts, both within a carrier and between networks. One message starts a session and another ends it. Carriers use them mainly to troubleshoot dropped calls and failed texts.
- Call Detail Records (CDRs). CDRs summarize each call, text, and data session: who was involved, when, for how long, and often which cell site your phone used. Carriers use them for billing and for settling charges with roaming partners.
Anyone with access to your call logs can identify your closest relationships, locate your home and workplace, and reconstruct your daily routine in more detail than you could yourself. If you called an oncologist a few years ago, there’s likely a record of it, how long you talked, and where you were. If you texted an old friend afterwards, there’s a log of that too.
Call metadata can even be correlated with publicly available information to derive identities.
Malte Spitz showed how far this goes. In 2011 he sued his telecom for the data it held on him, received roughly 35,000 records, and reconstructed a strikingly specific replay of several years of his activities.
Why call logs are easy to get
Three things make them an easy target.
They’re kept for a long time. Most carriers don’t say how long. Privacy policies typically promise to retain data for “as long as necessary to fulfill the purposes” of the policy. In 2022, responding to a request from the FCC chair, carriers disclosed that they kept geolocation data for as long as five years, far longer than phone service requires. One reason is to monetize it.
They sit in a few big targets. Carrier data is frequently breached. AT&T was one of many Snowflake customers hit by credential-based thefts, alongside Ticketmaster and Neiman Marcus. Salt Typhoon, the China-linked campaign, reportedly compromised every major US carrier, which could put call metadata for any American within reach.
They have other buyers. Retained logs are used to build detailed behavioral profiles that are sold to ad networks and data brokers and, in some cases, handed to the government.
The AT&T breach is the predictable result. The records were available to steal because they existed.
