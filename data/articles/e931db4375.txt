Resident doctors have returned to the picket lines on Friday in a strike over pay.
They are holding a full 24-hour strike which began at 07:00 GMT, marking their second such action this year.
British Medical Association (BMA) members voted in favour of industrial action after the union rejected a recommended 3.5% pay rise from an independent pay body.
Health Minister Robbie Butler said he remained committed to delivering the pay award and that he would "do everything in my power" to implement it this year - but said he was unable to do so in the absence of a Stormont budget.
Butler added that the recommended 3.5% rise was higher than that for other healthcare professionals working in Northern Ireland's health system.
But the BMA argues that doctors' salaries fell significantly in real terms during years of below-inflation pay rises and its members are now seeking "pay restoration".
"Our pay is eroding and we need to address that in order to retain young doctors in Northern Ireland," said Dr Steven Montgomery, chair of the BMA's Northern Ireland Resident Doctors Committee.
He said his colleagues were facing burnout and emotional distress due to work pressure, with their pay failing to reflect the demands placed upon them.
Resident doctors - formally known as junior doctors - are fully qualified medical practitioners who are completing postgraduate clinical training in a hospital or general practice under the supervision of a senior doctor.
How will the strike affect patients?
Emergency care, urgent surgery and most GP services will continue on Monday as senior doctors will remain on duty while younger doctors are on strike.
"There still is cover and if you are unwell or perceive yourself to be unwell you can still present to a GP surgery," said Dr Alan Stout, chair of the BMA's Northern Ireland Council.
He added that hospital emergency departments will remain open, but many planned hospital outpatient appointments will be affected by the strike.
"What will be cancelled is a lot of elective care," Stout said, explaining that would include services like scheduled surgery and planned scopes.
"The trusts have known about the industrial action for quiet some time and patients will have been notified," he added.
Stout said trusts had taken the lead on informing affected patients, and anyone with an appointment should still attend unless a trust had directly advised them not to.
Stout told the BBC's Good Morning Ulster programme that doctors were "frustrated, they's fed up and they're very angry now" about their pay situation.
He rejected the idea that local politicians were powerless to resolve the matter.
"What we have at the moment is a pass the parcel of blame. The health minister can't deliver it so he blames the Executive.
"The Executive can't deliver it so they blame the Treasury."
Stout argued that the current crisis in the health service was driven by workforce issues and the inability to attract and retain medical staff.
"It's a worldwide market at the moment and we have brilliant young doctors in Northern Ireland that are simply looking elsewhere," he said.
"If we don't keep them, our future looks really, really bleak."
Further action next week
Consultants and specialist, associate specialist and speciality grade (SAS) doctors will also take 24-hour action from 07:00 GMT on Monday.
They will provide what is known as Christmas Day cover, which means that most routine and elective services will be cancelled but full emergency cover will remain in place.
SAS doctors are specialist doctors employed by health trusts.
The three branches of hospital doctors also took strike action in June.
During that period of strike action more than 2,200 appointments were cancelled across the health and social care system, according to the health minister.
Butler said the fresh action planned for Friday and Monday would have "real consequences for patients, waiting lists and service delivery", especially during the busy winter period.
In England, consultant doctors have called off their two-day strike this month to hold talks with the government.
Montgomery said doctors wanted full "pay restoration", adding that the annual pay rise they were due to receive would not make up for the value their pay had lost over many years.
He added that BMA was "more than willing" to discuss with the minister and Department of Health how pay "erosion" could be addressed and what steps were needed to reassure members that there was a commitment to resolving it.
The minister said that to go beyond the recommended 3.5% offer would lead to "significant repercussions for nurses, teachers, police officers and indeed the entire public sector workforce".
"It comes on top of an average cumulative pay uplifts over the last three years of 30% for resident doctors, 25% for speciality, associate specialist, and specialist doctors, and 24% for consultants," Butler said.
The minister said he had met the BMA and outlined the financial constraints facing the Department of Health and his intention to issue the pay rises as soon as possible.
But he said that would require adequate funding to be given to his department.
However, Stout pointed out that the BMA's pay dispute dated back to the aftermath of the financial crisis in 2008.
"In those early years we actually worked with the government whenever they pleaded austerity and there was a whole series of 0% and 1% rises, way below inflation," he said.
"If you actually total up inflation since 2008 it comes to somewhere between 65% and 73% but the real-term pay has only increased by 40-45%, so we're miles behind."
