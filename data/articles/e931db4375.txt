Doctors involved in a 24-hour strike over pay have warned more health staff will leave Northern Ireland for higher wages and better conditions elsewhere.
Seamus Crumley is among medics who joined a picket line in Belfast after their union, the British Medical Association (BMA), rejected a pay rise offer of 3.5%.
He moved to Northern Ireland from his native Scotland last year but told BBC News NI that despite securing a more senior role he "took a pay cut to come here".
Health Minister Robbie Butler said he would try to do "everything in my power" to implement this year's pay award - but said he was unable to do so at present in the absence of a Stormont budget.
Butler added that the recommended 3.5% rise was higher than that for other healthcare professionals working in Northern Ireland's health system.
But the BMA argues doctors' salaries fell significantly in real terms during years of below-inflation pay rises so it is seeking "pay restoration" dating back to 2008.
It has blamed current pay levels in the sector for the problems the health service is having in recruiting and retaining staff.
"Northern Ireland is one of, if not the, worst paid places in the UK," said Crumley, who works in intensive care in the Ulster Hospital, Dundonald.
The anaesthetics registrar is married to a local doctor and they moved from Scotland just over 15 months ago so that his wife could live close to her family.
"The medical workforce here is very, very vulnerable to losing doctors to literally anywhere else in the UK, but also to the Republic of Ireland," he said.
Burnout and emotional distress
Resident doctors - formally known as junior doctors - are fully qualified medical practitioners who are completing postgraduate clinical training in a hospital or general practice under the supervision of a senior doctor.
"Our pay is eroding and we need to address that in order to retain young doctors in Northern Ireland," said Dr Steven Montgomery, chair of the BMA's Northern Ireland Resident Doctors Committee.
He said his colleagues were facing burnout and emotional distress due to work pressure, with their pay failing to reflect the demands placed upon them.
"We've got half a million people here in Northern Ireland on waiting lists," he told the same programme.
"That has not happened because of a few days of industrial action. That's happened because of 15 years of not investing in the health service - we need to reverse this trend."
How will the strike affect patients?
The strike began at 07:00 GMT on Friday and will affect non-emergency services, resulting in the cancellation of many planned outpatient appointments.
Urgent surgery and most GP services will continue as senior doctors will remain on duty while younger resident doctors are on strike.
"There still is cover and if you are unwell or perceive yourself to be unwell you can still present to a GP surgery," said Dr Alan Stout, chair of the BMA's Northern Ireland Council.
He added that hospital emergency departments will remain open, but many planned hospital outpatient appointments will be affected by the strike.
"What will be cancelled is a lot of elective care," Stout said, explaining that would include services like scheduled surgery and planned scopes.
"The trusts have known about the industrial action for quiet some time and patients will have been notified," he added.
Stout said trusts had taken the lead on informing affected patients, and anyone with an appointment should still attend unless a trust had directly advised them not to.
Passing the 'parcel of blame'
Stout also told the programme that doctors were "frustrated, they're fed up and they're very angry" about their pay.
He rejected the idea that politicians were powerless to resolve the matter.
"What we have at the moment is a pass the parcel of blame. The health minister can't deliver it so he blames the Executive.
"The executive can't deliver it so they blame the Treasury."
Stout argued the current health service crisis was driven by the inability to attract and retain medical staff.
"It's a worldwide market at the moment and we have brilliant young doctors in Northern Ireland that are simply looking elsewhere," he said.
"If we don't keep them, our future looks really, really bleak."
OisÃn Fearon, a resident doctor in Belfast, said many of his colleagues have already "emigrated to as far away as Australia and even America; New Zealand and it is definitely something that has crossed my mind".
"When people are offered better pay and conditions further afield, or even just a short skip over the border, then it's very understandable why people do take those opportunities," Fearon told the BBC's Good Morning Ulster programme.
Personally, he considered moving to Australia for better career prospects but said "ultimately family and local connections" kept him closer to home for now.
However, he recently began the process of registering to train in the Republic of Ireland and is keeping that option open.
Further action next week
Following Friday' strike, senior ranks including consultants and specialist, associate specialist and speciality grade (SAS) doctors will also take 24-hour action from 07:00 GMT on Monday.
They will provide what is known as Christmas Day cover, which means most routine and elective services will be cancelled but full emergency cover will remain in place.
The three branches of hospital doctors also took strike action in June which resulted in the cancellation of more than 2,200 appointments at that time.
Butler said Friday and Monday's action would have "real consequences for patients, waiting lists and service delivery", especially during the busy winter period.
In England, consultant doctors have called off their two-day strike this month to hold talks with the government.
The minister said that to go beyond the recommended 3.5% offer would lead to "significant repercussions for nurses, teachers, police officers and indeed the entire public sector workforce".
"It comes on top of an average cumulative pay uplifts over the last three years of 30% for resident doctors, 25% for speciality, associate specialist, and specialist doctors, and 24% for consultants."
Butler added he had met the BMA and outlined the financial constraints facing the Department of Health and his intention to issue the pay rises as soon as possible.
But he said that would require adequate funding to be given to his department.
'We're miles behind'
However, BMA representatives pointed out that their pay dispute dated back to the aftermath of the financial crisis in 2008.
"In those early years we actually worked with the government whenever they pleaded austerity and there was a whole series of 0% and 1% rises, way below inflation," Stout said.
"If you actually total up inflation since 2008 it comes to somewhere between 65% and 73% but the real-term pay has only increased by 40-45%, so we're miles behind."
Montgomery said doctors understood that Butler was under "financial constraints" and so the BMA was "not looking for this all to be paid in one lump sum".
Instead, he said they were looking "for a plan from the minister to make our way back to those levels and we understand that's a process that's going to take time".
