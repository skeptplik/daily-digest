As pollution mounts in its rivers, food and water, Thailand is pressing neighbouring Myanmar to tackle unregulated mining, while urging China to exert its influence in the war-ravaged nation.
Tap water supplies to 14 villages in Mae Sai district near the Myanmar border were suspended earlier this month after arsenic levels were found to be nearly 30 times government limits.
Bangkok this week hosted the first Thai-Myanmar working group on the issue — also attended by the Chinese Ambassador — where it said tests proved rare-earth and gold mines in Myanmar were causing the pollution downstream. The two sides agreed to joint water sampling on the Kok and Sai rivers.
But there was no public agreement to Thailand's request for a six-month suspension of operations, and how one could be implemented remains unclear, with Myanmar's military not in full control. Bangkok is also looking to China, the main importer of rare-earth materials from Myanmar, to pressure private Chinese operators, as a months-long concern becomes increasingly urgent.
Authorities warned residents this week against eating certain shellfish and other river species after tests in northern and northeastern Thailand found dangerous levels of arsenic and lead.
Saengwaree Suvirakan, a farmer from Tha Ton in Chiang Mai province who joined a protest in Bangkok this week, said fish stocks were declining in any case. "Now with the mines the fish are all gone," she said.
“Not from nature"
At the joint talks, Thai Ministers said high arsenic levels in the Kok and Sai rivers could only have come from rare-earth and gold mines on the Myanmar side.
"Arsenic does not come from nature, it came from mining sites. The source was from (Myanmar's) Shan state, no-one disagrees," Science Minister Yodchanan Wongsawat said. But how to tackle it remains an issue.
Thanapon Piman, senior research fellow at the Stockholm Environment Institute (SEI), welcomed the agreement on joint sampling sites. "This is going to be a first step to agree on the evidence, otherwise you cannot move to the next step, like stopping mining activity," he told AFP. But Myanmar intervening to stop mining would be "really difficult", he added.
Multiple ethnic armed groups have been operating there for decades, while a 2021 military coup sparked an ongoing civil war. Thai officials pinpointed two gold mines just over the border from Mae Sai as the main source of high arsenic levels in the Sai River.
Different groups control different parts of the wider area, among them Myanmar military and the United Wa State Army, an ethnic armed group with close ties to China.
Mining suspension
Many mining operations in Myanmar are run by Chinese companies, and Thai Prime Minister Anutin Charnvirakul raised pollution with President Xi Jinping this month. "China acknowledged that they are aware of mining by private Chinese companies in Shan state," Foreign Minister Sihasak Phuangketkeow said. "China made clear that they do not support activities that are improper, illegal or cause harm."
“Thailand sought China's backing for its six-month mining suspension,” officials said. But China's Foreign Ministry declined to comment, with a spokeswoman saying only that Beijing "pays close attention" to the pollution issue and would provide technical support.
The SEI's Thanapon said Thailand could help "slow down mining activity" by pressuring the supply chain on its side of the border.
Thailand is planning to ban exports to Myanmar of two chemicals used in rare-earth mining, and restrict imports from the country of six minerals including gold and rare earths, to limit unauthorised mines' access to buyers. For farmer Saengwaree, there is no time to waste.
“Even traditional customs were being hit by the pollution,” she said, noting this week's Loy Krathong festival, where residents and tourists send out small floats into waterways. "Usually it is very busy," she said, "but right now there is nobody in Tha Ton, there are no tourists."
