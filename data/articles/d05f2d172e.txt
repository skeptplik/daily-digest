Here's the public health guidance for possible lead exposure in Iowa
NPR's Juana Summers talks with Dr. Dan McCabe, medical director of the Iowa Poison Control Center, about how people can protect themselves after the Army detonates degraded lead azide.
JUANA SUMMERS, HOST:
The Iowa Poison Control Center has been providing public health guidance for residents who may be affected by the dust cloud that will follow that explosion. Dr. Dan McCabe is the medical director there, and he joins us now. Welcome to the program.
DAN MCCABE: Thank you very much for having me on.
SUMMERS: Dr. McCabe, am I right that you actually grew up near Middletown, where the detonation will happen?
MCCABE: Yeah, that's correct. My hometown is Burlington, Iowa, so right down the street.
SUMMERS: What's it like there, if you can tell us?
MCCABE: It's a very typical Iowa smaller town, very hardworking people, pretty blue collar. And most people there just want to do their jobs and take care of their families and just kind of have an overall good life.
SUMMERS: Yeah, I'm a fellow Midwesterner. I spent a lot of time in Iowa growing up. I have to imagine that you're hearing from people that you know in that area, loved ones, friends, family. What are you hearing from them? What are their concerns as we head towards this planned detonation?
MCCABE: Most of what I'm hearing is a sense of unease, some fear, but mostly just around what's going to happen next and what information do we have, how to keep themselves safe.
SUMMERS: Yeah. From a public health perspective, what is your top concern about the detonation of all of that lead there in Iowa?
MCCABE: I have to be clear that I don't have any information that's not out for public domain.
SUMMERS: Sure.
MCCABE: From what we're being told by our federal colleagues, they've done all the mitigation efforts that they possibly can, so we're truly hopeful that there'll be a lot less lead going into the environment than what we were originally concerned about. My biggest concern is actually the lead in the environment long term. I get very concerned about some individuals that are higher risk - children, always our biggest priority, and then pregnant patients and then patients that are overall malnourished. And we really get concerned about children because children put things - their hands in their mouth, and they put things...
SUMMERS: They touch everything.
MCCABE: Everything all the time. And so that's why even a little bit in our environment, we get very concerned about that because a child really needs to have their parents or their caregivers be extra diligent in helping them with that.
SUMMERS: You know, I think sometimes when people hear about potential lead exposure, one of the first things that comes to my mind, at least, is, is there a safe level of lead exposure?
MCCABE: The clear answer is there is no safe level for lead in our body. We have no use for lead. And once lead is in our body, it stays there for quite a while, if not forever. And again, we always get concerned about children because children have a developing brain, and lead can limit that development or can affect that development in ways that can be relatively vague but can go on to reduce their ability to reach all their goals, their highest levels in this life. There's also a lot of concerns for adults, but they tend to be less of our development from our brain and they're more related to other health effects.
SUMMERS: Dr. McCabe, you've said that brief exposure is not likely to cause a medical emergency, but there is concern about the impact of long-term exposure. Do you have any sense of how likely it is that residents might experience that kind of exposure that might be more worrying?
MCCABE: That is really unclear at this moment. Based on the modeling that the Army or EPA has provided us, we are hopeful that the environmental impact will be relatively minimal, and they have told us they are going to do a lot of sampling in the environment to actually help us determine what areas are affected and what areas individuals might need to have lead testing. The sampling after the fact will actually allow providers to know the patients that they're seeing are - were not at risk at all. We can do a lot of reassurance, hopefully, at that time. But time will tell with the surveillance and the monitoring after the fact.
SUMMERS: Dr. McCabe, just briefly, what's your biggest message for people in Middletown and the surrounding area who might be hearing our conversation and who are a little uneasy or uncertain about what might come next?
MCCABE: There are simple ways to try to reduce your individual exposure to lead in this situation. Close your windows. Turn off your HVAC units, or at least change them to recirculating until we get a all-clear from the U.S. Army. After the fact, while we're waiting for the monitoring, the surveillance data to be provided to us, would be very vigilant about washing your hands, washing your children's hands, wiping off any outside playground equipment that they might be playing on. Leave your shoes outside. If your car was outside during detonation, to rinse it off before pulling into the garage. Those simple measures should hopefully minimize at least the short-term risk for an individual in the coming weeks.
SUMMERS: Dr. Dan McCabe is medical director at the Iowa Poison Control Center. Thank you so much.
MCCABE: Thank you so much for having me on.
