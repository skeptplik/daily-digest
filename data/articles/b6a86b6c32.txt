Every morning for a month, Mildred Juarez woke up at five to be the first person in the showers at the immigration centre in Texas where she was being detained. Her friends had paid her bail, and she wanted to look presentable when she was finally released.
But every day, she was told her bond had not been paid.
Juarez was confused. Her friends had wired $2,600 (Â£1,970) to a bond company, Libre Immigration Services (LIS), that had been recommended by her lawyer. LIS said it would begin "the process of paying her immigration bond". But the next day came, and the day after that, with no release.
What she didn't know was that LIS was owned by a convicted fraudster, who had acquired the assets of another bail company co-founded by two men who also had fraud convictions. These two men were fined $811m in 2024 for scamming their clients.
Their firm, Libre By Nexus, which was at one point the largest immigration bail company in America, had appeared to shut down after federal regulators caught up with them.
But public records and BBC interviews with clients and former employees reveal a tangle of interconnected operations continued - along with the flow of cash - while leaving immigrants stuck in detention centres.
Since President Donald Trump has returned to office after promising an immigration crackdown, Immigrations and Customs Enforcement (ICE) has made over 1.2m arrests, and fast-tracked deportations. The administration has tried unsuccessfully to make it illegal to give migrants bail but the sharp increase in detentions has increased demand for bond companies to secure bail and their release.
Conditions in these centres can be poor. Held in custody, away from family, often scared to speak out, many have faced health problems and endured inadequate food or treatment, according to immigrant advocates and reports by the US government.
In the detention centre where Juarez was locked up, she and about 100 other detainees slept in bunks laid out in rows in a cacophonous room. The lights were left on all night and guards routinely woke everyone up for roll call. According to Castro and advocacy groups, detainees who caused trouble were put in "the hold" - solitary confinement.
The White House and officials from Homeland Security did not respond to the BBC's request for comment.
For Juarez, the wait to be bailed out of detention seemed interminable.
In an email to the BBC, a spokesperson for LIS said Juarez "misunderstands" their services - that they are not bondsmen themselves, but they connect detained individuals to those who are licensed bondsmen. They said another company ultimately decided not to work with her.
An email sent by LIS, and reviewed by the BBC, said that the company had paid a cash bond to ICE on behalf of Juarez. The message was forwarded to Juarez as proof of payment a few days before her release.
But it was another bail agent, who was not affiliated with LIS, that got her out of detention - after her friends raised even more money to hire another company.
"I was losing it inside there," Juarez recalled, using an expletive.
"I lost everything. When I was released I had nightmares I was back inside."
Libre Immigration Services never paid back her deposit, despite sending messages promising a refund. A spokesperson told the BBC that they would refund Juarez's bail amount once her friend verified their details.
Ankle monitors and debt
It is notoriously difficult for migrants like Juarez to make bail, which can be upwards of $7,000.
Once charged with illegal entry, a judge may grant bail, which allows the migrant to live freely while their case is heard, a process which can take years. Once a person's court case is completed, the bond amount is returned.
Bail agents, who are licensed to provide a down payment on bail and vouch that if the accused does not show up for court they will pay the rest, can help people get out of detention sooner. But many won't help migrants because they view them as too much of a flight risk. A 2024 government report found that a third of migrants not already in custody did not show up for court.
Libre by Nexus, also known as Nexus, was willing to take that risk.
Founded in 2013, its co-founders Michael Donovan and Richard Moore had served prison time in the 1990s and early 2000s for a string of fraud and forgery convictions.
Through his attorney, Donovan declined to comment. Moore did not respond to the BBC's request for comment.
Although people with felony convictions are barred from holding a bond licence in many states, Nexus operated as a kind of middleman between immigrant clients and licensed bondsmen.
Holed up in detention in Louisiana after crossing the border, Rosa Murillo heard about Libre by Nexus from another inmate, and the 18-year-old Honduran's mother called them to arrange her $7,000 bail.
A Nexus employee picked her up and took her to a hotel room where she was told she would have to pay $420 a month and wear a GPS ankle monitor until her case was completed.
Federal regulators accused Nexus of implying, in their contracts, that the GPS bracelets were court-mandated, when they were actually the private company's requirement.
Murillo got a job as a waitress and tried to hide the ankle bracelet for fear that people might think she was a violent criminal.
Over the course of a decade, Libre by Nexus enabled more than 20,000 migrants like Murillo to be released on bail - and took in more than $230m in revenue, court documents show.
But the ankle bracelets were a constant source of annoyance for Nexus clients released from detention, according to two former employees who spoke to the BBC.
A former Nexus co-owner, Evan Ajin, told the BBC that the company stopped requiring released detainees to wear ankle bracelets in 2018.
But Mark Supetran, who was based in the Philippines and worked for the company for a few months in 2022, says the bracelets were still in use when he worked for the company.
Supetran said he would field 80 calls a day from people who were either going through the process themselves or were the loved one of someone who was. Telling them they would have to wear a tag on release was part of the conversation.
"Part of the script was to say something like: 'The client will be wearing an ankle monitor.' So that got me thinking, like, why?" he recalled. "It doesn't make sense, we're not the government."
Not getting refunds of bail deposits was another complaint. Another former employee told the BBC he was instructed to tell callers that they would get their money back once their case was completed.
"But that that was never the case," he told the BBC. "I mean, you had people calling me in almost every day like 'Hey, am I gonna get my money back? Am I gonna get my money back?'"
Ajin, the former Nexus co-owner, told the BBC that the contract clearly stated that monthly fees did not go towards their bond, but were programme fees, and so they could issue no refund.
The US Consumer Financial Protection Bureau (CFPB), an agency created to stop predatory lending and other abusive financial practices, successfully sued Libre by Nexus and its parent company.
The lawsuit accused the firm of running a "predatory immigrant-services scam" that trapped victims into paying high, long-term fees. Nexus also misrepresented and concealed the terms, the lawsuit alleged, such as the non-refundable, monthly payments.
The judgment sided with the CFPB and a federal judge found the company liable for using predatory tactics against its clients in 2024, fining Nexus, its parent company, Ajin, Donovan and Moore $811m.
In addition to the hefty fine, the judge forbade the company from collecting on past debts.
Days after the 2024 order, Donovan sold a number of the company's assets, including customer lists and records - for $3.50.
As part of the agreement the buyer would also take on its debt.
Ajin says he ceased contact with everyone involved following the sale.
Moore is now in prison, after being convicted of filing fraudulent Nexus taxes. He and Donovan, who is also behind bars, are awaiting sentencing for insurance fraud and conspiracy. Donovan has also been charged with sexually abusing a boy under 12, but has not entered a plea in that case.
Business as usual
The purchaser of Nexus's assets was a Pennsylvania businessman named Vincent Smith, who had been sentenced to 10 months' probation in 2015 for conspiring with employees to write over $2m in fake bail bonds.
Smith called the new business Libre Immigration Services.
This March, the judge who issued the fine also ruled the sale of the company had violated her order by transferring the debts.
Judge Elizabeth Dillon voided the sale, but did not shut Libre Immigration Services down. She is due to review claims that LIS also violated the order by trying to build an app to collect debts from Nexus's old clients.
A former paralegal at Nexus, who went on to work for LIS, told the BBC about the app. She said after the sale, the lines between the two companies were blurred.
Another employee confirmed her account independently to the BBC, noting that the app was never finished because they could not obtain the client data.
A hearing date has not been set, and LIS has denied any wrongdoing, noting the company was never in possession of the client lists.
Libre Immigration Services continues to solicit immigrant clients and advertises its services online and on social media.
Its website features glossy photos of beaming families alongside the promise to "get your loved one released from a detention centre today!".
That wasn't Janie Aviles's experience. She had wired Libre Immigration Services $10,000 to help get her friend out of detention in Texas. But LIS did not pay his bail, Aviles said, until a month later, leaving him, like Juarez, in limbo.
When she called they offered various explanations and then stopped answering the telephone, she said.
Eventually she drove to the office listed on the website, which had shown a picture of LIS's logo hanging above the door. But on arrival, she found there was no storefront with the business's name and logo anywhere.
She said the company finally paid the bail a month after getting the funds, after she had reported them to the district attorney. No charges have been laid.
An LIS spokesperson said that "verification of significant information had to occur to prevent or minimise loss".
"The application was ultimately approved, and the cash bond was pledged to ICE. Presumably any delay related to the substantial bond amount and the heightened requirements of review."
They also said the office at that location was for business operations, and not client services, and that a sign had been ordered and would be installed, pending permits.
But the ordeal was enough to anger Aviles, who feared for her friend while he was stuck in detention. "I'm like 'This ain't right.'"
