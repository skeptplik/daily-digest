Nendo has selected Linde Freya Tangelder for Dezeen’s 20×20 project in partnership with Kohler. In this interview, the Dutch designer explains why she doesn’t find design inspiring.
Insofar as she creates furniture and lighting, Tangelder is technically a designer, but only just.
“How I think is not typical design,” she told Dezeen. “So I’m never thinking about function in the first place. The whole process is very intuitive, it’s just a cloud of connections in my head – more about concepts or stories that I want to tell rather than making a functional piece.”
“I’ve never been so inspired by design in general,” she added. “It’s a skill that fits very well to me, but I’d rather connect it in inspiration to art, to architecture, or another discipline.”
With a highly sculptural style, Tangelder also sometimes refers to herself as an artist – a label she says she wouldn’t have dared use when starting out.
She has made a name for herself through pieces that often play with unexpected juxtapositions of materials and use traditional methods of production to create an unmistakably contemporary aesthetic.
“I like to use poor materials, rich materials, and fuse them,” she explained. “For me, it’s sort of a synonym for how I think culture should be.”
In 2019, she was identified by Dezeen as a young designer to watch after exhibiting at Collectible, as well as winning that year’s Biennale Interieur designer of the year award.
Explaining the studio’s decision to select her as a 20×20 nomination, Nendo praised Tangelder’s approach for being “both remarkably gentle and quietly bold”.
“Driven by a playful curiosity to take architecture apart and put it back together, she reveals qualities in familiar materials that we have long overlooked,” the studio told Dezeen.
Referencing buildings in the medium of furniture has become something of a calling card for Tangelder.
Notable examples include the Bolder Chair, which recalls architectural columns, the Windows of Bo Bardi table, which mimics the shape of windows on the SESC Pompéia Factory in São Paulo, and Brick’s Reflection Chair with its terracotta backrest (pictured left).
These propensities are reflected evocatively in the name of Tangelder’s studio – Destroyers/Builders – though recently, she tends to work more under her own name.
“I’m inspired by architecture, things I see in buildings,” she said. “I think it’s just really interesting to scale it down to the field of artistic design, or limited-edition design.”
“In the history of architecture there’s so much to tell,” she continued. “For me it’s more diverse and also less superficial than the history of design, and I think that’s what interests me so much.”
“And also that it feels more essential, I think that is maybe the core. I just like brick. I like simple materials that are used for construction rather than smooth finishes just for decoration.”
Not that she is completely opposed to smooth, decorative finishes – as evidenced by the fact that an entire room in the home-slash-studio that she shares with her landscape-architect partner is used exclusively for lacquering.
In fact, most of the house, which is in a leafy area outside Brussels, is taken up by Tangelder’s atelier space.
She moved to Belgium shortly after graduating from Design Academy Eindhoven in 2013, and has become part of an increasingly rich design scene in the country over the past decade.
In the first years it was quite a struggle to start from out of nothing. Just to survive and make it happen, and to earn a project back – there’s so much investment in each project
“I moved to Belgium because I really like the culture, the art scene, architecture as well,” said Tangelder. “The design scene I thought was actually quite minimal, or I didn’t really see a lot of studios at that time. But I liked that as well, it felt a little bit more new for me.”
“I think it developed a lot, the landscape in design in Belgium over the last 10 years,” she considered. “There’s a lot happening right now.”
It has certainly been a productive decade for Tangelder, whose back-catalogue is impressively extensive considering she has only ever employed a very small number of assistants.
Part of that has been made possible thanks to collaborations with the healthy network of craftspeople across Belgium that Tangelder has established to help with production.
“That has been super important for me,” she said. “Each material that I really love, I have a partner now.”
But moreover, the chunky portfolio is a reflection of a huge amount of hours spent working in the studio. Though she says her work-life balance is improving, Tangelder works almost every day including weekends, and has been known to go years without a holiday.
“I really like it, so for me it’s the best way to spend my time,” she said, before pausing.
“I should think about it more as fun time, because of course it’s also a struggle. You’re always struggling over all the decisions that have to be made.”
So that means you take risks all the time. It’s maybe more intense, but also more fun
Like many independent designers, Tangelder has had to battle to earn a living from her discipline.
“In the first years it was quite a struggle to start from out of nothing,” she remembered. “Just to survive and make it happen, and to earn a project back – there’s so much investment in each project.”
Over time, she says she has acquired a reasonably good sense of which pieces are likely to prove more lucrative, though she has resisted the temptation to focus her energies entirely on these.
“I think when you decide to only continue projects that you know will have commercial success, then it will be less experimental,” she explained.
“So that means you take risks all the time,” she added. “It’s maybe more intense, but also more fun.”
Most of Tangelder’s work involves one-off or limited-edition pieces for galleries, but she has also partnered with brands.
A big moment came in 2022 when she was commissioned by Cassina to produce a collection of side tables and pouffes, and she has continued to work with the Italian brand each year since.
This year she was able to put on a solo exhibition at Milan design week supported by Cassina, showcasing a combination of her independent pieces and products created for the brand.

It was an unusual example of small-scale making and larger-scale production design being displayed side-by-side.
Far from being a barrier to creative expression, Tangelder has found that these commissions can be an enabler by providing up-front revenues, unlike solo work.
“So it gives less pressure, I would say, which is very good for the creative process,” she said.
But you can have too much of a good thing, and while she enjoys the more collaborative experience of working for a brand she limits this type of work to two projects per year to ensure she has plenty of time for her own pieces.
Tangelder’s current obsession is cast and oxidised metal, so she reports spending a lot of time these days in a foundry in Antwerp. Her most recent piece is a grungy take on a conventional desk lamp, in white bronze.
“I just think the material is very me,” she remarked. “That desk light tells a lot about how I like to see objects. It’s really in between a sculpture and a functional object.”
Looking to the future, she is interested in working more with fashion, having previously designed a chair for Dior.
And, she would like to work on a larger-scale installation – perhaps an outdoor sculpture, she suggests.
In a world where for various reasons the boundary between design and art is increasingly blurred, Tangelder is undoubtedly at the forefront.
She is an interesting 20/20 choice from Sato, who is typically associated with minimalist, highly functional product design.
For her part, Tangelder sees her work as partly an indirect response to the Sato approach – a continuation of a conversation.
“I have, of course, quite a different form language, but I think it’s also a reaction to what he’s done,” she said.
“He’s so clear in the decisions that he makes, he’s a master in it, and I’m really grateful that he sees what I’ve been doing.”
