Sheikh Hasina has repeatedly vowed to return to Bangladesh.
Now the ousted prime minister has put a date on her homecoming - 16 December - and even outlined how she intends to make the journey.
The government in Dhaka, which had previously dismissed the prospect, is taking her latest declaration more seriously.
Hasina, 79, who fled to Delhi in August 2024 after a student-led uprising ended her 15-year rule, plans to go back on Victory Day, the anniversary of Bangladesh's triumph over Pakistan in the 1971 liberation war, her spokesperson said.
She was sentenced to death after being convicted of crimes against humanity for her role in the crackdown, in a trial held in her absence that raised questions about due process. Her Awami League party is banned in Bangladesh. The UN says as many as 1,400 people were killed by security forces during the uprising.
The government says she would be arrested immediately if she returned and has promised to carry out the death sentence.
Hasina now wants the Bangladesh government to arrange a flight from Delhi. If that fails, she could travel commercially or cross the land border.
"She is even prepared to walk into Bangladesh," Abu Obaidha Arin, a key aide to Hasina, told the BBC.
India and Bangladesh share a 4,096km (2,545 mile) border across five Indian states - West Bengal, Assam, Meghalaya, Tripura and Mizoram. Her supporters, Arin said, had identified nine possible crossing points. "This is a carefully thought-out, precise plan. This is serious."
Dhaka has responded with defiance, insisting Hasina must be extradited.
"Reality and desire are different things," Humaiun Kobir, Bangladesh's state minister for foreign affairs, told reporters in Dhaka. "[The reality is] she's not a regular commercial passenger. She can only be brought back in handcuffs."
India's foreign ministry said on Wednesday that Hasina's extradition request was being examined through the "established process", adding that a voluntary return would be a separate matter.
Many in Dhaka believe Hasina's declaration is more than a promise of homecoming.
They believe it is an attempt to test the government's authority, force her way back into the political conversation and exploit divisions among the parties that united to oust her. It also threatens to turn India's unresolved problem of sheltering a former ally into a more immediate diplomatic crisis.
The date is significant. Hasina is invoking Victory Day to reclaim her place in Bangladesh's founding narrative. Aides say she has spent months trying to keep her party alive in exile.
Arin says Hasina has been in regular contact with party leaders and workers through interactive audio conversations on Telegram, keeping alive a network that might otherwise have been difficult to sustain from exile.
Over the past 18 months, he says, she has held more than 300 such meetings, reaching supporters across all 64 districts and the country's 300 constituencies. They are organised through the Awami League's 78 units and "can last four or five hours".
The meetings allow Hasina to listen directly to party workers, hear their grievances and understand what is happening on the ground, Arin says. He insists the response has been substantial and claims the party "could mobilise five million people to welcome her along the route from the border to Dhaka, forming a human chain".
"We have the organisational capacity to make that happen," he says.
That may be an ambitious claim. The Awami League remains a mass party with millions of supporters, but it is banned and largely shut out of mainstream politics. Much of its leadership is abroad, in jail or underground. Police have arrested supporters and filed thousands of cases against party workers.
Yet its supporters have continued to take to the streets, staging flash protests that have grown more frequent and confrontational in recent months.
Several districts have reported bomb attacks, arson and vandalism, though responsibility for some incidents remains disputed.
Gopalganj, Hasina's ancestral district, has proved particularly difficult for the authorities to control.
Awami League supporters have attacked police stations, external, torched vehicles and targeted homes and offices belonging to ruling Bangladesh Nationalist Party (BNP) leaders, reports say. Highways have been blocked, external with felled trees and burning tyres, bringing traffic to a standstill. In some areas, police have withdrawn, external amid fears of further attacks.
Supporters have staged 40 demonstrations and more than 140 riots up to 23 November, according to data from conflict monitoring group Acled (Armed Conflict Location and Event Data Project). At least 11 people, including two civilians, have been killed in political violence this month, six directly linked to the unrest, the group says.
The party's ability to cause disruption, however, is not necessarily evidence of a broad resurgence.
Many in Dhaka remain sceptical that Hasina will actually return, viewing the announcement as a means of galvanising a demoralised party.
Yet the Awami League has achieved something politically: it is back in the conversation. Immediately after Hasina's fall, even mentioning the party was taboo. Now its future is being debated across the political spectrum.
Shafiqul Alam, editor of the English-language daily Daily Waadaa and former press secretary to the Muhammad Yunus-led interim government, believes the party is "exploiting cracks in the unity forged during the uprising".
He told the BBC that "disagreements" over the July Charter, external, proposed constitutional changes and the referendum have "widened divisions" between the elected BNP government and rivals Jamaat-e-Islami and the youth-led National Citizen Party (NCP).
"The Awami League sees an opening," Alam told the BBC. "The party believes this is an opportune moment to take to the streets, exploiting a weakened police force and divisions among the forces of the July uprising."
Analysts say if Hasina returns it could just as easily deepen hostility as revive her party's fortunes. The government has framed the sporadic recent violence as terrorism, while supporters insist their demonstrations are peaceful and accuse the authorities of repression.
The larger question is whether Bangladesh can sustain the exclusion of a political constituency as substantial as the Awami League without creating another source of instability.
For India, the dilemma is increasingly difficult to manage.
Delhi has sheltered Hasina, but her public remarks have angered Dhaka, which wants her extradited. India has sought to repair ties through diplomatic outreach and talks on trade, border security and other bilateral issues, but relations remain strained.
The chill is striking: nearly 10 months into his tenure, Prime Minister Tarique Rahman has yet to visit India.
A trip was reportedly in the offing in August, but Hasina's press conference in Delhi proved a setback to efforts to reset relations.
Avinash Paliwal, who teaches politics and international studies at SOAS University of London, argues that Hasina's latest announcement has "shifted the burden of choice to Dhaka rather than Delhi".
Delhi, he says, "feels it has tried its best" to work with Rahman's government over the past 10 months - "from appointing a political heavyweight as high commissioner, external with cabinet rank to offering fresh talks on security and trade".
"The failure of this outreach has left India disillusioned with Dhaka. India seems to have decided that bilateral relations will likely remain tense whatever it does, so there is little reason to stop Hasina from stirring the political pot."
Delhi has the leverage to restrain Hasina from making political statements on Indian soil, Paliwal argues, but may not want to exercise it.
Nor, in his view, is it in India's interest to see the Awami League permanently excluded from Bangladeshi politics.
"The political party still enjoys pockets of support across Bangladesh and is weaved into the social and political fabric of the country," he says. "So, yes, it is in India's interest, and also in Bangladesh's long-term interests, to rehabilitate the Awami League and take the path of political reconciliation."
That view is increasingly contested in Dhaka.
"There is a perception in Bangladesh that India wants to rehabilitate the Awami League. There are issues India has not resolved, including the extradition of Hasina. India has also not renewed the transit facilities that could benefit Bangladeshi garment exporters, while the Ganges water treaty, external is nearing expiry without much progress. But has India done enough to win back trust?" says Alam.
"For now, only tourist and medical visas have resumed. Everything else remains in limbo."
A Dhaka-based foreign-policy expert, who asked not to be named, says the two countries remain locked in "mutual frustration".
Hasina, he says, remains central to the impasse. "She remains the elephant in the room, but we also have to find a way to move beyond Hasina."
Paliwal argues that the least damaging outcome for India would be Hasina returning to face a fresh trial, with the Awami League rehabilitated under a different operational leadership.
"It will reintroduce a degree of balance in Bangladeshi politics, and will afford India space to protect its long-term interests and equities better," he says.
For now, 16 December is not yet a confirmed journey. But Hasina has already succeeded in making her return a question that neither Dhaka nor Delhi can ignore.
"December could be chaotic. Everyone in Dhaka is on edge," says the foreign-policy expert, warning of protests and confrontation as Hasina's proposed return date approaches - and the possibility that it could be postponed yet again.
"Hasina is unlikely to return, as she would face a death sentence. But her comments have clearly galvanized her supporters," says Pearl Pandya, Acled's South Asia analyst.
But the impasse is also complicating India's ties with Bangladesh. As Paliwal puts it, the "next window of serious negotiations will now open only after Hasina returns to Dhaka, or Dhaka relents under pressure and returns to the table".
