Vanity Fair's Taylor Swift exposé sparks backlash
Critics have been taking aim at profile's claims, sources and tone
Vanity Fair's The Mastermind profile offers a less-than-flattering account of Taylor Swift's relationships and public image — though it does not name the singer directly.
The piece, which Canadian journalist May Jeong says drew from interviews with more than 100 people, rests some of its claims on anonymous accounts and memories of events from decades ago. And critics have questioned what the article establishes — and what it asks readers to infer.
CBC News has contacted Jeong and Swift's representatives but did not hear back by publication time.
Claims about how Swift treats people
An anonymous music industry source identified as "The Exec" compares Swift to Regina George, the popular antagonist in 2004's Mean Girls. Another source known as "The Person Familiar" alleges that Swift "comes after you" if you disagree with her.
Toronto culture writer Stacy Lee Kong said "an anonymous source is only anonymous to the public." The journalist, fact-checker and editors would know the source’s identity, she said, as would the lawyer. While that may reassure readers that the source was checked, Lee Kong said, it doesn't prove the account is true.

The profile says people close to Swift "do not dare speak ill of her — dare not speak of her at all." It cites a lawyer who chose to speak through an intermediary, a manager who asked the reporter to delete their correspondence and a pastor who said they needed the family’s permission to comment.
Lee Kong noted that people may refuse to comment or request anonymity for many reasons, including privacy or concern about professional consequences.
Disputed memories of school
Swift has spoken publicly about feeling excluded and bullied as a child. In the profile, former classmates and a teacher challenge parts of her recollections. Former classmate Chanice Skinner describes Swift as part of "the popular crowd," while music teacher Sharon Luyben disputes a story about friends moving away from Swift at lunch.
Some readers questioned the weight given to memories from decades ago. One X user wrote, "If you're coming for a 37 year old because of a bad experience you had with her in 7th grade you gotta let it go and move on."
One former classmate's recollection that Swift owned Ugg boots in three colours also became a focus of online jokes. The classmate said her single mother could not afford a pair. Critics questioned what the anecdote added to a profile making broader claims about Swift's character.
Another X user wrote, "She had three pairs of Ugg boots UGH SHES THE WORST!!! This is pathetic. 💀💀💀💀💀"
Lee Kong acknowledged that readers may want a story that feels "a bit more fair" and said she understood the criticism of the profile's emphasis on details such as the boots. At the same time, she argued that a profile of a powerful celebrity does not need to be a complete accounting of every positive and negative aspect of that person.
Feuds, nicknames and old disputes
Charli xcx, left, and Taylor Swift pose at an event at Madison Square Garden in December 2014 in New York City. (Dimitrios Kambouris/Getty Images for iHeartMedia)
The profile discusses songs by pop stars Charli xcx and Olivia Rodrigo that some fans have linked to Swift. Some listeners have interpreted Charli xcx's Sympathy is a knife as reflecting insecurity around a major pop star and Swift's Actually Romantic as a response. Rodrigo has not publicly identified the subject of the grudge.
The article also complicates the simple feud narrative, including mention of Swift praising Charli xcx's songwriting and dancing during one of Rodrigo's performances.
It also revisits Swift's dispute over the rights to her early recordings. After Big Machine Records sold the recordings to Scooter Braun's company, Swift said she had not been given the opportunity to buy them. She later re-recorded the albums.

Wealth, wedding coverage and online reaction
Vanity Fair's account of Swift and Travis Kelce's wedding at Madison Square Garden, as well as the couple's reported donations of $26 million US, drew attention to the profile's tone.
The line "Beyond need lies want. Beyond want, greed" was especially criticized. In his column, Variety chief music critic Chris Willman called the piece a "failed exposé," while some readers said it made them more sympathetic. One X user wrote: "The Vanity Fair hit piece on Taylor Swift just made me like her even more lol."
The case for scrutiny
According to Lee Kong, the profile's format is "referential to Taylor Swift's work herself." It uses labels such as "The Exec" rather than names and includes hidden messages like bolded letters that refer to Swift's song Look What You Made Me Do. Still, she acknowledges that some readers may find the piece mean-spirited or ineffective.
Celebrity journalism can also be shaped by reporters' dependence on access, Kong said. Outlets may hesitate to criticize public figures if they worry about losing the chance to interview them.
At the same time, she argued, scrutiny still matters because celebrities can wield enormous cultural and economic influence and because fandom itself shapes public behaviour.
"I think telling the truth about people that play these really important roles actually is serious journalism."
