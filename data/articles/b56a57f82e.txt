Paraguay's impeached ex-president charged with crimes linked to collapse of major bank
Santiago Peña charged with breach of trust, administration for personal gain
A Paraguayan judge on Saturday ordered former President Santiago Peña held in pretrial detention at a military facility while prosecutors investigate his ties to digital lender Ueno, whose collapse has left millions of customers struggling to access their accounts.
Judge Humberto Otazú ordered Peña, who has been charged with breach of trust and administration for personal gain, held at the facility, saying detention was necessary to ensure he remained in the country while the case proceeded.
The detention, a day after Peña's removal from office, deepens a political and financial crisis that has prompted protests in Asunción, swept up senior officials and led regulators to try to contain risks to the wider financial system.
Peña's attorney, Ricardo Preda, told journalists that Peña was pleading not guilty to the charges.
"We will submit to the investigation and I am sure that when this investigation is over the result will be the dismissal," Preda said.
Otazú also ordered former central bank chief Carlos Carvallo to pretrial detention in a holding centre after prosecutors charged him with breach of trust, producing false public documents and administration for personal gain.
Carvallo did not respond to a request for comment.
Conflict of interest accusations
Peña has been accused of using his political influence to steer hundreds of millions of dollars in public funds into Ueno while regulators overlooked risky insider lending.
Prosecutors say that relationship created a conflict of interest as Peña encouraged, facilitated or influenced decisions benefiting the bank.
Rumblings of the crisis started in June, when inspectors at the central bank found a pattern of risky lending to insiders, reported ABC Color, a leading Paraguayan newspaper.
Ueno Bank executives arrive in custody at the anti-corruption prosecutor's office in Asunción on Wednesday. (Jorge Saenz/The Associated Press)
Ueno had extended large sums to companies tied to its owners, beyond what the law allows and without enough collateral to cover the debts if they went bad.
By November, regulators investigating Ueno's loans to companies linked to its owners found poorly documented lending, the central bank said, as well as expenses the bank had wrongly counted as assets.
Once the books were corrected, Ueno's capital — the cushion banks must keep to absorb losses — fell to less than half the legal minimum.
A person stands outside a closed branch of Ueno Bank in Asunción on Thursday. (Cesar Olmedo/Reuters)
The central bank announced the lender's seizure Tuesday — freezing the accounts of nearly three million customers — and began a process of transferring deposits and recoverable assets to other banks before liquidating what remained.
Ueno's app, cards and transfers went dark and its branches locked their doors, leaving desperate depositors lined up outside.
On Wednesday, a rush to open new accounts elsewhere crashed other banks' mobile apps, fuelling public panic.
People line up outside a branch of the Banco Familiar bank in Asunción on Wednesday. (Daniel Duarte/AFP/Getty Images)
The central bank on Saturday said the equivalent of $160 million US had been paid out to nearly 1.2 million savers, under the state deposit guarantee scheme.
Peña, who was a shareholder in Ueno’s holding company until March 2025, has denied wrongdoing and said the bank's problems were a regulatory matter.
The two charges against Peña could carry a sentence of up to 10 years in prison each, prosecutors say.
Carvallo resigned as central bank chief earlier this week as the crisis surrounding Ueno escalated. He had previously headed the digital lender until 2023.
Prosecutors have charged 27 bank officials and associates with breach of trust and attempted fraud.
'The public is fed up'
Peña submitted his resignation Thursday, but Congress did not accept it and pressed ahead with an impeachment trial. Opposition lawmakers insisted on his removal to prevent him from leaving office with immunity.
"He must go, but not home. He must go to a maximum-security prison," opposition Sen. Yolanda Paredes told reporters. "The public is fed up with impunity."
His ouster brought Vice-President Pedro Alliana to power, with the longtime ruling Colorado Party politician sworn in on Friday to complete Peña’s term through August 2028.
Pedro Alliana addresses lawmakers after taking the oath of office as Paraguay's new president on Friday after Peña's impeachment. (Jorge Saenz/The Associated Press)
The collapse has left millions of customers struggling to access their accounts and prompted protests in Asunción.
Thousands of protesters demanding access to their frozen savings and accountability for Ueno's failure have clashed with police firing rubber bullets and pepper spray this week.
Paraguay's largest teachers' union, which has more than 40,000 members, plans to begin an indefinite national strike Monday to press for protection of public pension funds.
Protesters gather outside Peña's residence in Asunción on Friday. (Cesar Olmedo/Reuters)
On Saturday, a few dozen protesters gathered outside the attorney general's compound hoping to express their frustration with Peña, who was driven quickly out of the building.
Some of the protesters said they also want Paraguay's new president, who was a close ally of Peña, to be investigated.
"They are all accomplices," said Liliana Jara, a demonstrator who showed up at the site. "They are part of the same clique."
