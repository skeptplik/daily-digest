The cadence of prime numbers has a pattern; it isn’t simply chaos. Mathematician Bernhard Riemann suspected this was the case, but he never proved it conclusively, and his mathematical successors have spent more than a century trying to demonstrate that he was right. The Riemann hypothesis may now be proven, and it could reveal the most basic structure of our number system, while also opening the door for a new understanding of the vast world of mathematical functions.
In 1859, Riemann made a casual remark about a mathematical function in his memoir, qualifying a statement about it as “very probably” true. The fact that he never proved it more definitively has held the world of mathematics in suspense ever since, and the “Riemann hypothesis” became the field’s most famous and stubbornly unsolved problem – until now. A new AI-assisted proof by Levent Alpöge at Anthropic may have just ended a whole era of mathematics by confirming Riemann’s hunch from nearly 200 years ago.
“This result, if true, will be the culmination of a struggle thousands of mathematicians have attempted in the past,” says Christopher Hughes at the University of York in the UK. The proof of the Reimann hypothesis is also one of the Clay Mathematics Institute’s Millennium Prize Problems, each of which comes with a $1 million prize for a solution.
The Riemann hypothesis has loomed so large over mathematicians for decades because it concerns mathematics’ most simple yet hard-to-understand objects: prime numbers. These are numbers that aren’t divisible by any number other than 1 and themselves, and any number can be built by multiplying primes. In this way, primes are the bones of all systems of numbers; everything is built on top of them. But how primes are distributed among all numbers is, on the face of it, unpredictable. This is where Riemann made an intervention.
He hypothesised that the way primes are spaced throughout the number line can be inferred from another set of numbers, now tied to a mathematical function called the Riemann zeta function. These numbers are complex – so each is actually a pair of numbers, one called real and one called imaginary – and when they are plugged into the Riemann zeta function, the function spits out zero. Confusingly, these numbers that result in a zero from the function are called “non-trivial zeros”. Riemann discovered that summing over a sequence of these complex numbers allowed him to predict the distribution of primes up to a certain high number. Effectively, he came upon a formula that could make sense of primes’ seemingly random appearances among numbers.
But the formula only works if all the zeros always sit on the same vertical line when plotted in a coordinate system where the horizontal axis tracks their real number constituents and the vertical axis their imaginary ones. Riemann calculated some of these zeros, which confirmed his idea, but could not prove the hypothesis more generally. It became the biggest unsolved problem in all of mathematics. “It is a legendary problem, the queen of all the Millennium Problems,” says Scott Armstrong at Sorbonne University in Paris. “For the whole field of mathematics, there could not be a bigger theorem.”
In the intervening years, mathematicians used computers to verify that the first 12 trillion nontrivial zeros line up on Riemann’s vertical line and much work has gone into proving that the zeros at least must lie in a strip of the coordinate systems around this line. In fact, a previous release of AI proofs by OpenAI on 6 October contained the proof of a weaker version of the Riemann hypothesis, placing the non-trivial zeros in the neighbourhood but not exactly on the line Riemann predicted.
In a statement released to the public, Alpöge wrote that his proof, developed with Anthropic’s Claude AI, is over 250,000 pages long, and it has not yet been fully read, digested or understood by the mathematics community. Even its translation into the language of the proof-checking software Lean is 17,000,000 lines long. That means it will take time to carefully adjudicate. “I have not had a chance to read the 17 million lines of Lean, so cannot speak to their truth,” says Alex Walker at University College London. And the announcement didn’t illuminate the details of the method, he adds.
Consequently, it is currently unclear exactly what mathematical tricks the AI leveraged, though early commentators suggest that it drew on a vast swathe of mathematics spanning several fields including number theory and arithmetic geometry. Some have suggested the AI made connections across these fields that don’t already clearly exist in the literature.
The Lean code is in the process of being adapted to efficiently run on a computer that can review it, which will provide a crucial check on the new proof. “While Lean itself has some issues, so far none of the AI results with Lean certificates have had to be retracted,” says Scott Aaronson at the University of Texas at Austin. Still, it may take years to unravel all the details of the new proof and reap any of its mathematical insights, he says. But the ramifications will certainly be enormous and extensive.
Notably, it resolves the biggest open question of number theory by finally making primes make sense. Numerous results also build on the assumption that Riemann was right and will now stand on firmer ground. And then there are implications for functions similar to the Riemann zeta function, known as the generalised Riemann hypothesis. “An extremely large number of results have previously been proven subject to the generalised Riemann hypothesis,” says John Nicholson at the Max Planck Institute for Mathematics in Germany. He says there are also a number of fast algorithms for computing quantities in algebraic number theory whose correctness depends on the generalised Riemann hypothesis. The Riemann hypothesis even has ties to theories of chaos in quantum systems in physics.
“The failure of the Riemann hypothesis would create havoc in the distribution of prime numbers,” wrote Enrico Bombieri at Princeton University when outlining the Millenium Problem more than a quarter of a century ago. Now, thanks to a combination of human ingenuity and relentlessness, and the computational power of AI, that distribution may have been shown to be not that dangerous after all.
