Countries including South Korea and the Philippines are becoming increasingly attractive to renewables developers such as Singapore-based Peak Energy, as governments accelerate clean energy adoption to overcome fuel disruptions due to the U.S.-Iran war.
Seoul has “set targets to connect more power to the grid and push costs down,” said Gavin Adda, chief executive of the company backed by Stonepeak Partners, an alternative investment firm that manages about $98 billion in assets. “You’re seeing a big increase in the number of developers trying to work out how to operate there.”
The growing interest comes as conflict in the Middle East continues to choke flows of liquefied natural gas from Qatar — the world’s second producer last year — and keeps prices of the fuel at multi-year highs. That’s forced governments to turn to alternatives, including accelerating the adoption of solar and wind. South Korea depended on Qatar for about 15% of its LNG supplies last year, according to World Bank data.
South Korean authorities in September announced measures to ease restrictions on renewable energy projects connecting to the power grid, particularly in the southwestern Honam region. The grid-connection date for one of Peak Energy’s solar farms has been brought forward by five years to 2029, with other developers seeing similar improvements, Adda said.
The country’s power demand should also increase as the artificial intelligence boom drives a rapid buildout of data centers, and Peak Energy has received requests to supply electricity to hyperscalers, he added.
Peak Energy has $250 million in projects in South Korea and another $100 million’s worth getting underway in 2027. These account for a large share of the firm’s 300 megawatts of operating assets, which are spread across eight Asia-Pacific markets including the Philippines, Japan, Australia and Taiwan.
The Iran conflict has also pushed the Philippines to bring renewable projects online earlier. The Southeast Asian country already allows full foreign ownership of projects and has a green lane to speed up approvals for major investments.
“It’s the fastest country in Asia for developing renewable projects,” Adda said in an interview last week. “So I can shake hands with you as a landowner, and I can start construction one year to 18 months later.”
The Philippines is expected to add 5.6 gigawatts of new solar capacity this year, more than all of its Southeast Asian peers, according to BloombergNEF forecasts. While the pipeline remains robust, the Department of Energy earlier this year terminated agreements for nearly 18 GW of clean-power projects, as many prospective investors had no plans to build them and were waiting to sell the rights.
