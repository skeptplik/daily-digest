Genomic testing can answer a variety of pressing health questions a person (and their clinician) might face throughout their lifespan.
Unfortunately, these valuable tests have become fragmented and iterative for most patients. A patient may receive a carrier screening test before or during pregnancy; a cancer predisposition test to screen for inherited cancer-predisposition variants like BRCA, the breast cancer gene; and a pharmacogenomic test before starting a medication. Each test is a new blood draw or buccal swab, a new wet lab workflow, a new spot on a genome sequencer, a new analysis, and new costs borne by patients and insurance companies.
With the precipitous decline in the cost of whole genome sequencing, it’s time to consolidate testing by sequencing genomes once and reevaluating them when needed.
As research has shown, patients face many challenges to accessing genomic testing including financial barriers, limited access to providers, geographic limitations, and clinician biases. Since the genome can enable diagnoses and care for so many health conditions, a more streamlined and uniform approach to genomic testing would likely benefit many patients. I call it “test once, evaluate often.”
Is one test really enough?
A “test once, evaluate often” approach could start at any age. For some families, that might be at birth, as an optional add-on to the newborn screening their baby already gets. A single whole genome sequence could be stored, along with other medical records information, for future analysis. Any time a clinician orders a new genomic test, the lab would simply reevaluate the relevant genes and issue a report.
This approach would dramatically lower the cost and turnaround time needed to answer genomic questions by eliminating the sample handling and wet lab processing of patient samples, potentially improving access to genomic information for patients who would otherwise choose to forgo testing due to costs.
Such a paradigm would only apply to tests looking at germline DNA (the DNA we are born with). Clinical genomic tests seeking to understand the genetics of other biological characteristics, such as somatic DNA in a cancer tissue, circulating fetal DNA in a noninvasive prenatal test, or circulating tumor DNA in liquid biopsy panels would still be needed to look for genomic variants beyond germline DNA when patient circumstances dictate.
What about newborns?
Newborn screening is one of public health’s great successes. For more than 60 years, nearly every U.S. baby has had a heel-prick blood test for dozens of serious, treatable conditions, catching thousands of babies each year who look perfectly healthy.
For many, early diagnosis is the difference between life and death. Infants with severe combined immunodeficiency who receive a transplant by 3.5 months survive 94% of the time. Babies destined for the most severe form of spinal muscular atrophy who receive gene therapy before symptoms emerge go on to sit independently and breathe without permanent ventilation. Any interruption to newborn screening would cost children their lives.
Genome sequencing builds on that foundation. In the BabySeq Project, 9.4% of newborns had an unexpected genetic risk for childhood-onset disease. In New York’s GUARDIAN study, 72% of families opted in and 3.7% of babies screened positive, including for treatable conditions that standard screening misses. Because sequencing alone misses some cases that biochemical tests catch, it should add to the heel prick, not replace it.
In childhood, reporting can stay focused on conditions where knowing early changes care; the rest of the genome can wait until there’s a question to answer. And as with any public health program, clear consent and transparent data use must be built in from the start.
Regular evaluation of the genome is critical
The need for genomic information changes with patient circumstances and throughout their lifespan. This is why regular evaluation of the genome is needed in place of the piecemeal approach to ordering individual tests today.
Unfortunately, genomic science is not yet in a place where a single point of evaluation is practical. Evidence about emerging gene-disease relationships and new understandings of variants associated with hereditary cancer and other diseases continue to be adopted into clinical practice, meaning that an analysis conducted 20 years into the future would likely deliver more information than is currently possible.
The genomics community has honed a version of this practice, termed reanalysis, in the rare disease space. There, it is commonplace to reexamine a patient’s exome or genome based on emerging clinical evidence about gene-disease relationships.
Reevaluation is a bit different. Reanalysis is done for the same testing indication (i.e., finding the genetic cause of a rare disease) and reevaluation is studying the same genome for a new testing indication.
The National Human Genome Research Institute envisions a future where genomic data is systematically collected and used more regularly and efficiently for patient benefit. Getting there means moving clinical and laboratory medicine toward a system in which a genome sequenced once, whether at birth or later in life, helps a person’s clinicians manage their health throughout their life.
Making this vision a reality will require several changes to the way medicine is practiced and paid for today.
First, health systems, laboratories, and policymakers need to agree on how genomic data will be stored securely for decades and who can see it. Patients already trust online patient portals with their lab results and medical records, and genomic data can be held to the same standards of security and transparency.
These questions are already being actively worked on. Leading newborn sequencing programs, such as New York’s GUARDIAN study and England’s Generation Study, ask parents for opt-in consent, keep data under strict access controls, and are studying how long it should be kept. Leftover newborn screening blood spots have also been stored for years in many states, often without parents knowing, which is why consent and transparency have become central to this work. Any program that stores genomes, at birth or later, must start with fully informed consent: patients and parents should know what is kept, where, for how long and who may use it, and be free to say no.
Second, additional genetic counseling resources would need to support patients along their genomic health journeys, helping balance clinical needs, desires for information, and the familial implications of genomic insights. That support matters even more as care becomes increasingly patient-driven, with more people ordering tests themselves, reading their own results, and coming back with new questions.
Finally, and perhaps most important, insurers need a way to pay for evaluating a genome that has already been sequenced. Today, insurance can cover reanalysis, but there is no clear path to pay for reevaluation, the step that makes “test once, evaluate often” work. Paying for it would save insurers money, too, since it could replace repeat sequencing over a patient’s lifetime. Without reimbursement for prevention-oriented sequencing, genetic counseling, and genomic evaluation, we can’t improve equity in genomic testing.
Sharing data, finding answers
Something I’ve seen again and again in the rare disease setting is that parents of a child without a diagnosis are almost always eager to share their child’s genetic data. They know that a match with another family somewhere in the world may be what finally explains what’s happening, and many new gene discoveries start exactly that way.
If we protect genomic data well and show people what sharing makes possible, more of us may come to see it the way these parents do: not as a risk to minimize, but as a path to answers, prevention, and cures.
Kelly Hagman, M.S., is a genetic counselor and vice president of medical affairs and strategic integration at Ambry Genetics, a Tempus company. She led the launch of the industry’s first clinical exome and has authored more than 55 peer-reviewed publications.
