Fewer international students are studying at US universities as the Trump administration cracks down on immigration and reshapes higher education, data released on Monday shows.
Overall, US campuses saw a 9% decrease in international enrolment this autumn compared to last year, according to a survey from the Institute of International Education.
And the number of new students entering the United States for the first time fell by 15%.
“No one aspires to send their kids to get educated in the US,” said Veena Dubal, general counsel for the American Association of University Professors, whose father travelled from India to attend Tuskegee University, then the Tuskegee Institute, in the late 1960s on a student visa.
"It feels like an unsafe place. That’s the message that the Trump administration has been sending to families all over the world and they’re listening.”
Last year, the enrolment decreases were partially offset by large numbers of students who stayed in the US for temporary work after graduating.
But after months of speculation, the Department of Homeland Security (DHS) proposed requiring schools to pay a $70,000 (€63,583) fee for every student who participates in the optional practice training programme.
Although the rule hasn't been finalised, participation in the programme, known as OPT, dropped 6% this fall, according the survey.
International students facing political headwinds
The proposed rule, which is expected to face legal challenges, is part of wider DHS efforts that include terminating the legal status of thousands of international students, along with detaining and deporting many of them.
And this summer, the administration finalised a rule that will prevent international students from staying in the US for more than four years without approval from the federal government, although a judge has temporarily blocked it.
DHS Secretary Markwayne Mullin has said the changes were intended to crack down on loopholes that international students were exploiting by extending their studies.
Although the US remains the top destination for international students globally, the changes are having an effect, college leaders told the IIE.
Among more than 800 US colleges and universities surveyed, 88% blamed the visa application process and 70% blamed travel restrictions for the enrolment drops.
And if OPT options are limited, 95% of schools predicted that international students would likely look to another country, according to the survey, which offers an early look at trends before full data is released next year.
Politics aren't the only reason students are looking elsewhere, said Mirka Martel, head of research, evaluation and learning for the institute. Its survey found more global competition for international students and the rising cost of a US degree also contributed.
Fanta Aw, the executive director and CEO of NAFSA, an association that represents international education, said students also are worried about research funding.
Last year, the Trump administration began dismantling the federal grant-giving process and attempting to exert more political influence over which projects get funded.
“They’re saying to themselves, ‘Too much uncertainty in the United States. It’s too difficult, and you know, more importantly, we have many more choices today than we did 10 years ago. Why subject ourselves to this?’” Aw said, noting that students are going to places like China.
NAFSA, in partnership with the international education technology company JB International, found that amid the enrolment decline, international students’ contributions to the US economy dropped by $2.4 billion (€2.1 billion) this autumn.
Colleges see budgets squeezed
US colleges and universities heavily recruit students from overseas as a source of tuition revenue. So the drops have punched big holes in tuition revenue at some schools.
At Cleveland State University, international enrolment has dropped by 46% in two years, from 1,542 in fall 2025 to 828 this fall.
And fewer than 50 new international students started this year, said Provost Nigamanth Sridhar, who estimated that the budgetary impact ranges from $2-4 million (€1-3 million).
Decades ago, he arrived in the US on an F1 student visa and participated in OPT. Eventually, he went through the process to became a naturalised citizen.
“I am here in this position, in this job, because I had the opportunity to come to the United States as an international graduate student and then decided to stay here and build a career," said Sridhar, who is from India. "So international education is personal to me."
The biggest drops were at the graduate level. This autumn's data shows a 15% drop in international graduate enrolment, compared to a 3% decrease at the undergraduate level.
The report also found declines in international students from India, which sends more students to the US to study than any other country.
