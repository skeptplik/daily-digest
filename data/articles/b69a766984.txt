- The light carries valuable information about the early history of the universe.
Researchers at the European Space Agency on Tuesday announced the discovery of the most distant, and therefore oldest, quasar ever observed.
The discovery, made by a German astronomer and his team, was published in the journal Astronomy and Astrophysics.
A quasar refers to an extremely bright core of an active galaxy powered by gas falling into a supermassive black hole.
Quasars represent a brief phase in a galaxy's life, during which the nucleus can outshine the rest of the system.
"As enormous amounts of gas, dust and matter fall toward the black hole, they form a swirling accretion disk that heats up to extreme temperatures and radiates tremendous amounts of energy, outshining the entire rest of the galaxy by factors of hundreds or even thousands," said astronomer and study co-author Silvia Belladitta of the Max Planck Institute for Astronomy in Germany and the INAF Bologna Observatory of Astrophysics and Space Science in Italy.
"This makes quasars visible across billions of light-years, allowing us to observe them from the very early universe. Studying quasars helps astronomers understand how the first galaxies formed and evolved, and how the supermassive black holes at their hearts grew to such enormous sizes," Belladitta said.
More evidence that black holes were extremely massive very early on
The newly discovered object, formally known as J1504+6252, is nicknamed Dún Aonghas.
The red dot in the image below corresponds to seeing the universe as it was roughly 13.1 billion years ago — just 5% of its current age.
"I think it adds to the evidence that some objects in the early universe developed remarkably quickly. Just 630 million years after the Big Bang, this black hole had already grown to about 360 million times the mass of the sun and was shining as a quasar," said astronomer Julien Wolf of the European Southern Observatory in Germany, lead author of the research.
The discovery then supports the scenario in which supermassive black holes form through the direct collapse of large gas clouds and subsequently accumulate additional matter.
Dún Aonghas was first detected by the European Space Agency's Euclid telescope, with images having been characterized using a combination of ground- and space-based telescopes.
The ESA's orbiting telescope is meant for missions that uncover the mysteries of dark matter, which is the invisible glue lumping galaxies together, and dark energy, the force that pushes them apart.
