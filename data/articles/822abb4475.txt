'Unacceptable': China, EU among major economies against new US tariffs
China, the European Union and other major trading partners on Thursday criticised new US tariffs of up to 20 percent proposed by US President Donald Trump after a probe into alleged global overcapacity, raising the prospect of fresh trade tensions with allies and rivals alike.
Major economies from China to the EU hit out at proposed new US tariffs after Washington concluded its months-long probe into alleged excess production, potentially opening a new phase in trade tensions with both allies and foes alike.
The new tolls, which follow an investigation launched days after the Supreme Court struck down US President Donald Trump's previous duties, range up to 20 percent and face a period of public comment before taking effect, at the earliest in January.
The levies target some of the United States' largest trading partners, with China, the European Union, India, Bangladesh, Cambodia, Indonesia, Japan, South Korea, Malaysia, Singapore, Switzerland, Taiwan, Thailand and Vietnam in the crosshairs.
Some, such as Japan, denied having excessive production.
"Japan does not have any non-market policy practices or structural excess production capacity... It is regrettable that a proposed measure targeting Japan has been announced in spite of this," the country's top government spokesman Minoru Kihara said.
A senior US official, speaking on condition of anonymity, noted that the proposals sought to take existing trade deals into account.
China said it reserves the right to take necessary measures to safeguard its legitimate ‌rights and interests, after the United ‌States proposed new tariffs on Chinese goods based on a Section 301 investigation over excess capacity.
The ‌US investigation is an act of unilateralism, ‌and China firmly opposes it, Commerce Ministry spokesperson He Yadong said at a press briefing.
China urged the US to "correct it wrong practices and returnto the right track of resolving issues through dialogue and consultation," He said.

'Real concern'
The European Union said it rejected the premise of new tariffs and expected the United States to stick by a deal struck last year.
"The EU and the US share a real concern about global overcapacity, much of it state-driven and concentrated in third countries. We regret the lack of joint action," European Commission spokesman Olof Gill said.
The EU's trade commissioner Maros Sefcovic, who also rejected the US tariff decision, said that it ‌was important Washington respects the 15% levy the bloc had concluded with the United States.

And Singapore said on Thursday it was "very disappointed" with the proposals and denied it was protecting its domestic market or subsidising manufacturing.
Vietnam also faces a 20-percent tariff; Cambodia and Indonesia 18 percent; and India 17 percent, according to the USTR's office.
The EU, South Korea and Taiwan, which have signed trade deals with the United States, will see tariffs broadly capped at 15 percent.
In a separate announcement, the USTR also proposed additional tariffs on goods from Brazil, including aircraft, over alleged inaction on addressing US trade concerns.
The Brazilian president's office called the measures "unacceptable" and said Brasilia would "not yield to this type of threat."
Trump's tariffs have targeted Washington's friends and foes alike, and are part of a push in his second term to remake global trade by forcing countries to buy US products or produce their goods in the country.
Overcapacity concerns affect a range of sectors, from batteries to cement and chemicals to processed food, the senior US official said.

'Hollowing out'
The duties could add further pressure on US businesses already squeezed by earlier tariffs, with firms now likelier to pass on expenses to consumers already frustrated with the high cost of living.
Affordability was a key issue in this month's US midterm elections, which saw Trump's Republican Party swept out of power in both chambers of Congress by Democrats.
The announcement came as data showed the Federal Reserve's preferred inflation gauge at a three-year high in October, adding to pressure to hike interest rates.
Trade lawyer Ryan Majerus said that he expected this probe to be among "the most important" actions for the Trump administration, given its underlying concern that factories with excess capacity start operations abroad and "never get turned off, even if there's no demand."
This potentially leads to an oversupply of goods, depressed prices and a "hollowing out" of US manufacturing, he added.
While the US Supreme Court struck down Trump's global tariffs in February, his sector-specific duties remain intact.
The new levies will not stack on top of those sector-specific tariffs on items like automobiles and steel, the senior US official said.
In July, Trump imposed a separate wave of tariffs on 60 economies, accusing them of insufficient action against forced labor.
For economies facing both the forced labor and excess production tariffs, the levies would not stack up and the higher rate will prevail, the US official said.
