On Nov. 22, streamers who uploaded their GTA 6 gameplay on YouTube were suddenly hit with a barrage of copyright strikes. They all came from a single source: Colombian reggaeton artist Frank "El Santo."
The Medellín-born singer and songwriter, whose real name is Andrés Mauricio Arango Flórez, played GTA 6 on stream on his YouTube channel. His footage was then used to auto-claim copyright on anyone who was playing the game live on YouTube. This resulted in indiscriminate strikes against users, including big channels such as theRadBrad (14.1 million subscribers) and HollowPoiint (5.2 million subscribers).
Reggaeton artist hits major YouTube streamers with GTA 6 copyright strikes
This seemingly absurd situation arose from a combination of specific circumstances and YouTube's often-criticized copyright policies. Frank El Santo's YouTube channel contains his official music videos, so it's possible that the platform mistakenly deemed the GTA 6 gameplay video eligible for copyright claims. After commenters pointed out the issue during his stream, Arango Flórez deleted the gameplay video, but the copyright strikes continued until the YouTube team intervened.
Arango Flórez's only official response to the issue is a comment under his now-deleted gameplay video, stating that the claims were not filed by him and were instead the result of the automated Content ID system. However, YouTube doesn't necessarily issue copyright claims automatically. Many angry fans online are claiming that the artist must have done this on purpose.
Regardless of the intent, this episode highlights that YouTube's copyright policies are either ineffective or outright damaging to its users. The company relies on automated processes and AI-assisted tools to detect copyright infringement, often resulting in videos being taken down or demonetized without proper reason. As HollowPoiint put it in a post on X where he praised YouTube's quick intervention to resolve the GTA 6 El Santo issue, "Now we just need a better system in place."
GTA 6 has been streamed nonstop since the game launched on Nov. 19. Millions of people worldwide are experiencing the game not by playing it themselves, but by watching other people play it. While this can often result in biased opinions, it's also undeniable that not everyone will be able to play Rockstar's game from the start, especially considering its hefty price. Despite its shortcomings, streaming is still an important element of video game culture; after one of the biggest launches in entertainment history, YouTube should probably refine its policies to make sure content creators don't get hit unnecessarily.
