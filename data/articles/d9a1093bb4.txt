Saudi Arabia continues to execute people at an alarming rate. On Saturday, Jawad Abdullah Qureiris and Fadel Hassan al-Safwani were executed in Saudi Arabia's Eastern Province. Both men were convicted of terrorist offenses, according to Saudi authorities. In 2011, al-Safwani was a prominent organizer of the peaceful protests that called for civil rights and the release of detainees. Qureiris, who was a minor at the time, participated in one of these protests in the city of Qatif.
The UN Working Group on Arbitrary Detention called for Qureiris' release in November 2024, after finding that his detention was arbitrary. The opinion also covered four other men. Qureiris was the fourth of these five to be executed. The fifth, Yousef al-Manasif, remains at risk after Saudi Arabia's Supreme Court upheld his death sentence, according to human rights organizations.
The two executions brought the number of people executed in Saudi Arabia in 2026 to 211, according to an AFP tally reported on Monday. The news agency said that the tally includes 126 people convicted of drug trafficking and 17 people executed on terrorism-related charges. Of the 211 people, 100 were Saudi nationals, while the rest were foreign nationals, AFP said.
In July, Saudi authorities executed five Ethiopian migrants for nonviolent drug-related offenses, Human Rights Watch reported. In October, Saudi authorities executed two Bangladeshi nationals, according to rights organizations. Human Rights Watch separately reported the execution of another Saudi national, Hassan Zaki al-Faraj, on October 17, for alleged offenses committed when he was a minor.
"In 2025, a record high of at least 356 people were executed and executions have continued at a high rate in 2026," Ahmed Benchemsi, Human Rights Watch's MENA spokesperson, told DW. "Saudi Arabia has executed more than 2,000 people in the past decade," he confirmed.
According to the Death Penalty Information Center, Saudi Arabia ranks third in executions after China and Iran. However, comprehensive global data for 2026 are not yet available, and many countries treat execution numbers as state secrets. However, the ranking is consistent with earlier findings from Amnesty International, which ranked Saudi Arabia as the world's third-most prolific executioner between 2022 and 2025, behind China and Iran.
According to the NGO Together against the death penalty, or ECPM, the two main methods of execution in Saudi Arabia are stoning or beheading. However, in many cases, including those of Qureiris and al-Safwani, death sentences are issued as discretionary rulings, called ta'zir. Ta'zir means that neither the crime nor the punishment is defined in Islamic law, contrary to fixed punishments under the Saudi interpretation of Islamic law, or Sharia. As a result, little to no information about the execution or the fate of the bodies is provided.
Is the Iran war affecting Saudi domestic repression?
Following the US-Israeli attacks on Iran in February, Saudi Arabia has been targeted by Iran and the Iran-aligned Houthi group in Yemen. In turn, Saudi Arabia's de facto leader, Crown Prince Mohammed bin Salman, is faced with a profound dilemma as the attacks have killed and injured scores of people and threaten Riyadh's efforts to diversify its economy under the Vision 2030 reform plan. Several summits and international sports events had to be canceled or postponed in light of the ongoing danger.
"The Saudi government has shown itself to be unable to defend its population from repeated Houthi attacks on its airports, exposing the vulnerability of the country despite world-record spending levels on defense equipment," Sarah Leah Whitson, human rights lawyer and former executive director of the US-based human rights watchdog DAWN, told DW. "Amid the war in Iran, the Saudi government has resumed its relentless pace of executions and corporal punishment sentences," she said.
This week, watchdogs reported on a Kenyan woman in Saudi Arabia who was sentenced to 100 lashes for giving birth outside marriage.
"The recent executions and the sentencing of a migrant domestic worker to 100 lashes for having a child out of wedlock are clear indications that the Saudi government continues to rely on brute repression to rule the country," Whitson told DW.
Saudi Arabia's repression against the Shia minority
Maryam Aldossari, acting director of the London-based human rights organization ALQST, echoes this view. "Saudi Arabian authorities have undoubtedly used the war in Iran and wider regional tensions to intensify their crackdown, most notably through their increased use of the death penalty and as a tool of political repression against the country's Shia minority," she told DW.
Saudi Arabia is predominantly Sunni. The country's oil-rich Eastern Province — from which the recently executed men Jawad Abdullah Qureiris and Fadel Hassan al-Safwani hailed — is home to most of Saudi Arabia's Shia minority. Iran, on the other hand, is predominantly Shia. Saudi authorities have long viewed some forms of Shia political activism with suspicion, partly because of concerns over Iranian influence and alleged links between domestic dissent and Tehran, accusations that rights groups say have been used to justify repression of the minority.
However, Neil Quilliam, associate fellow in the Middle East and North Africa Programme at the London-based think tank Chatham House, remains cautious about drawing a direct causal connection between the conflict involving Iran and developments in Saudi Arabia's human rights environment. "The kingdom has introduced social reforms over the past decade, including measures that have expanded opportunities for women, while retaining controls over political dissent and activism," he told DW.
"What periods of regional tension can do is heighten official concern about national security," he said, adding that this reduces tolerance for any public criticism or perceived threats to social cohesion. "Whether that amounts to a change in the human rights situation is harder to demonstrate, but there is certainly a sense among some observers that space for dissent has become even more constrained, not only in Saudi Arabia, but in other Gulf states too," he said.
DW reached out to Saudi Arabia's Human Rights Commission on Monday, November, 16, 2026 but has not received an answer at the time of publication.
