This group of seamstresses mends shirts to make chemo easier for cancer patients
Seamstresses in Ohio and other states are making zippered shirts for cancer patients so they can more comfortably undergo chemotherapy treatments.
JUANA SUMMERS, HOST:
Chemotherapy can be long and difficult - the treatments, the side effects, the uncertainty. Even small tasks can feel like a huge undertaking. A group of seamstresses hopes to fix one small challenge. Kendall Crawford of The Ohio Newsroom reports.
KENDALL CRAWFORD, BYLINE: Every couple of months or so, Pat Cochran turns her brightly colored craft room in southwest Ohio into a classroom. She calls it...
(SOUNDBITE OF ZIPPER ZIPPING)
CRAWFORD: ...Her zipper school. Instead of fastening zippers onto jackets or pants, Cochran demonstrates how to sew them onto a shirt for someone undergoing chemotherapy.
PAT COCHRAN: You're going to sew that, and you're going to iron that all the way around, except for a bit at the top. OK? You're going to leave that open.
CRAWFORD: She started back in 2018 after her friend, Dennis Seger, described having to change clothes to receive chemotherapy through a treatment port in his chest.
COCHRAN: He did not want to put on a patient gown. You know, you're cold anyway, and you take off your clothes. And you're, you know - and then you have to put everything back on.
CRAWFORD: It was a small indignity, but one she knew, as a studied seamstress, she could fix. She made Seger three T-shirts with a zipper across the chest that allowed nurses to access his port more easily. He wore them to every chemo appointment.
COCHRAN: He came back and he said, well, the nurses want to know if you'll make us some more.
CRAWFORD: She enlisted some of her friends to help meet the demand. Now, almost a decade later, the group gets around 7,000 new donated shirts a year and makes them chemo-friendly. They call themselves the gang of seamstresses.
COCHRAN: Gangstresses - that's what I call them, the gangstresses.
CRAWFORD: Patty Luther traveled an hour from central Ohio to join the gang. Cochran inspects her zipper work and advises Luther to add some soft swimsuit fabric underneath its teeth.
COCHRAN: A lot of patients have...
PATTY LUTHER: Sensitivities?
COCHRAN: Sensitivities.
LUTHER: Yeah, yeah, yeah.
COCHRAN: Yeah.
LUTHER: Yeah.
COCHRAN: So we try to make it as comfortable as we can.
CRAWFORD: Luther then took what she learned back to her church and started her own chapter. That's how it's grown. Cochran teaches the technique, that person teaches another. In addition to Ohio, there are now gangstresses in Florida, Wisconsin, Indiana and North Carolina.
COCHRAN: Everybody knows somebody that's had cancer or has cancer. And they want to help that person.
CRAWFORD: Volunteers deliver the T-shirts to cancer treatment centers, where nurses hand them out to patients. The seamstresses leave a card with their name. That human touch meant a lot to Sheila Sennet's husband, Dan.
SHEILA SENNET: There's not a lot of joy to be had in infusion centers (laughter). But that shirt sure made Dan's day.
CRAWFORD: Dan died from cancer in 2023. But Sheila says she hasn't forgotten how the simple blue shirt made him smile at a time when smiles were hard to come by.
SENNET: Oh, and you better believe me when I tell you that shirt needed to be cleaned and ready to go the next time he had chemo (laughter). He just wore that shirt out literally.
CRAWFORD: The same was true for Dennis Seger, Cochran's friend who inspired the gang's start. A photo of him grinning in a zippered graphic tee sits near the sewing machine that Cochran works at.
COCHRAN: We're going to sew this puppy in.
CRAWFORD: Dennis died in 2019. But Cochran says she can still feel his presence through this work.
COCHRAN: Maybe we're running low on shirts of a certain size or something. And, like, within a few days or a week, shirts show up. That's all I'm going to say.
CRAWFORD: It keeps her showing up, too.
(SOUNDBITE OF ZIPPER ZIPPING)
CRAWFORD: Turning clothes into comfort, one zipper at a time.
For NPR News, I'm Kendall Crawford in southwest Ohio.
