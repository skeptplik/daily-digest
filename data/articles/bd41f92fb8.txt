The US government’s top medical research agency has released three new studies linking glyphosate, the world’s most widely used pesticide and an active ingredient in Roundup, to cancer. The National Institutes of Health (NIH) findings contradict a controversial recent Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) conclusion that the weedkiller is not a carcinogen, and deals a blow to the White House, which has been closely allied with Roundup’s producer, Bayer.
The NIH’s three papers could have significant political and legal fallout. The EPA is in the process of a court-ordered re-evaluation of glyphosate’s cancer risks. The NIH findings generate new pressure for the EPA to find that the substance is linked to cancer, which would subject it to a modest increase in regulations.
If the EPA again finds that glyphosate is not carcinogenic, the NIH studies would complicate the legal terrain for it and Bayer. The papers also represent a major public relations hit for Bayer, which has relentlessly defended the herbicide, for which it recorded $2.8bn in sales in 2024.
Nathan Donley, environmental health science director at the Center for Biological Diversity, said the studies raised further questions about chemical companies’ influence on the EPA’s pesticide division, which “parrots industry talking points” in its safety reviews.
“Americans would be smart to heed the cancer findings of the nation’s most trusted scientific institution rather than the industry-influenced claims of the EPA,” Donley said. “The EPA’s pesticide office has made a mockery of pesticide science for far too long.”
The EPA did not respond to a request for comment.
In a statement, Ruveon, a Bayer subsidiary that manages Roundup in the US, said in a statement: “The US EPA, like other leading health authorities globally, has previously and repeatedly concluded that glyphosate does not cause cancer.”
The NIH findings arrive during a year in which the White House has come under fire from across the political spectrum for its support for Bayer. That includes an executive order from Donald Trump that aimed to protect the substance’s production and provide immunity to its producers, and an amicus brief in support of Bayer in an eventual US supreme court case that partially shielded the company from lawsuits.
The NIH did not carry out new studies, but conducted a systematic review of decades of scientific literature from across the globe. It found an association with blood cancers in humans, and that the pesticide caused several types of tumors in animals.
Advocates say the NIH used proper scientific protocol, which is followed by the World Health Organization’s International Agency on Research for Cancer (IARC), and other credible research organizations.
The EPA’s pesticide division reviewed most of the same studies several years ago but came to a different conclusion because it deviated from its own methodology, Donley said. A court threw out the EPA’s findings in 2022 because it did not use proper protocol, and ordered a new risk assessment.
The EPA is expected to finish it early next year, Donley said. The EPA and NIH are often aligned on many chemicals’ risk assessments, but the pesticide division chronically underestimates cancer risks because of its flawed methodology, he added.
“This agency is completely incapable of doing a good, clear, robust cancer assessment, not just with glyphosate, but all pesticides, because they don’t follow their own methodology,” Donley said.
The administration’s support for glyphosate has driven a wedge between pro-industry figures in the White House, and the Make America Healthy Again (Maha) movement, which the health secretary, Robert F Kennedy Jr, spearheads.
Kelly Ryerson, a Maha advocate who has met with top agency officials to advocate for stricter pesticide policies, told the Guardian she is “extremely hopeful that glyphosate’s day of reckoning has arrived”.
“The release of the government-performed studies on glyphosate further prove what many independent scientists have claimed for decades: glyphosate can cause cancer. The EPA will be forced to change its determination of carcinogenicity in the upcoming review,” Ryerson said.
She noted the NIH papers are in addition to a recent US Department of Health and Human Services-commissioned study that similarly reviewed literature on glyphosate’s impact on hormones and found it probably reduced sperm counts and testosterone.
Donley said he is “99.9% certain the EPA is going to again find that glyphosate does not cause cancer”.
“They cannot possibly get their head around any other option, so they’re going to come out with that, then they’re going to get sued, and they’re going to get their behinds handed to them in court, again,” Donley said.
