I love my old iPhone 14, but I’m starting to face the facts: It’s going to die. The battery is about to go; apps take longer to load than they used to. Some of it I can fix. I can go to the Genius Bar and swap the battery. Maybe I could even do it myself; that’d buy me some time. But eventually, Apple won’t support this wildly expensive device at all, and there’s not much I can do about it.
Apple did start offering self-repair services a few years ago, but it involves renting nearly 80 pounds of equipment and requires you to use official parts. When all is said and done, it doesn’t save you that much money versus just going to the Apple Store. But if I buy a screen or a camera component from a third party, there's a chance my iPhone is gonna lock me out of certain features because it doesn't recognize it.
This problem goes far beyond iPhones. Manufacturers have put similar locks on all kinds of gadgets, from video game consoles to cars to smart home devices, making them hard to modify and even harder to restore after some firmware update renders a certain feature unusable.
Not surprisingly, a lot of people think this isn't fair, that there should be a better way to do it. I think at this point it's safe to say it qualifies as a movement, one that unites under the banner of “right to repair.” Built around the radical idea that when you buy something you should be able to truly own it, right-to-repair advocates believe consumers have the right to maintain, fix, or modify any piece of hardware they’ve purchased. The movement has gained even more traction now that AI makes it even easier to hack the software powering consumers’ devices.
To find out what’s next for the right-to-repair movement, I went straight to the source: Louis Rossmann, a YouTuber and consumer rights advocate who recently organized the Own Your Shit tour, which went around the US earlier this year to call for the death of Section 1201 of the Digital Millennium Copyright Act, which prohibits users from bypassing digital restrictions put on devices by manufacturers.
Rossmann and I talked about more than just Section 1201. We also discussed the US midterm elections, putting out bounties to find product fixes, and what will happen as the right-to-repair movement collides with the rise of AI.

Do you mind telling us a little bit about what Section 1201 of the DMCA is and how it ties into this conversation and makes it a little bit more fraught to do that unlocking?
Section 1201 of the DMCA makes it illegal to bypass a technical protection measure, otherwise known as a digital lock, to gain access to a protected work, even if it’s for the purpose of repair. It was put in place to try and stop piracy, and it was put together in one of the most corrupt ways possible.
Congress repeatedly said no to this law. No, this is way too restrictive. So the architect of it, Bruce Lehman, decided to try and get it negotiated into an international treaty. Then he came back to the United States and said, “Look, we signed this treaty, so now you have to pass this law. We’re going to be out of step with our obligations to the international community.” So he’s potentially sidestepped Congress in order to get this law passed that says that you cannot bypass a digital lock, even if it’s on something that you own.
Now, I can’t go to your house and break into your home. That’s illegal. I should not be allowed to do that. But if I spend $600,000 on a house, and I notice that there’s a locked closet in the house, once I’ve paid $600,000 for the house, I should be able to break that lock to get into that closet, because I own the house.
The problem with this law is that it allows companies to lock you out of things that you have already paid for. And the way that you see this being abused now is when you see companies taking away features from you or sending out firmware updates that stop features from working on the device after the company has gone bankrupt and they figure out, “Oh, wait, we need more money.”
I’ve had companies that failed before, and I really wish I had the ability to say, “You know what? I realize that I sold it at a price that’s a little too low. I’m gonna need to take $5 from every customer I had in the past year.” I’m not allowed to do that. I can’t stop my customers’ products from working.
But because I was selling a physical good that did not have any sort of firmware on it that connects to the internet, I’m not able to do that. Section 1201 of the DMCA is a law that came out in the late 1990s. This was before most normal people even had the internet, much less had their washing machine, their television, their dryer attached to the internet like they are now.
