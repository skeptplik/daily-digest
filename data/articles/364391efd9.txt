North Korea has declared Switzerland's top diplomatic representative in Pyongyang persona non grata and ordered him to leave the country within 48 hours, state media reported on Wednesday.
The North Korean Foreign Ministry also called for the closure of Switzerland's diplomatic office in the capital, less than a month after it reopened following a lengthy shutdown during the COVID-19 pandemic.
According to the state-run Korean Central News Agency (KCNA), Pak Hyon Su, director general of the ministry's European Department, summoned Swiss diplomat Sylvain Astier and handed him a memorandum demanding the office's closure.
Pyongyang said the decision was in response to Switzerland's sanctions against a North Korean children's camp, describing the move as an openly hostile act.
"Any act of violating the sovereign rights of the DPRK and the interests of its citizens can never be welcomed by us," KCNA reported, using the acronym for North Korea's official name.
Why did Switzerland impose sanctions on North Korea?
Switzerland placed the Songdowon International Children's Camp in the coastal city of Wonsan under sanctions on November 4.
Swiss authorities accused the camp, which also hosts Chinese and Russian students, of participating in the "transfer of Ukrainian children from temporarily occupied territories of Ukraine" to North Korea.
They also said the facility contributed to "efforts aimed at the ideological conditioning and russification of Ukrainian children".
On Tuesday, Kim Yo Jong, the influential sister of North Korean leader Kim Jong Un, condemned the sanctions arguing that the measures violated North Korea's sovereignty by interfering with its responsibility to provide children with cultural and recreational opportunities.
How has Switzerland responded to North Korea's decision?
Switzerland's Foreign Ministry said on Tuesday that reopening its diplomatic office was independent of its sanctions policy.
"The reopening of the Swiss diplomatic office in Pyongyang is in line with our universalist foreign policy. Its aim is to maintain channels of dialogue, including with states with which we have differences on certain issues," the ministry said.
It stressed that this approach applied regardless of Switzerland's participation in UN sanctions over North Korea's nuclear program or EU sanctions related to Russia's war against Ukraine.
Swiss authorities were not immediately available for further comment on Wednesday.
Switzerland has maintained diplomatic relations with North Korea since 1974 and has long served as an intermediary between Pyongyang and Western countries.
Its office in the North Korean capital closed during the COVID-19 pandemic in 2020. Swiss Foreign Minister Ignazio Cassis formally reopened it during a visit in October, when he discussed normalizing relations.
It was the first visit by a Swiss government minister to North Korea since 2003.
The office operates under the Swiss Embassy in Beijing, which handles diplomatic and consular relations with North Korea. Pyongyang also maintains an embassy in Bern.
Only a few European countries have diplomatic missions in North Korea, including Sweden, Poland and the Czech Republic.
What does North Korea's move mean for relations with the West?
Experts say the expulsion is intended as a warning to other Western countries, including the United States, which has been seeking to reestablish contact with Pyongyang.
"North Korea is using Switzerland as a test case to show that sanctions and dialogue are incompatible," Lim Eul-chul, a professor at South Korea's Kyungnam University, told news agency Reuters.
Ahn Chan-il, a researcher originally from North Korea, told agency AFP that Pyongyang, backed by its allies China and Russia, wanted to make an example of Switzerland.
Earlier this week, North Korean leader Kim Jong Un personally received credentials from the new ambassadors of 10 countries, including Russia and Sweden, in a rare diplomatic ceremony.
Kim said Pyongyang was seeking to expand relations with countries that respected its sovereignty but would oppose attempts to restrict the rights and development of sovereign states.
