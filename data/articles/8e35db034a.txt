Jacques Cavallier-Belletrud, Louis Vuitton‘s master perfumer and the fifth generation of a Grasse perfume family, grew up with oud as a family story. His father smelled it for the first time in the 1960s. It wasn’t the real oil, but it was powerful enough to stay with him, and for the young perfumer, oud became a fantasy he could only imagine.
	The fantasy became a material in Saudi Arabia, where oud specialist Abdul Samad Al Qurashi took Cavallier-Belletrud to a factory near Mecca and walked him through how real oud is produced. Louis Vuitton’s master perfumer has been building on that lesson ever since. Today he says the house is the world’s leading buyer of oud for perfumery.
His first trip to the region, through Oman and Saudi Arabia, came three decades ago, the same span as the trade show that now anchors the Middle East beauty calendar. Last year, Beauty World Dubai honored Cavallier-Belletrud with its Lifetime Achiever Award, accepted on his behalf by the mayor of his hometown, Grasse. This year he attended in person for the fair’s 30th edition.
Cavallier-Belletrud sits down with Beauty Inc for an in-depth discussion about the clichés he hears about the region’s taste, why he rethought the Extrait and what the show’s 30 years mean to him.
You’ve been coming to the region for more than 30 years. What has stayed with you?
Jacques Cavallier-Belletrud: I first came to the region through Oman and Saudi Arabia, and then Dubai. Over the years, I’ve seen how deeply fragrance is part of life here. People grow up with it; it’s connected to family, culture and tradition. Perfume isn’t simply an accessory or a commodity. It can carry meaning and express something about you, even when it’s difficult to find the words.
You’ve said you want to challenge clichés about fragrance in the Middle East. What do people get wrong?
J.C-B.: There’s a cliché that people here only like oud or very powerful fragrances. That’s not true. People here appreciate good perfume, and they can love freshness as well as richer scents. The important thing is quality: A fragrance should last, but it should also be harmonious and not overwhelm the people around you. Dubai is one of the best places to meet people who are knowledgeable and passionate about perfume.
What does a “good perfume” mean to you?
J.C-B.: It has to carry a meaning and a philosophy. The concept is important, but the fragrance itself matters too: the materials, the balance and what you feel when you wear it. Especially in a culture where fragrance has been part of everyday life for generations, it can become a way of expressing yourself without speaking. That depth is something I admire.
How did your interest in oud begin?
J.C-B.: I was fascinated by oud before natural extracts were available to me. I used to imagine what it smelled like. Then, around 22 years ago, I met Abdul Samad Al Qurashi in Saudi Arabia. He was a remarkable specialist, and he introduced me to the secrets of oud. When I discovered it at a factory near Mecca, I understood how complex, mysterious and beautiful it was. I knew I wanted to work with it.
How has that relationship with oud shaped your work at Louis Vuitton?
J.C-B.: My interest came from a genuine love for the material, not from a commercial calculation. Commercial success is a consequence of what you create. At Louis Vuitton, we have been able to work with oud extensively, and we are the world’s leading buyer of it for perfumery. I also wanted to bring the inspiration of Middle Eastern perfumery into our work as a tribute, rather than present it as something offered to the region from outside.
You’ve described Ombre Nomade as a tribute to the region. What did you hope to capture?
J.C-B.: I wanted to capture something of the style and heritage of perfumery in the Middle East and bring that inspiration into the world of Louis Vuitton. Around 70 percent of its clients are from outside the region, which is fascinating to me. It suggests that an idea rooted in a particular place can speak to people across the world.
You’ve also rethought the Extrait. What did you want to do differently?
J.C-B.: I didn’t want to make stronger versions of fragrances that already existed. Those perfumes were complete as they were. Instead, I wanted each Extrait to explore its own olfactory family and character. The challenge is to create something with presence and lasting power while keeping it in harmony. A fragrance can be powerful and long-lasting without being too much.
How are fragrance consumers changing?
J.C-B.: There are so many launches that it’s difficult for people to follow everything. But consumers, particularly a new generation, are learning through social media and becoming more demanding. They want to understand what’s in the bottle and why. That’s good news for people who care about the quality of their ingredients and the thought behind a fragrance. For a luxury house, it’s a pleasure to share that knowledge with clients.
As Beauty World Dubai marks 30 years, what makes the event meaningful to you?
J.C-B.: It brings together people who create fragrances, produce raw materials and work across beauty. Dubai is a natural meeting point, but the event’s importance goes beyond business. Beauty and perfumery are connected to well-being, freedom, love, friendship and humanity. Beauty World Dubai has understood the importance of bringing these people together, and its influence is now international. That exchange of ideas and creativity is what makes the gathering so special.
