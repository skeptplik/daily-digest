Nostalgia and nihilism collide in David Fincher's gritty but edgeless follow-up to Quentin Tarantino's 'Once Upon a Time in Hollywood'.
The year is 1977, and the movies ain’t what they used to be.
Over the last decade, a New Wave of young filmmakers have taken over, moving from indoor sets to city streets to film grittier, meaner, counter-cultural stories that reflect the disillusionment of the times.
In the process, Hollywood’s prior artifice has been shattered; no longer a glossy, well-oiled machine solely controlled by big studios.
But nothing lasts forever — and something is about to come along (as it always does) that once again changes everything: Star Wars: Episode IV - A New Hope.
As screenwriter and filmmaker Paul Schrader told film historian Peter Biskind for his book "Easy Riders and Raging Bulls": “Star Wars was the film that ate the heart and the soul of Hollywood. It created the big-budget comic book mentality.”
The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth (TFMCB) takes place in the summer of ‘77, right at the height of Star Wars mania. While only glimpsed on cinema marquees in passing shots, its background presence sets the tone for a movie that’s not only wrestling with shifting attitudes and the violent tug of time, but also itself.
A follow up to Quentin Tarantino’s Oscar-winning Once Upon a Time in Hollywood (2019), TFMCB focuses on its character Cliff Booth (Brad Pitt), the former stuntman to actor Rick Dalton (Leonardo DiCaprio) who once kicked Bruce Lee’s a** and took down a bunch of Manson Family members while high on an acid soaked cigarette.
In other words, Cliff is cool. That's kind of his whole thing.
Eight years later, he’s living with his dad (Peter Weller) and working multiple jobs in Los Angeles: a projectionist at the New Beverley Cinema, a cocktail maker at a bar with mud fighting contests, and a “fixer” for celebrities being blackmailed by criminals.
Oh, and he also sometimes acts in porno movies under the stag name Randy.
The plot mostly hinges around Cliff’s relationship with an excitable TV actor named Charlie Peters (Scott Caan), who finds himself in a spot of trouble with some Filipino gangsters that are now threatening to kill his wife (Carla Gugino).
But mis-adventures aside,TFMCB is primarily a hangout movie.
Similar to George Lucas's American Graffiti, it excels at immersing viewers in retro Americana — albeit a crumpled and nonchalant version. As we ride with Cliff through the streets of LA, the staticky sounds of KRLA radio station a near-constant presence, you can almost smell the tobacco smoke wafting through the screen and it's just fun soaking up the atmosphere, slouching into it like a weathered chair.
Booth himself is as engaging as ever and manages to maintain some of his enigma despite being front and centre. This is thanks to Pitt's endearing performance, which once again leans into the character's effortless bad-a**ery — part surfer-dude, part grindhouse hunk.
But if Once upon a Time was Tarantino’s ode to a golden age of cinema - a re-writing of history with a fairy tale ending - TFMCB is a cynical reckoning with reality and the inevitability of change.
While Cliff confronts this by coasting on his own legends and clinging to the fringes of an increasingly loose-moralled industry, he knows no happy ending is coming — and who cares? None of it “means sh**” to him anyway.
Director David Fincher (Seven, Gone Girl) was the perfect choice for capturing such apathy, dipping Tarantino’s script in a vat of slick gloom that sharpens and darkens the mood. And it is at points very dark — both in its on-screen brutalities, and its visuals, which swap Once upon a Time's vibrant 35mm film for the dirge of desaturated digital.
Still, the nostalgic vibes of its predecessor remain, and anyone with an interest in this particular era of Hollywood filmmaking history will find themselves drawn to its niche cultural references and rich world building; scenes bathed in neon sleaze and analogue media ASMR.
Beyond the movie’s cool persona, however, there’s not much substance to hold onto.
Cliff Booth is a great character, but what made him so beloved in Once upon a Time was his bond with Rick specifically and his interactions with other equally-charismatic characters. In TFMCB, there are very few interesting characters to anchor him in the same way. The criminals are bothersome but bland, Peter Weller as Gus Booth is woefully underused, and TV star Charlie Peters is eccentric in an oddly underwhelming way.
The most compelling on-screen dynamic is between Cliff and the glamazonian Hollywood agent Karen Crosby (Elizabeth Debicki), who share a memorable scene on a balcony discussing the terms and conditions of Cliff’s slapping kink. But the chemistry between the two tends to simmer more than sizzle, and Crosby, outside of a fun speech about movies being like cheeseburgers, is mostly relegated to solemn looks and the occasional burst of sass.
Meanwhile, a lack of overall narrative purpose means that Cliff meanders from moment to moment, which can be compelling in its own way, but lessens the impact of any actual action. Even the tensest plot points, like a violent home invasion, start to feel like disruptive filler (can't we just get back to making banana daiquiris?)
TFMCB alsomarks the final movie to be made as part of Fincher’s exclusive partnership with Netflix (following Mank and The Killer), with only a brief two-week theatrical release in select cinemas before streaming. This, along with Fincher’s admitted use of some artificial intelligence in post-production, create a notable flatness to its aesthetic; one that also examples the very thing it laments: the slow degradation of creative spirit to time.
This appears on screen like a literal push-pull between nostalgia and nihilism that ultimately flicks a lit cigarette on Tarantino's celluloid romanticisms.
It once again raises questions about the current state of cinema. About its profit-driven and streamer-led decision making that has created a more homogenised and sanitised media landscape where original, renegade ideas (like those of New Hollywood) are stifled and pre-existing ones mined of their spark.
But if we take Crosley's view that movies are "just meant to be fun", TFMCB works as an entertaining couple of hours spent with a former stuntman in a Hawaiian shirt — the Fincher with fries and a side of Tarantino dipping sauce.
The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth is out at select European cinemas now ahead of its Netflix release on 23 December 2027.
