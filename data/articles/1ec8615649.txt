International health officials said Tuesday that measles is no longer considered eliminated in the United States, a finding that was widely expected but that marks an embarrassing and avoidable backslide in the country’s basic public health standing.
The announcement, made after a meeting of a committee of the Pan American Health Organization, a regional World Health Organization body, is a largely symbolic recognition of transmission that’s already occurred. But it could have political ramifications, with resurgent Democrats already attacking the Trump administration — in particular, health secretary Robert F. Kennedy Jr. — for not responding forcefully to recent outbreaks and for mixed messaging around the benefits of vaccination.
Health authorities in 2000 determined that the U.S. had eliminated measles, a milestone victory. But the country almost lost the designation after outbreaks in 2019, and has been experiencing far bigger outbreaks in the past two years than in the decades prior.
“Losing measles elimination status is a tragic loss for our country,” Susan Kansagra, chief medical officer for the Association of State and Territorial Health Officials, told reporters Monday. The announcement formalizes what public health departments have been seeing on the ground across the U.S. in recent years, she said. “Measles has taken a hold in our country.”
Officials bestow the measles elimination designation on a country that prevents persistent spread of the virus. Cases can still occur, when people infected outside that country import the virus, and limited transmission can take place from those infected people. But if those chains of spread are not stopped within a year, measles is considered to be endemic again.
“Elimination must be continuously protected,” Jarbas Barbosa, the PAHO director, said at a news briefing.
This year, the U.S. has reported more than 5,000 measles cases for the first time since 1991, a figure that’s widely presumed to be an undercount. Last year’s total of 2,289 cases was at that point also the highest number seen since 1991, according to data from the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention. The overwhelming majority of infections — 96% — are in unvaccinated people or people whose vaccination status is not known.
The PAHO commission’s review focused on a period starting in January 2025, and depended on establishing that a series of cases continued for at least 12 months.
“It is unconscionable that we have this amount of measles,” said Noel Brewer, a public health expert at the University of North Carolina, who chairs the group that provides data on the measles landscape in the U.S. to PAHO.
“We need a plan,” Brewer said last week during a briefing for reporters. “We need to have a concrete and integrated measles elimination plan that allows us to either sustain the gains we’ve made in the past or to return to a state where measles is no longer stalking children in their schools, in grocery stores, and in their churches. The United States is no longer a safe place for children with respect to measles. It is not as prevalent as some other diseases. At the same time, it is a fully preventable disease.”
The U.S. has seen 10 measles-associated deaths this year, according to state data, though the CDC’s website currently lists six deaths. Some recent deaths reported by states have yet to be added to the CDC’s count, but federal officials have also squabbled with Pennsylvania over its tally. New York and Wisconsin recently reported their first measles deaths in at least 30 years. Last year, there were three measles deaths.
Experts fear that measles deaths — practically unheard of in the U.S. for over 20 years — could become regular events going forward, even if they are not expected to occur nearly as frequently as in the pre-vaccination era, when some 400 to 500 people died every year from infections.
Doctors are also reporting an increase in complications — both maternal and neonatal — from measles infections in pregnancy.
In a statement Tuesday, CDC Director Erica Schwartz said the U.S. is “focused on stopping outbreaks.”
“The measles elimination designation is not what protects Americans. Vaccination does,” she said. “We know where transmission is occurring and we’ve invested $100 million in state and local health departments to reach those communities,” she added, referencing funding the CDC has recently distributed to areas facing outbreaks.
The CDC recommends that children receive two doses of the measles, mumps, and rubella vaccine, with the first shot at 12 months and the second at 4 to 6 years. Some doctors offer the first dose to younger babies if they live in an area where the virus is spreading. The CDC’s statement Tuesday said “we will encourage MMR vaccination, expand access to vaccines, and help communities close gaps in protection.”
While Kennedy has at times acknowledged that the best way to prevent measles infections and spread is the MMR shot, including in a social media post Tuesday, he also has a long history of attacking vaccines with false arguments. As health secretary, he has downplayed the severity of outbreaks, overstated the risks of the vaccine, and pointed to unproven treatments as possible solutions for infections.
Public health experts have criticized the federal government for not doing more to address measles even as infections mounted and as it became clear in recent months that the country’s elimination status was well on its way to being lost. On Tuesday, Sen. Michael Bennet (D-Colo.) called on Schwartz to resign after the PAHO decision was announced.
“The fact that the flag was raised, and it was not heeded, is to me the most alarming thing,” said Maia Majumder, a computational epidemiologist at Boston Children’s Hospital, who co-authored a Lancet paper in May about the at-risk elimination designation. U.S. health officials are “not interested in doing the things that are necessary to maintain our measles elimination status,” she told STAT.
Kennedy, meanwhile, has argued that other countries have also seen more measles cases.
Canada lost its elimination status last year, and PAHO’s reverification commission also determined this week that measles had returned to endemicity in Mexico and Bolivia. A number of European countries — including the U.K. and Spain — have also lost their designation.
The U.S.’s susceptibility to measles has been building for years. Vaccination rates within certain regions and communities have slipped for any number of reasons: a rise in vaccine skepticism, the spread of misinformation online, failures of public health campaigns, or the perception that measles doesn’t pose a threat. Adults have made up sizable portions of infections in some recent case clusters, a sign that it’s not just a nascent retrenchment of parents not immunizing their children that’s allowing for transmission.
But for a long time, unvaccinated people were generally protected from the highly contagious virus because they were surrounded by so many others who were vaccinated, increasing the chances that any burgeoning outbreaks would be quickly snuffed out. (The CDC defines an outbreak as three or more linked cases.) Now, though, pockets of susceptible people, both adults and children, have grown so large that the virus can circulate among them for extended periods. Plus, more measles spread in other countries increases the likelihood of imported cases.
While vaccine coverage of 95% of people is considered sufficient to keep a lid on measles transmission, the vaccination rates for kindergarteners has dropped below 90% in nearly 20 states. Looking at local levels can reveal alarmingly low vaccination rates within individual counties and school districts.
Brewer noted that the CDC has quietly been supporting states in their outbreak responses, including with the recent funding. But the fact that top U.S. officials, whether Kennedy or Schwartz, aren’t regularly encouraging vaccination, as would’ve happened under previous administrations, including the first Trump presidency, has created a vacuum for rhetoric that undermines vaccination to fill.
“We need leadership to step up,” he said.
