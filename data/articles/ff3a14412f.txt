The United States has announced new regulations aimed at curbing Chinese firms’ remote access to advanced AI chips through data centres in third party countries, coming amid the scandal surrounding chipmaker Nvidia. Beijing on Tuesday (November 17, 2026) slammed the new restrictions, accusing Washington of “abusing export control measures”.
Nvidia’s provision of chips to companies running data centres in Southeast Asia has come under the spotlight amid questions surrounding founder Jensen Huang’s relationship with Alice Huang, the CEO of one such Singapore-based company, Megaspeed. With Nvidia barred from selling its most advanced chips in China, many Chinese companies instead sought access to its technology through data centres in countries such as Malaysia.
The U.S. Commerce Department’s Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) announced an interim final rule (IFR) “to address remote access” to advanced AI chips and servers, saying it was “designed to ensure that existing restrictions on access to sensitive American technology cannot be circumvented through remote computing arrangements involving data centres in third countries.”
The new rule was announced days before President Donald Trump’s arrival in Shenzhen for meetings with President Xi Jinping on the sidelines of the November 18-19 APEC Summit, which will also see a rare trilateral leaders’ summit along with Russian President Vladimir Putin.
The rule states that in addition to the current license requirements for advanced semiconductors, a license will be required “to export, reexport, or transfer (in-country) advanced semiconductors to Indonesia, Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand, or Brazil”, which it named as “countries which present a higher-than-normal risk of diversion.”

China hit out at the moves, calling it “yet another case of the U.S. overstretching the concept of national security, and abusing export control measures to interfere in normal trade and tech cooperation between China and other countries and go after China’s high-tech industry.”
“Such typical economic coercion and long-arm jurisdiction run counter to the common understandings between the two countries, harm the legitimate rights and interests of Chinese companies, and disrupt global supply chains,” Foreign Ministry spokesperson Guo Jiakun said. “China will do what is necessary to firmly safeguard its legitimate and lawful rights and interests.”
China’s Ministry of Commerce in a statement said the new rule would affect the stability of the global semiconductor industry, adding that the U.S. was “misusing export controls to interfere indiscriminately in normal trade cooperation between Chinese enterprises and companies from other countries.” “The U.S. approach runs counter to the important consensus reached by the two heads of state, undermines market rules and the international economic and trade order, and affects the stability of the global semiconductor industry and supply chain,” the statement said.
