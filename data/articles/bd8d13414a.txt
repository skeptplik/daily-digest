There’s a bell ringing in the Underground.
The copper-line page phone in Shaft #4 is going off, but it takes a minute for any of the miners to notice. They’re too busy dealing with a tunnel collapse and subsequent break in the geothermal line servicing Sectors 51–57.
It’s late morning, as measured by the water clocks and those who swear by them. It’s midnight, as determined by the Moon and stars, cavorting a few thousand feet above their heads. But the oldest miner on this crew is 60, which is about 30 years too young to remember stars or moons, or appreciate the difference between morning and night.
This is one reason why the Mona Lisa Lottery, so loved by the old-timers, fails to stir up the same excitement it used to. Too intent on repairs, the miners forget that the annual drawing is scheduled for today.
Eventually, the ringing registers and curiosity calls them away from their work. Headlamps bob up and down in the dark. The sound of axes and drills drops off sharply, save for one stubborn miner still working, right below a rock face chiselled with the encouraging-but-long-false words: This Way Out.
They gather round the ringer box and the foreman hushes them before pressing the phone tight to one ear, cupping his free hand over the other. Every noise echoes tenfold in the tunnels, and the foreman has to ask the voice on the other end to repeat themselves.
“Abel?” the foreman repeats. “Are you sure?”
A few miners huff wry laughs at the name, muttering, “Of course it’s him.”
The foreman looks around, failing to find the lucky winner among his workers, “Well, where is he?”
A couple outstretched thumbs and jerks of the head give the foreman his answer, unmistakably directed towards the only pickaxe still singing in the dark.
****
No one can opt out of the Mona Lisa Lottery. No one can give away their ticket to someone else.
But Abel’s tempted to decline the honour. He’s got work to do. And climbing up 2,000 feet to wait in line with the other winners for hours, all to stare at a square 30-by-21 inches for 5 whole minutes, doesn’t seem like a good use of his time.
It’s only Adèle, the old woman in the cave next door, who convinces him to make the trek upstairs.
Adèle was ten when the evacuations happened. She’s now two months shy of being a proper centenarian. Her mind isn’t as sharp as it used to be. She can’t remember what she had for breakfast, but she remembers the horrors they descended from well enough, all those lines of desperate people sent down below with canned goods, heirloom seeds and not much else.
The treasures preserved from the Old World are few and Abel’s been given the chance to see one of the last. Arguably, the best. If she wasn’t blind as a bat, Adèle might kill him for his ticket. If she didn’t love him like a son, that is.
“Don’t be a fool,” she tells him plainly. “Go see what you’ve been missing.”
****
The journey to the higher levels snakes up ladders and through tight squeezes, skirting the underbelly of the Safe Zone. There’s a breath of strange breeze up here, not conjured by any air machines. It makes some winners too nervous and they abandon the wait, rushing back down the way they came.
Abel’s not afraid. But he still thinks it’s a waste of time.
Adèle warned him that the wait for the Mona Lisa was just like this. When she was eight, her family waited for two hours just to get a glimpse. It doesn’t make sense to him. He doesn’t understand how a mere painting could be worth the trouble.
He’ll never know. Because that’s not what the Mona Lisa Lottery is all about. Despite the name, Abel’s not queuing for oil paint on a poplar panel.
The Mona Lisa is not in the Underground. They couldn’t waste the limited space. Leonardo’s lady still sits in the crumbling ruins of her museum gallery, her half-smile gone grimmer these days. She’s lonely without visitors. A wild boar passed her way a decade before, searching for truffles in the jungles of the overgrown Louvre. No one’s been by since.
She sighs into her patient smile; she waits with folded hands. Ever the optimist, she thinks her friends may return someday.
