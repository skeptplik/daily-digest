Russia resumes attacks, with fortified positions, after Oleshky ceasefire
Luke Harding
The fake nuclear threats to Ukraine came as Russia resumed its attacks on the southern city of Kherson, just hours after the end of a ceasefire during which hundreds of people were evacuated from the nearby Kremlin-controlled town of Oleshky.
Kherson’s military administration said a Russian drone hit a hospital at 7.40am. Video footage showed wrecked rooms, with windows blown out, doors broken, and debris on the street. Nobody was hurt, officials said.
There is evidence that the Russian army used the six-day ceasefire to strengthen its positions in Oleskhy and to rotate in new troops. Bodies of dead civilians were also removed from a hospital morgue.
Footage shared with Sky News showed Russian soldiers deploying heavy military equipment and delivering ammunition. They carried large bags into burned out houses and buildings and disappeared into basements, the broadcaster reported.
Armoured vehicles were spotted moving around Oleshky’s streets. The occupied city is 3 miles from Kherson, on the opposite bank of the Dnipro River. Before last week’s truce, resupply was difficult because of the threat from Ukrainian kamikaze drones.
According to the commander of Ukraine’s 34th Separate Marine Brigade, Dmitry Pulinets, Russian soldiers were previously unable to drive in the city, “neither during the day, nor at night, nor in bad weather”.
“Now they have this opportunity. And for the last few days this movement has been happening constantly,” he told Sky News.
The ceasefire expired at 11pm on Sunday. Both sides hailed the local pause in the fighting – the most significant since 2022 – as a success. Ukraine’s ombudsman, Dmytro Lubinets, who visited Oleshky to coordinate the mission, said it could be repeated on other parts of the frontline.
However, Vladimir Putin’s spokesperson Dmitry Peskov said on Monday there were no plans for another truce elsewhere. He said it was “standard practice” for army units to regroup in “such circumstances” and added that he assumed Ukraine’s armed forces had “done the same”.
'I was briefly terrified'- Kyiv residents on fake nuclear warning
Luke Harding
My colleague, Luke Harding in Kyiv, has also filed this dispatch from the Ukrainian capital where residents received those fake alerts last night, saying they were at risk of an imminent nuclear strike.
Many residents ignored the alerts, which began shortly before 11pm local time, assuming that the app had been compromised.
Others were scared. “I was briefly terrified,” one woman, Nina, said. “I lay on my bed and wondered what was coming.”
The app reported a high possibility of nuclear radiation late on Sunday. It also wrongly warned of a chemical threat to the Ukrainian capital and street-to-street fighting. The messages were written in English with spelling mistakes, as well as in Ukrainian.
A message written in Russian also claimed Ukraine was about to be hit by Oreshnik missiles, which can carry nuclear warheads. The Kremlin has used the ballistic weapon three times before, hitting the Kyiv region, and the cities of Dnipro and Lviv.
It was unclear on Monday who might have carried out the cyber-raid, or why. Russian officials claimed hackers had also sent fake emergency alerts there after gaining access to the app of the ministry for emergencies, based in Moscow.
One of their posts mentioned the hacking group Hitcore. Mash, a Russian Telegram channel with links to the security services, said it had spoken to representatives of Hitcore and that they had claimed responsibility for the attack. The report could not be verified.
My colleague, Jon Henley, is covering this latest wave of supermarket fridge cyber-attacks.
He notes that in the France attacks last month, hackers had remotely overridden the settings on thousands of freezers, fridges and chiller cabinets, pushing them from as low as -27C to well over 50C.
Stores had to discard vast quantities of fresh meat, dairy and frozen products, with many suffering losses ranging from tens of thousands of euros to more than €130,000 (£109,000). Two stores in Alsace reported a combined loss of €270,000.
The Intermarché, Coopérative U, Carrefour, Leclerc and Monoprix chains were among those affected.
Most supermarket cold aisles are connected to the internet to allow remote maintenance and alerts for system failures.
In the French attack, the hackers pirated controllers mostly supplied by the Danish company Danfoss. They disabled alarm systems before adjusting the thermostats or extending the defrost programme by hours.

'Disinformation as much a part of war as bombs and bullets'
Frances Mao
Back to the cyber warfare we saw last night in the app hacking in Ukraine, and then Russia.
As I noted earlier, this was direct attack on a core channel of communications between Ukrainian authorities and citizens; this app is embedded in everyday life in a war zone.
And while the full impact of the hack is still unclear – how many Ukrainians were deceived and believed the warnings for example - residents nonetheless have described how alarming it was getting a message on their phone warning of potential nuclear warfare.
Cyber-warfare expert Don Smith, from IT security firm Sophos, says these sorts of attacks are aimed at grounding down people’s mental toughness.
Disinformation is as much a part of war as bombs and bullets. Targeted information attacks could aim to impact the mental resilience of the people of Ukraine.
Their resilience is a key part of their ability to defend against the Russian military.
However he noted the ameaturish nature of this attack. One of the message sent out, in Russian, was almost jokey and casual in tone, reading: “Hi! There will be a nuclear alert across Ukraine. Don’t worry, it’s Oreshnik flying”, a reference to Russia’s hypersonic missiles which can pretzel and outpace most interceptors.
Usually the best information warfare is not obvious or explicit, where this very clearly is. It could indicate desperation from Russia.

Ukraine and Russia have reported corresponding cyber-hacks on their app warning systems overnight.
These are the mobile apps which send alerts and air-raid warnings, alerting citizens to when missiles and drones are incoming (and thus advising residents to seek shelter or go down to a bunker).
This appears to be the first breach of the app, on such a scale, during the war so far; directly immobilising one of the most important channels of communication authorities have to help advise citizens.

Ukraine’s Air Force issued clarification within the hour on Telegram, urging people to remain calm and disregard the messages, one of which was written in Russian.
Some hours later, Russian authorities said its warning app had been hacked and had to be taken offline. A Ukrainian hacker group has claimed to have sent fake alerts warning of nuclear warheads to users inside Russia.
