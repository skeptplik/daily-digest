Spoilers for Chapter 1-3 of GTA 6 follow.
I have just successfully busted a Kingfisher operation just north of Cottonmouth Lake in Mariana County. It was beautiful. Jason took point, armed with a shotgun liberated from an M7 clubhouse, guarding a chokepoint as hordes of rednecks threw themselves at him in an unceremonious, slightly humiliating pantomime of existential futility. Lucia, confident in her improving gunmanship, stood behind, aimed over Jason's broad shoulders, and took out stragglers with a custom hunting rifle pilfered from the Kingfisher's own safehouse. Not so safe anymore, is it, losers? This one's for Donnie, I think as I kneecap the final goon and rifle through their safe.
"That one was for Donnie," Lucia says to Jason as they get in their favourite car - Jason's sunset yellow Declasse '72 Tulip, with a suped up engine and custom parts. God damn, I think, this game can read my mind. And I'm not sure if I would have had this intimate little moment of grief and reconciliation if I hadn't got in the habit of using the game's Cinematic Camera. Your dual protagonists can chat even when you, the player, are in the driving seat - of course - but there's so much going on in the world of Leonida (and so many pedestrians and motorists that like to behave like maniacs and cut you off) that I find their conversations are often cut short or terminated altogether by the chaos of the world.
So, hopping into the car, hitting 'down' on the D-Pad and then hitting 'Start' will instigate the Cinematic Camera, and if you've already set a destination on the map or via the handy new quick navigational menu (simply hold 'down' to see this), your chosen lovebird will start automatically driving to their next location. For me, in this example, it was the Pelican River to Vice City's Bayside, and Boobie's apartment. Probably about 15 minutes of real-world time; enough space to fuss my dog, grab a drink, reply to my editor, and really take the time to soak in the absurd level of detail Rockstar has crammed into its opus. On this specific journey, I also heard a really touching, sweet interaction about Jason's insecurities regarding Lucai's mum. Goosebumps.
GTA 6 is an action-packed game; there aren't a lot of menial jobs going for Jason and Lucia - they're on the run, after all, so I don't think a traditional PAYE-compliant nine-to-five is really on the cards. As such, even the smaller money-making activities on offer are quite stressful: dirt-track events, street racing, pawn store robberies, hit-and-runs, stick-ups… there's only so much a bit of meditation in a park can do to decompress you. So I've taken to enjoying life on the roads. After a big, somewhat impromptu heist on two Kingfisher operations, I need a bit of time to slow down and smell the proverbial roses (or should that be Tulips?).
Jumping into Jason's car, turning the radio to The Chamber 106.6, and letting the star-crossed lovers meander their way home listening to Slayer's Kerry King and Tom Araya play a selection of metal tracks from the past four decades is good for my brain. I actually think these moments of leisure are essential to the rhythm of GTA 6: dial it up, cool it off. It creates this almost tidal ebb and flow, the resultant flotsam and jetsam ending up fenced or stashed as the rhythm dictates.
I'm usually a very active and fussy player when it comes to games; I can't stand long cutscenes taking control away from me, and I absolutely will skip story scenes on a second playthrough if it's getting in the way of hands-on controller time. So I'm quite surprised at the ease with which I've given up my agency for these longer rides. But it's a testament to Rockstar's writing. I want to see what Jason and Lucia are saying to each other. I want the camera to show me the world passing me by outside my window as I get from A to B. I even sometimes schedule and phase in little detours to C and D, to be honest, because I feel so zen as I watch the bruise-coloured sky sets over the Keys (and I want to hear the end of Symphony of Destruction, maybe).
My Lucia is dressed a bit like someone you'll see cutting about outside Nottingham's Rock City club - cute little tank top, leather jacket, nose and ear piercings galore. One afternoon, after driving out to Hamlet to have a long overdue catch-up with her mum, I set the Cinematic Camera and auto-drive settings on to take her back to meet Jason at a bar (another place, incidentally, you can turn on the Cinematic Camera and just watch them hang out together). On her way back home, with the sunlight dipping below the horizon, the fiery orange ambience caught the whites of her eye and the metallic zip of her leather jacket. She smiled to herself as Tool's Sober came on the radio. Christ, I thought, I'm there.
GTA 6 has this odd capacity to transport me. I think it's because the levels of simulation in Leonida are so artisanal, so well-pitched, and so well-executed. I can believe I'm there. I want to sit in this muscle car, no doubt smelling of leather and oil, listening to Tool with Lucia. I want to feel the humid air of Watson Bay coalescing on my skin, warm air blowing through my hair. I want to join in on Jason and Lucia's in-jokes, laugh as they do little impressions of each other and coo as they make up and hold hands during the longer drives. I want to stick my head out the window like Lucia does and smell the Cuban food grilling in the food trucks, or hear the kids partying on the beach.
With the Cinematic Camera turned on, I have the chance to do that. I don't have to worry about making money, paying back favours I owe, checking in with Brian, topping up my BuckMe account. I can just engage in this fantasy - this simulation - Rockstar has built for me. And in these moments where I take my hands off the wheel, hang out with these people I've dressed up and ride around in the car I've built, it really does feel like it's been made just for me. I can't remember the last time a game made me feel that way.
