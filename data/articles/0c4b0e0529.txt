Reality star Sam Wood will spend Christmas in jail after again being denied bail on domestic violence charges over allegations he strangled and assaulted his wife while the family was holidaying at Noosa on the Sunshine Coast in October.
The court of appeal today heard a third application by Wood, 46, for bail. He has spent eight weeks in custody since being arrested.
Wood is accused of assaulting his wife, Snezana, whom he met on Network Ten’s hit reality show The Bachelor in 2015.
Wood is charged with two domestic violence offences: assault occasioning bodily harm and strangulation in a domestic setting. The strangulation charge had placed Wood in a “show cause” position where there was a presumption against bail unless he could demonstrate he did not pose a risk of reoffending.
His legal team has indicated he will fight the charges and he has not yet entered a plea.
Queensland appeal justice David Boddice on Friday delivered the court’s judgment on the bail appeal.
“The appeal is dismissed,” Boddice said.
Boddice found that a phone call from prison between Wood and his father provided “a sound evidentiary basis” to conclude that, if Wood were granted bail, there was “a real risk that he would seek to interfere with witnesses, namely [his wife, Snezana]”.
Boddice was referring to a recording of that conversation, played in court, in which an “obviously distressed” Wood was sobbing as he told his father that he needed to talk to Snezana, and that his domestic violence charges would be dropped if she recanted her statements to police.
“I need to be able to talk to my wife,” Wood said in the recording. “There is no way she would want this.”
After Wood’s father responds by saying he did not believe it would be so simple, as police were pressing charges, Wood replied that police would not “have a leg to stand on” if his wife changed her statement and made “a stat dec to say this actually did not happen”.
“[H]is express words are consistent with an attempt to indirectly contact his wife for the express purpose of having her change her statement to falsely claim that nothing had taken place between her and [Wood] on the night in question,” Boddice found.
Wood’s lawyer had argued that the risk of Wood interfering with witnesses could be overcome by “the inclusion of conditions that he not contact the complainant, either directly or indirectly”.
“The difficulty with accepting that contention,” Boddice found, was that Wood had provided sworn evidence to the primary judge to whom he first applied for bail that he understood and would meet those conditions.
Wood’s fresh appeal, Bodice found, “amounts to no more than a repeating of that intention”, even though there was evidence that “immediately upon a refusal of bail, he sought to have an intermediary contact [Snezana] for the express purpose of having her alter her evidence”.
“Against that background, there can be no confidence that conditions could be imposed, which would render the risk of interference with witnesses no longer unacceptable,” Boddice found.
“This court can have no confidence that the appellant would comply with those conditions.”
Wood is now seeking mediation with his wife.
Lawyers for the Melbourne-based fitness influencer made the mediation request days after his distressed prison call, asking to speak to his wife.
Justice mediation in Queensland provides a chance for the alleged offender and victim to discuss the matter, but can only occur if the latter consents.
The mediation matter was adjourned until 7 December.
