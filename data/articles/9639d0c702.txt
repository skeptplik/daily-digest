Santiago Lastra might have become a mathematician if it weren’t for a box of Ritz crackers. Growing up in Cuernavaca, Mexico, the future chef behind London restaurants KOL and Fonda was the sort of kid with more of an appetite for national math competitions. “There would be four questions and five hours to answer those four questions,” he remembers. “I loved that. I wanted to do that for the rest of my life.”
Then, at 15, Lastra spotted a crab dip recipe on the back of a cracker box. He made it for his family and watched as an ordinary day changed shape around something he had cooked. “I became obsessed with the idea that you can create a special occasion just by cooking something for someone,” he says. The mathematical brain moved into the kitchen. In his first cookbook, Mexican Soul: A New Style of Cooking, Lastra writes about the magic of both disciplines: starting with a few ingredients (or figures) and a recipe (or equation) and creating something greater than the sum of its parts.
The chef has spent the past month touring the U.S. in support of the book: He’s made tacos at New York’s Santo Taco, cooked at Yucatan-style seafood spot Holbox in Los Angeles, and enlisted The Bear’s Will Poulter to put a Mexican spin on the Italian beef sandwich at Chicago’s storied Mr. Beef (the real-life inspiration for the show’s original restaurant). Along the way, he’s helped introduce American audiences to a philosophy of Mexican cooking that is as resourceful and intuitive as it is traditional and communal.
It’s an instinct that has taken Lastra on a circuitous journey back home. He started out cooking Italian food, then left Mexico for kitchens in Spain. Various fascinations with French techniques, the experimentalism of Mugaritz, and New Nordic cuisine followed. “As a young kid, when you leave Mexico, you feel that you made it,” he says. “It took me a long time to realize that what we had in Mexico was really the answer.” Lastra began applying Mexican techniques to local ingredients in Scandinavia, then traveled and cooked across 27 countries. Returning to Mexico to work on Noma’s Tulum pop-up allowed him to look at his homeland with newly curious eyes. “Being Mexican became my superpower,” he says. “I was able to share culture through food, and it opened the doors to the world for me.”
KOL was his attempt to make that realization tangible: the Marylebone restaurant that Lastra opened in 2020 cooks Mexican food through seasonal British ingredients. Fonda, its more casual and rambunctious sibling, followed four years later. “I wanted KOL to be one of the best restaurants in the world,” he says, to prove “that Mexican food should be recognized at the level of all the best cuisines in the world.” Not everyone Lastra hoped to touch could ever make it to his London spots or the global pop-ups. “What really makes a difference culturally is the food that people do at home,” he says. “Home cooking is more personal.”
Mexican Soul posed some questions. How do you cook food that feels fundamentally Mexican without fresh masa, the right dried chillies, or a specialist grocer? Lastra didn’t want readers buying a two-pound bag of chipotle for one dinner. “I had to change the way I think,” he says. He began writing new rules for the weeknight home cook, rather than an ingredients-led chef. The transition required its own education. He pored over British supermarket aisles, and during a family beach vacation, Lastra locked himself inside to watch hours of Jamie Oliver. He and his brother Eduardo, his collaborator on the book and at KOL, went into a supermarket and decreed that nothing for the shoot could be ordered elsewhere.
“It tasted great,” he recalls. “I was like, wow… I think I’ve been overcomplicating myself all my life.” Just like in his childhood math competitions, limitations could provide the route to a more interesting solution. The book’s first half teaches some culinary grammar: Sourness, heat, sweetness, smoke, fat, and masa are defined and built into pillar dishes such as guacamole and quesadillas to demonstrate how those sensations work together. “Every time I create a dish, I always see flavors,” Lastra explains. “They’re like your paintings, your colors.”
The second half gets freer: tomato and cherry aguachile, potato rajas tamales, asparagus pipián. Innovating beyond traditional Mexican cooking, this style is rooted in the cuisine’s history and designed to work with seasonal, local, and accessible ingredients from wherever its cook lives. Lastra resists the idea of Mexican food as something stuck in amber. “The most beautiful thing about Mexican cooking, or Mexican food and Mexican culture in general, is that it keeps evolving,” he says. His proudest—and most last-minute addition—was a tortilla. Nearly defeated by the challenge of creating one without corn masa, Lastra had the novel idea of using mashed potato. Combining it with potato starch, he pressed and cooked the dough, and after days of tweaking, “suddenly it worked and it puffed!” It made it into the book just before printing.
Home-testing in the Lastra household has been even sweeter. After we speak, Lastra’s mother—who almost never cooks—is making the book’s mackerel a la Veracruzana for her sons. She’s already sending photographs as she shops for ingredients. “I think she’s never cooked a specific recipe from a cookbook in her life,” he says. “So that’s going to be such a highlight.”
Below, Santiago Lastra’s recipe for albóndigas and Mexican rice, two ways.
Albóndigas
Serves 4
Meatballs came to Mexico from the Middle East during the colonial period. They’re a classic of central Mexican home cooking, and are also served in casual eateries called fondas, where they are served with rice and bread or tortillas. The sauces they are served with vary from loose sauces made with cooked green tomatillos to rich tomato and guajillo chilli-based broths. I serve mine in a tomato caldillo with Mexican rice—a classic from our hometown. This is one of my favorite Mexican dishes, and one I would often choose to make if I am hosting friends for dinner.
- 1 lb 2 oz ground beef
- 1½ teaspoons sea salt flakes
- 1 teaspoon ground black pepper
- ½ oz peppermint, with stalks, finely chopped
- 6 large eggs, hard-boiled and quartered
- Plain (all-purpose) flour, for dusting
- 1 quantity Tomato Caldillo
- Vegetable oil, for deep-frying
For the bread cream:
- 3½ oz crustless white bread
- 3½ fl oz/scant ½ cup heavy cream
- 6 large eggs
- 1 roasted garlic clove
To serve:
- Red Mexican Rice
- Bread or tortillas
- Sliced cucumbers
- First, make the bread cream: combine all the ingredients and allow the bread to soak for at least 5 minutes. Then blitz in a food processor or with a hand-held blender until smooth.
- Put the beef, salt, pepper, and peppermint into a bowl and combine with your hands, then add the bread cream and work with your hands until smooth. Cover and leave in the refrigerator for 20–30 minutes to set.
- Remove the meat from the refrigerator and use an ice-cream scoop or spoon to divide it into 1¾ oz balls. Press 2 boiled egg quarters into the centre of each ball and close the meat around them, then dust the balls with flour.
- Half-fill a saucepan with oil and heat to 375°F and fry the meatballs for 3 minutes, turning and basting as needed, until golden but still pink on the inside. (They will finish cooking in the caldillo.) Remove the meatballs from the oil with a slotted spoon and place on a tray lined with paper towels to drain the excess oil.
- Pour the tomato caldillo into a saucepan and bring to a simmer, then add the meatballs, cover, and cook for 5 minutes.
- Serve the meatballs and sauce hot with rice, bread or tortillas, and cucumbers.
Red Mexican Rice
Serves 6
Red rice is present at most lunches for any Mexican growing up, whether at home or eaten in a fonda. The preparation is simple: rice with peas and carrots, cooked in a tomato and chilli caldillo. The result is a spicy, umami rice with a beautiful color. Serve with stews and tortillas.
- 20 fl oz/2½ cups water
- 2 tomatoes
- 1 garlic clove
- 1 teaspoon paprika
- 2 teaspoons vegetable oil
- 2 onions, 1 peeled and whole, 1 diced
- ½ carrot, peeled and diced into ½ inch cubes
- 9 oz/1 cup basmati rice
- 1¾ oz frozen peas
- ¼ oz/1½ teaspoons sea salt flakes
- Put the water, tomatoes, garlic, paprika, and the whole onion into a food processor and blend until smooth.
- Heat the oil in a saucepan over a medium heat and fry the diced onion and carrot for 3–4 minutes until the onion is translucent and the carrot is beginning to soften.
- Add the rice, tomato water, and salt, cover and cook for 12–14 minutes until the rice is just cooked, adding the peas in the last 2 minutes of cooking.
- Remove the pan from the heat and leave to stand with the lid on for 5–10 minutes before serving.
White Mexican Rice
Serves 6
Like the red version, this preparation adds a bit of spice, texture, and umami to plain rice. It’s served with all sorts of stews and other dishes in central Mexico, and also with broths like chicken consomé—the kind your mom might make for you when you’re unwell. Fresh jalapeños add a beautiful spicy finish. Perfect with chicken or any other proteins and refried beans.
- 1 teaspoon vegetable oil
- ¼ onion, diced into ½ inch cubes
- ¼ carrot, peeled and diced into ½ inch cubes
- ½ garlic clove, crushed
- 9 oz/1 cup basmati rice
- 1 jalapeño chilli, quartered
- 20 fl oz/2½ cups chicken broth
- ¼ oz/1½ teaspoons sea salt flakes
- 1¾ oz frozen peas
- Heat the oil in a saucepan over a medium heat and fry the onion, carrot and garlic for 3–4 minutes until starting to brown.
- Add the rice, jalapeños, chicken broth, and salt, cover and cook for 12–14 minutes until the rice is just cooked, adding the peas in the last 2 minutes of cooking.
- Remove the pan from the heat and leave to stand with the lid on for 5–10 minutes before serving.
