The NSW coroner has found the death of an Aboriginal man in custody in 2023 was not only preventable but was “one of the most egregious deaths” she had investigated.
Corrective Services NSW has been recommended to overhaul officer training for self-harm responses, and ban officers’ access to TV and YouTube while on shift.
Tian-Jarrah “TJ” Denniss, a Wiradjuri man, took his own life on 5 August 2023 at Silverwater prison.
The 33-year-old was in the prison’s complex placement unit (CPU) for inmates at acute risk of self-harm. In his time in NSW, Denniss had 17 interfacility transfers, and had occupied more than 100 cells in his last year of life.
In her report released on Friday, the state coroner, Judge Teresa O’Sullivan, found it was eight minutes between when Denniss covered up the camera in his cell at 6.56pm and when a cleaner reported to correctional officers that Denniss was appearing to self-harm, and another four minutes until a correctional officer reached Denniss’s cell.
His cell was not opened for another seven minutes after that, and CPR was not commenced until after 7.12pm. Denniss was pronounced dead at 7.53pm.
At 6.53pm, Denniss had used the “knock up” system to contact correctional officers, and threatened self harm, to which a correctional officer replied: “Oh will you stop threatening me”.
An officer monitoring the cell was watching sport on the TV in the office at the time.
O’Sullivan said in her report the evidence “discloses a cultural crisis in empathy and professionalism” within Corrective Services NSW “where self-harm is treated as manipulation or a disciplinary matter, abusive language is used towards inmates” and officers in monitoring roles are distracted from their duties.
“I consider that not only was Tian-Jarrah’s death preventable but that it is one of the most egregious deaths I have investigated,” she said.
“There is a critical need to take the lessons from this case and improve practice and procedure across the custodial system in NSW so that the tragic circumstances of Tian-Jarrah’s death are never repeated.”
The report found officers thought Denniss was “just manipulating” because he had made two earlier self-harm attempts in the shift “which did not seem genuine”, and assumed he was “playing possum” and they risked being assaulted if they entered his cell.
O’Sullivan said if the fear was genuine, the danger of any surprise attack is diminished as more officers arrive at the cell, and any suggestion Denniss was “playing possum” for seven minutes was “implausible, to say the least”.
The coroner also found the 17 facility transfers and 100 different cells were “a significant factor undermining Tian-Jarrah’s wellbeing” and it was inevitable he would not be in an optimal state to receive and positively respond to mental health and behavioural interventions.
Among her recommendations, O’Sullivan has said Corrective Services NSW should issue directions “prohibiting the use of personal electronic devices, the viewing of movies, television, YouTube, social media clips, or other forms of entertainment while on duty in any post with observational duties”. She said the agency should also investigate use of blocks or firewalls to restrict access to entertainment.
Corrective Services was also told to review its policies and training for assessment of unresponsive or injured inmates, including in situations where they might be “playing possum” to ensure that such concerns to do not result in undue delay in welfare checks or cell entry.
Officers should be trained that the concept of “playing possum” must not operate as a static assumption, O’Sullivan recommended.
O’Sullivan urged the agency to implement the recommendations urgently “given the number of Aboriginal deaths in custody has never been higher”.
Managing coronial advocate, James Pendler, from the NSW Aboriginal Legal Service, said the findings showed “TJ wasn’t humanised in jail”.
“The call of the family is that the recommendations are implemented and that this is taken seriously, so that this doesn’t happen again, not just for Aboriginal people but for all people in custody,” Pendler said.
“TJ came into contact with the justice system from an early age and unfortunately was involved in the all-too-common spiral of in and out of custody and he was caught up in this system that wasn’t safe for him.”
