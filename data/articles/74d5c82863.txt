Reader Jenny Ozanne writes to tell us of a horrific incident that befell her partner. He had bought Audrey Niffenegger’s novel The Time Traveler’s Wife as a CD audiobook, to listen to on long car journeys.
“After a while, he complained that the plot was completely impossible to follow,” writes Jenny. “It was only when I checked his player that I realized he had accidentally set the CD to ‘shuffle’. “
Jenny raises two points, which Feedback heartily endorses: “I’m still not sure what’s more impressive: the story’s ability to survive total chronological chaos, or the fact that he listened to three random chapters before suspecting anything was wrong.”
Feedback would suggest that The Time Traveler’s Wife might be the ideal novel to experience on shuffle, since the narrative is heavily non-linear. The main character hops uncontrollably back and forth through history. This led us to wonder: would any similarly chrono-displaced stories survive the experience of being played on shuffle, or even be improved by it?
We wondered, for instance, about David Mitchell’s Cloud Atlas. This has six stories set in different periods, including a future in which human clones are used as cheap labour, and a more distant future in which everything has gone a bit Mad Max. The stories are linked by characters in later timelines reading about the earlier ones, and by subtle coincidences. What would it be like on shuffle?
However, after careful reflection, we realised there is only one correct answer to this, and that is Shane Carruth’s 2004 film Primer, which tells the story of two tech bros who invent a time machine in their garage. At first, they use it to go back in time and make money on the stock exchange, only to become caught up in an escalating web of betrayal and deception as they endlessly rewrite their lives.
Primer is so overcomplicated, so mind-wreckingly confusing, that some poor soul once went so far as to draw a diagram trying to convey its timeline. Posted on Reddit by u/Clawz114, the diagram looks like a cross between a metro map and a flowchart of the inner workings of a chemical factory. It is therefore possible that playing the scenes in random order might actually cause the film to make more sense.
The scent of success
It’s that time of year when many of us are thinking, “Oh blimey, I need to buy everyone presents” and then having a meltdown when we can’t think of anything. The last refuge of the true gift scoundrel is, of course, the scented candle. This item might as well come with a note saying, “Sorry, I couldn’t think of anything”.
Readers looking to step up their scented candle game may wish to investigate JD + Kate Industries, which is run by a pair of humour writers with a sideline in themed scented candles. They have taken these tedious items to new and different places.
For instance, consider the Late Bronze Age Collapse scented candle, which “commemorates everyone’s favorite late 13th century [BC] widespread societal collapse”. Happily, it doesn’t smell of violence and decay, but rather of “burning cedar”, since “Lebanese cedar was a popular building material in the Late Bronze Age”.
Those with more contemporary tastes may wish to consider the AI scented candle, which has a little sculpture of a data centre on the top, “surrounded by a post-apocalyptic, bombed-out city”. What does it smell of, you ask? “Diesel generator exhaust fumes”.
Finally, those nostalgic for the headier days of space exploration might appreciate the Apollo 11 scented candle. Disappointingly, this doesn’t smell of moon dust, which, as we understand it, basically smells of burning because it reacts with oxygen on contact. Instead, its scent mimics “a certain powdered orange drink mix often associated with the space program, but that was not brought on Apollo 11. The astronauts brought a generic, grapefruit-flavored powdered drink mix instead, because they selfishly did not consider us and our desire to market candles.”
Feedback has no idea if these candles are any good, but we enjoyed JD + Kate’s website a lot.
By the letter
Let us foray once again into the wonderful world of scientific acronyms. First, two reader claims that, for reasons that will become obvious, we cannot verify.
Michael Berkson tells us: “As a solicitor my late wife was involved in development of insurance for doctors. The firm had to tell the client that they needed to choose a better name. Their original choice was Professional Indemnity Support Scheme.” Michael says the clients were open to replacing “scheme” with “service”, which helps not at all.
Likewise, Jim Taylor asserts that he was once confronted with a military programme intended to ensure that spare parts would always be available for key equipment. It was called the Contractor Repair Advanced Provisioning System.
Finally, a story we can confirm is true because we looked it up. Reader Geraldine Beare is an indexer, which is one of those jobs that we often forget about but would swiftly notice if it wasn’t done. “I am at present indexing a book on goats,” says Geraldine. Through this assignment she has learned the acronym “OMAGOD”, which she recommends saying “quickly, or perhaps in a vaguely Scottish accent”. It actually stands for ovine mouth and gum obscure disease.
