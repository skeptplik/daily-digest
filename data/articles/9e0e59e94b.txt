Anyone who accidentally switched to BBC One at about 9.45am on Sunday to be greeted by Theresa May awkwardly espousing the virtues of Brexit could have been forgiven for thinking they had woken up in around 2018 – and not being especially pleased at the idea.
This is one of the problems now facing May’s party. For all that it is a reflex action for Conservatives to push back against closer ties with the EU, Andy Burnham’s decision to pursue such a course plunges them back into their own unhappy place.
For Tory MPs and activists with a full set of Brexit campaign medals, this risks being especially triggering. May’s repeated attempts to cobble together a departure deal that could unite her party involved repeated humiliations, among them the heaviest ever parliamentary defeat for a British PM, when the Commons rejected her Brexit plan by a majority of 230.
With the afterglow of Boris Johnson’s “get Brexit done” 2019 election triumph now seven years and four prime ministers ago, under Kemi Badenoch the party has opted to do less of the, in David Cameron’s resonant phrase, “banging on about Europe” and instead focus on policy areas such as the economy, social security and defence.
In a speech to the CBI conference on Monday, Badenoch will condemn Burnham’s new deal with the EU, sealed at last week’s Brussels summit, but based on the argument that he botched the negotiations, not that closer links as such are bad in themselves.
“If we can make our trading relationship better, good. I’m all for working on a smarter deal,” she was to tell the business leaders’ gathering.
The reasons for this caution are obvious. To begin with, even more ardent Tory Brexiters struggle to articulate any tangible benefits to the UK in the decade since the referendum, beyond slightly niche constitutional points about self-determination.
In her BBC interview, May, who comments sparingly on live political subjects despite being an active Tory peer, did not even try to argue that Brexit had been a success, instead saying it was impossible to know, given events such as Covid and Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, and then adding: “I don’t think Britain’s state today is about whether or not we left the European Union.”
Many voters are much clearer about what they think. Polling suggests that a clear majority of Britons view Brexit as both a failure and a mistake in the first place, with similar support for rejoining the EU, although this depends in part on the pre-conditions for doing so.
This is a product of both lived experience and what pollsters delicately call “demographic churn” – that is, the death of many of the disproportionately older Britons who voted leave and their replacement on the electoral rolls by younger people with more positive views about the EU.
A new constituency-level poll on Brexit sentiment found that hundreds of previously leave-backing areas, including Burnham’s Makerfield seat, now support rejoining.
Of the 632 Commons seats in England, Scotland and Wales, it found that voters in 512 would rejoin the EU, with 47 too close to call. Just 73 areas – slightly over 11% of the total – still want to stay out.
For the Conservatives, the battle has not ended. Badenoch will lambast the PM when he sets out his new EU deal in parliament on Monday. Her deputy, Alex Burghart, has already promised that a future Tory government would unpick it.
But make no mistake, Europe is no longer the Conservatives’ defining political thread, and division, as it was for several decades. May might, understandably, be unable to move on from the issue that made her PM and then destroyed her career. But her party, like the country, has no choice.
