Of all the Magic: The Gathering sets released this year, I had the least amount of interest in Star Trek, but after playing a few hours as part of a Magic Arena preview this week, I’ve completely changed my tune. I bear no ill will toward the United Federation of Planets, and I recognize the profound impact Trek has had on pop culture over the last 60 years. I’ve just never become a fan. More importantly, most of my skepticism towards it as a Magic set stems from its incongruous nature as hard sci-fi crossing over with a high-fantasy trading card game that I adore.
If there’s one thing I do know about Star Trek, however, it’s that its vision of the future is brimming with optimism and curiosity. In tandem with that is an emphasis on the importance of diversity. I remember my dad watching Star Trek: The Next Generation when I was very young, and the “Wookiee guy” kind of scared me. These days, Worf (who is actually a Klingon) would probably be my favorite character. So it makes sense that what finally won me over about Magic's Trek connects directly to the franchise's idealistic core message.
Spock’s dad sold me on the Federation
I had a predictably frustrating experience playing Draft and Sealed on Arena while trying out a few different color combos. Nothing seemed to click. (I tried and failed a mostly green ramp and counters strategy.) Then I got Sarek, Federation Ambassador (even I know that’s Spock’s dad!) in a new pool of cards and immediately recognized that he has strong Commander vibes thanks to a new Magic mechanic designed specifically for the Star Trek set.
Sarek’s "Federation" ability lets you draw one card for every five creature types among non-Borg creatures you control at the start of combat on your turn. If you draw three or more (you have 15+ creature types), he puts two +1/+1 counters on all of your creatures as well. He feels like one of those Commanders with a powerful ability that’s challenging to execute fully on, but if you like a convoluted deck-building challenge, he’s probably one of the more immensely satisfying Commanders from the set.
Since Sarek is Bant (white-blue-green), that gave me access to a large number of cards in my pool, and I quickly realized I had a few more Federation abilities there. Captain Jonathan Archer immediately reminded me of cards like Rinoa Heartilly or Rufus Shinra that generate their own animal companion. For Captain Archer, that’s his trusty beagle Porthos. With two copies of the captain in my deck, his Federation ability became the glue that held it all together: For a cost of one green mana, he taps to give one target creature +X/+X where X is the number of creature types among non-Borg creatures you control.
It’s not game-breaking by any means, but that kind of scalable buff means that you can reliably ensure that one of your creatures gets consistently big enough to punch through your opponents defenses, or at least whittle away at their board.
A diverse crew makes a stronger Magic deck
Archer is a Human Officer, while the Porthos creature token he creates is a Dog, so together they already bring that buff up to +3/+3. But Star Trek at large is chock-full of aliens with jobs, so all I really needed to do was look for useful creatures to help pad out the deck. Xenobotanist is a Vulcan Scientist (score two points for the Federation!) mana dork that can tap to add one mana of any color. David and Carol Marcus, Creators (Human Scientist) also help with ramp by creating a New Planet land token upon entering.
Perhaps the most clutch addition for this Archer strategy was Shuttle Ace, a Kelpian Pilot that has flying during your turn and gains you two life whenever it becomes tapped. Because of all the Spacecrafts in this set, Star Trek has plenty of flyers, but that didn’t stop Shuttle Ace from becoming a solid late-game finisher for me. (You can also tap him to charge up your own Spacecraft for some easy lifegain.)
I also had Hoshi Sato, Exolinguist, which has a Federation ability when she enters that lets you look at the top X cards of your library based on your non-Borgs, then you put one card into your hand and the rest on the bottom of your library. That grants some pretty huge card advantage, letting me sneak Shuttle Ace in when I needed him most.
The other really important card in this deck is Saavik, Stoic Student. She has Flash and her Federation ability deals X damage to target attacking or blocking creature based on your non-Borg count when she enters. In one late-game match, I tricked my opponent into blocking an underpowered Porthos and then dropped Saavik in during combat to obliterate a strong blocker. My opponent actually rage-quit, which is always a good sign that your deck is doing something right.
The general strategy here is to go wide with a variety of creature types. Since size doesn’t matter as much as diversity, I also dipped into some +1/+1 counter production to beef up my army. Brad Boimler, Eager Ensign, is an interesting card for that since every time he taps, for the rest of your turn, he adds an extra counter every time you distribute a counter. In a set full of Spacecraft that require you to Station, you can tap him very easily without putting him in combat where he might die. Perhaps my best combat trick happened when I did exactly that and then played Test of Conviction, a dilemma card that can put a +1/+1 counter on each creature you control. Thanks to Boimler, that became +2/+2 to the seven creatures I had on the board.
The few times I drew Sarek, he proved himself worth the inclusion. Since he’s a Vulcan Advisor, all I needed was one of the many Human Officers available to me and one other creature type to trigger his card draw. Having it come at the start of combat feels a bit awkward, but with enough instants and Flash creatures, it’s still totally worth it. None of my Limited matches dragged on long enough for me to reach the 15 creature types needed to trigger his counter distribution, but I got close enough that it certainly feels possible. If I’m lucky enough to pull a physical copy, I’ll surely build him as a Commander.
Star Trek’s optimism is more than flavor text
According to Wizards of the Coast’s official description of the Federation mechanic, it has everything to do with the fundamental ethos of the Federation itself: “The Federation draws strength from its many species contributing their knowledge and resources toward a better future.” That’s kind of a beautiful sentiment in itself, and the way it translates into the mechanic makes for a compelling gameplay strategy that also serves as a flavorful adaptation of Trek’s central theme.
It’s easy to pull off in a sci-fi set featuring a bunch of different aliens, but I’m curious to see how it might function outside Limited formats. One of the other Federation cards is Jean-Luc, Mon Capitaine from the Enterprise-Q scene box. Captain Picard sports some spectral faerie wings here, and all of your faeries enter with an additional +1/+1 counter on them for each non-Borg you have out. That strikes me as a really interesting and hilarious Commander deck to try building. There are about 308 legal creature types in Magic, so what might that look like if you make a deck with just enough powerful faeries supplemented by a wide variety of creature types?
To make a deck like this work, you don’t need to hyper-focus on a singular strategy or synergistic mechanics. You can get away with a few core pieces and include a bunch of cards that provide you with value as long as you pay attention to creature type variety. Because each species brings its own strengths to the table, those strengths contribute to a better future — and a better Magic deck.
