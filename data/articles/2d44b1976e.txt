More than 150 child safety groups and experts across six continents have urged Amazon and eBay to stop selling AI toys this Christmas until they are proved to be safe.
In a letter sent to Amazon’s president, Andy Jassy, and eBay’s president, Jamie Iannone, the coalition called for the online retailers to stop the sale of AI toys and offer refunds to customers who have already bought one.
The coalition, which includes the psychologist Jonathan Haidt’s The Anxious Generation Movement and UK and US child safety groups, says the risks associated with these toys are “well documented”.
One plush bunny was listed for ages three and older while being connected to ChatGPT, whose own terms require users to be 13 or older. When tested by the UK group Alliance 4 Children, the toy suggested a child could give consent to being touched for pleasure.
The listing was pulled by Amazon, but after 48 hours the toy returned to the platform under a new retailer, according to Alliance 4 Children. The second listing has now been removed by Amazon.
“These toys are cheap, easy to buy and largely untested,” the letter stated, also noting that they were marketed towards children as young as three years old.
The letter states that nearly 30 AI-enabled toys were available on Amazon’s UK website in late October, while about 50 were recently found on sale on Amazon in the US.
Several toys were advertised as being able to clone family members’ voices and provide “emotional growth tracking” for children.
In March 2026, a study by the University of Cambridge for the Childhood Trust found that young children were forming emotional bonds with AI toys. It also found no evidence on possible benefits to children’s development.
“The precautionary principle applies,” the letter said. “These products should be shown to be safe before they reach children, not withdrawn one at a time after harm is found.”
Last Tuesday, the United States Public Interest Research Group published a report after testing seven AI toys. It found that most of the products reviewed were still too faulty to be on the market, and had big privacy issues or responded inappropriately to questions from children. One toy was found to unexpectedly take photos of the testers.
On Friday, a letter signed by 52 MPs and peers was handed in at No 10, calling for the business secretary, Jonathan Reynolds, to restrict the sale of AI toys until they have been proved safe.
The letter also asks for the Office for Product Safety and Standards (OPSS) to issue an alert for parents and retailers about the risks of buying AI toys before Christmas.
Signatories include the MPs Jess Asato, Sharon Hodgson, Andrea Leadsom and Caroline Dinenage.
“AI chatbot toys are not safe for children – it is as simple as that,” said Asato. “Parents expect that toys they buy for their kids are safe; they don’t expect those toys to give sexual responses or advocate suicide.
“In the run-up to Christmas, it is imperative that the government acts to make retailers and parents aware of the very real risks and work to prohibit the sale of these toys.”
The government launched a review into its toy safety regulation in July, including the emerging risks posed by AI-enabled toys. Calls for evidence closed on 6 October.
A spokesperson for Amazon said: “We require all products sold by third-party sellers in our store to comply with applicable laws and regulations. Amazon is committed to the safety of children and families.
“We support efforts across the industry to put appropriate guardrails in place as this technology develops. We’ve removed the named products in question as we review claims.”
A spokesperson for eBay said: “We are committed to protecting buyers and sellers on our marketplace. We continually evaluate our policies, engage with regulators, and comply with applicable laws to help ensure our marketplace remains safe, trusted, and responsive to evolving safety standards.”
A spokesperson for the Department for Business, Innovation, Science and Trade said: “We are very concerned by these reports and are already investigating these products, as well as the potential risks posed by AI in toys.
“There is no excuse for anyone to sell products that could put children at risk, and companies need to do all they can to act quickly and put things right.”
The OPSS has been contacted for comment.
