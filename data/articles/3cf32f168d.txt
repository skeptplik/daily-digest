A couple who are expecting their first child have said their excitement has turned "into complete worry" because of the suspension of maternity services at their local hospital.
Shelly Magee was due to give birth in Newry's Daisy Hill Hospital in January but from Monday, the Southern Trust is moving all hospital-based births to Craigavon.
Her partner, Clinton McKeever, said they are now dreading the much longer journey from their home in Mullaghbawn to Craigavon in the middle of winter.
The trust announced the temporary suspension of maternity services at Daisy Hill in September, saying it had been unable to recruit enough consultant obstetricians to maintain a safe service for both mums and babies.
From Monday, it has advised all expectant mothers in the Southern Health Trust to go to Craigavon Area Hospital - not Daisy Hill - for all labour and birthing services, inpatient maternity care and emergency gynaecology support.
The trust said an estimated 13 women could possibly deliver their babies on Monday at Craigavon.
That includes planned C sections and those booked in for induction - four of these had originally planned to have their babies in Daisy Hill.
'Scandalous'
It added that patients will receive the same high standard of care from experienced specialists, including staff from Daisy Hill who are relocating to Craigavon.
But the expectant couple have not been reassured by the trust's statements.
Magee said Craigavon Area Hospital is "50 minutes from the house" by car but her partner pointed out that could rise to 90 minutes in heavy traffic or severe weather.
"A child doesn't choose a time to come," McKeever told BBC News NI.
"To strip away a maternity service and claim it's for the safety of women is scandalous... there's no thought of safety in that journey."
He said he was speaking from experience, having "had a taste of the journey already in a scenario that would be considered a crisis".
About five years ago, his father was badly injured in a fall during which he suffered several broken ribs and a punctured lung.
McKeever drove his father to Craigavon, having been advised by their local health clinic that it would take too long for an ambulance to get to Mullaghbawn.
"The traffic was crazy and he felt every bump - I felt guilty for every bump. I got him there in the end but it was a very, very stressful journey," he recalled.
"To know that I have to do it again with Shelly here, it's very concerning. It's took the excitement out of the whole thing and turned it into complete worry."
To avoid having to drive to Craigavon in active labour in early January the couple are now considering booking a hotel or rented accommodation near the hospital.
Despite their concerns, they were full of praise for midwives who cared for them in Newry and Crossmaglen, acknowledging staff also face uncertainty.
"They're brilliant, they keep up with everything, but they're in the dark as much as we are, that's the frustrating thing," Magee said.
Hospital campaigners have also raised concerns about what the trust is describing as a temporary suspension.
Conor Patterson, chair of the Daisy Hill Future Group, expressed "deep concern" that the move would affect "time-critical access to maternity care".
He claimed it subjects families in counties Down, Armagh and the border area to "life and death risks".
"Moving up to 1,800 births from Newry to Craigavon risks not just destabilising Craigavon's maternity unit but will threaten also its ability to safely provide other acute medical services."
How many births will be affected?
According to figures from the trust, 1,713 babies were born at Daisy Hill last year, while Craigavon dealt with 2,768 births.
When asked how Craigavon will deal with hundreds of extra births each year, the trust said it was continuing efforts to secure consultant obstetricians for Daisy Hill. There are also plans to open an additional ward.
It said it was also preparing continuity arrangements to temporarily consolidate births and inpatient maternity care at Craigavon from Monday.
The trust employs 15 consultant obstetricians. However, sickness, reduced working hours and maternity leave means not all 15 can contribute to the on-call rota.
Despite ongoing recruitment efforts across the UK and internationally, the trust said it has been unable to recruit enough consultants to maintain a safe and reliable service at both hospitals.
Its spokesperson said inpatient maternity care at Craigavon had been identified as the safest and most sustainable arrangement for maintaining the trust's services.
The number of midwives employed within the maternity units at both acute hospitals has dropped from pre-pandemic levels.
In October 2019, there were 55.6 midwives employed on a full-time basis at Daisy Hill Hospital and 95.04 full-time in Craigavon.
As of last month, that had dropped to 47.57 midwives at Daisy Hill and 82.58 in Craigavon.
However, the trust said midwifery care has changed over the past seven years, with more midwives now working in the community.
The trust added that 16 newly-appointed midwives are due to start work between November 2026 and January 2027.
Its medical director for maternity and women's health, Dr Beverley Adams, said all efforts had been made to avoid the suspension and she apologised for any disappointment caused for women who had planned to give birth at Daisy Hill.
"Teams at both hospitals have been working hard to manage these challenges and prepare to provide all births, inpatient maternity care and emergency gynae care for the Southern Trust at Craigavon Area hospital."
A spokesperson for the trust said that of the 1,088 planned births up to the end of February, it estimated that 294 of these were originally planned for Daisy Hill.
A proportion of these women may already have been referred to Craigavon based on risk.
To support the maternity service at Craigavon, a total of 37 staff, including midwives and sisters, are to transfer.
It is not the first time that Daisy Hill has lost hospital services because of staffing problems.
Its emergency general surgery was permanently moved to Craigavon in 2024, two years after it was temporarily suspended due to recruitment challenges.
