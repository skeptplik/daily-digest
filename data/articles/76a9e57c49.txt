My wife, Diana, and I come from big families, so Thanksgiving means wall-to-wall parties. Last year we spent Thursday with her family, with the weekend earmarked for mine. On the Friday, we’d get together with Diana’s cousins.
We were looking forward to a fun night. When someone announced a game of Eat the Bag (known as the Cereal Box Game in the UK), I was raring to go.
The premise is simple: a paper grocery bag is placed in the centre of the room, and players take turns picking it up with their teeth. After each round, a strip is torn from the rim, making it harder to reach.
Diana’s family are competitive, but I am, too. I’ve played sports and kept in shape. Some people went for a side‑splits stance. Others, a deep squat. I’m pretty flexible so I positioned myself in a forward lunge. One by one, players dropped out until it was just me, Diana and the reigning champion, her cousin Jimmy.
I was determined to not let my wife beat me. I went into my lunge, braced my hands on my right ankle, and twisted inwards. All my weight was loaded on my front leg.
“You’ve got it, James!” someone shouted.
Suddenly, there was a loud crack, and I collapsed to the floor. I heard gasps, then saw concerned faces looking down at me. I went into shock and started to sweat. Diana said my face started turning white. Thankfully, adrenaline blocked the initial pain.
Someone called an ambulance, and it arrived in minutes. When the paramedics announced they’d have to manually straighten my leg to stabilise it, everyone quickly disappeared to avoid the gruesome scene. They gave me morphine before cutting off my jeans. I don’t remember it, but Diana told me I said: “My favourite pair!”
It took four paramedics to pull the femur back into place. I’m grateful that part is a blur.
At the hospital, the orthopaedic surgeon explained that by turning my knee out and my body in, I’d put a twisting force on my femur. It’s strong but not designed for that kind of pressure. My flexibility played a part, too. “Most people would have pulled a muscle and stopped,” he said. “But you kept going.”
A metal rod would have to be threaded through my femur and fixed with screws. I was waiting for surgery when Diana told me her cousin Tia had filmed the game on her phone. I asked her to send me the video. I needed to understand what had happened. Watching my thigh bone give way made me feel queasy. Even the surgeon recoiled.
The video was put on the family chat. When my niece Jessica asked to post it on her social media, I told her to go ahead. I couldn’t believe it when, a few days later, she told me it had gone viral, with hundreds of thousands of views. I even had nurses coming into the ward asking, “Is this you?”
It was an amusing distraction. I filmed a follow-up video from my hospital bed, because people from all over the world were demanding an update. By the time I left the hospital a week later, the original video had been watched over a million times.
At home, I was on a walker for four weeks, then I used a cane. I’m a landscape gardener – thankfully, it was our slow season. Still, I felt guilty that I couldn’t help Diana with the kids. I also felt bad for messing up Thanksgiving. My friends and family reassured me their only concern was that I was OK. They would check in on me and deliver meals.
It would have been easy to feel down about the long recovery ahead. I chose to focus on the upsides, like spending more time with Diana and the kids. When Christmas approached, I wrapped my walking cane with coloured lights. I set myself little goals. The first was attending a concert in the new year. “Aren’t you that femur guy?” someone shouted, as I hobbled through the crowd.
I’m still working on getting back to full fitness. Keeping a sense of humour has helped, as well as Diana’s support. This Thanksgiving weekend, the cousins are gathering again. Jimmy has made it clear that I forfeited last year’s game by falling, and that he remains the champion.
He’ll be defending his title again. This time I’ll stick to cheering him on from the sidelines.
