England has the largest gap between higher and lower socioeconomic groups when it comes to frailty compared with 30 other countries, a major study has found.
The research, led by academics at King’s College London and published in BMC Medicine, looked at levels of frailty in 71,703 people from 31 countries including England, the US, China, France, Italy, the Netherlands, Sweden, Greece, Cyprus, Latvia and Lithuania.
Frailty, defined as a person who struggles with multiple daily activities and cannot easily recover from minor illnesses, was measured on a scale from 0 to 100, based on the proportion of health problems and functional limitations a person experienced, with higher scores corresponding with greater frailty.
The study found that for all countries, at age 50, those in the lowest socioeconomic group had frailty scores nearly seven points higher than people in the highest socioeconomic group. And by age 80, this gap had widened to about 12 points, which suggests that socioeconomic inequalities in frailty become more pronounced with age.
England specifically had the widest gap of frailty between higher and lower socioeconomic groups at age 50. The study did not cover Scotland, Wales or Northern Ireland.
Dongfeng Tang, first author of the study from the department of primary care and data science at King’s College London, said the findings showed that developed nations such as England could “still have wide socioeconomic disparities in health compared to other countries.
“In cross-national data, England had lower-than-average numbers of physicians per population and higher numbers of people with unmet healthcare needs. These relative differences in healthcare capacity and access may disproportionately affect people from lower socioeconomic groups.”
Tang added that the findings also showed the “dramatic effect of socioeconomic disparities on healthy ageing. People from lower socioeconomic groups tend to experience poorer health across the life-course and accumulate health problems at earlier ages.”
He added: “It is well established that people from lower socioeconomic groups tend to experience poorer health. What our research adds is a clearer picture of how these inequalities in frailty develop across mid to later life and how substantially they differ between countries. We also found that lifestyle factors, particularly physical activity and social engagement, accounted for part of these inequalities, although much of the gap remained unexplained.”
The researchers also identified social and physical inactivity as the two main drivers of the higher levels of frailty in people with lower socioeconomic status, suggesting that there are strong links between frailty and social habits.
Matthew O’Connell, senior lecturer in population health sciences at King’s College London and the senior author of the study, said: “It is highly concerning that people from lower socioeconomic groups had higher levels of frailty across all countries studied, with the greatest inequalities in England.
“More encouragingly, the increasing role of lifestyle factors at older ages suggests increasing physical and social activities in mid to later life might help to reduce frailty at older ages. This is something that could be encouraged for everyone as they age and could be a targeted invention for groups potentially at higher risk.”
