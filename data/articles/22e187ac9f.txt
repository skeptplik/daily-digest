In many parts of Taliban-ruled Afghanistan, male doctors are no longer allowed to treat women. The consequences have been highlighted in a recent United Nations report, which offers disturbing insight into the harsh reality many women face.
In one case, a woman from Kunar province described how she suffered several miscarriages without being able to access medical care.
Her case highlights the increasing number of pregnant women who urgently need medical assistance and are unable to receive it because of restrictions imposed by the Taliban.
And overall, women in Afghanistan are increasingly losing access to essential healthcare. This is due both to the Taliban's restrictions and to cuts in international aid funding.
Taliban remove women from public life in Afghanistan
"If a pregnant woman dies because male doctors are not allowed to treat her, her husband can marry another, younger woman. That is how the Taliban think," said 28-year-old Tamana Zaryab Paryani.
Paryani is an Afghan women's rights activist and one of the best-known voices of resistance against the Taliban.
A trained lawyer, she worked as a newspaper journalist before the Taliban returned to power. Following their takeover in August 2021, she took part in protests against restrictions on the rights of women and girls. In January 2022, she was detained by the Taliban in Kabul alongside her sisters. After her release, she managed to reach Europe.
Since then, she has continued to advocate internationally for the rights of Afghan women. At the end of October, she addressed the UN Security Council, describing the repression of women in Afghanistan and urging the international community not to normalise their systematic disenfranchisement.
"This is gender apartheid. We expect the international community to recognise it and stand with the women of Afghanistan," Paryani told DW.
Since returning to power in 2021, the Taliban have issued more than 100 decrees severely restricting the lives of women and girls. Girls are barred from secondary education, while women are denied access to universities. Women's opportunities to work outside the home are also severely restricted.
The UN Assistance Mission in Afghanistan (UNAMA), which monitors and regularly documents the human rights situation in the country, reported in September 2025 that inspectors from the Taliban's Ministry for the Promotion of Virtue and Prevention of Vice were further restricting women's access to healthcare through the enforcement of regulations and decrees.
According to the UN, around 2.6 million girls are excluded from secondary education. This is likely to lead to more girls being married off at an early age and to an increase in maternal mortality, UN Women warns.
Afghanistan has a population of around 45 million. With a median age of approximately 17, it has one of the youngest populations in the world. Around 42% of its population is under the age of 15.
The world's 'worst women's rights crisis'
"What is happening in Afghanistan goes beyond gender-based persecution, as already defined under the Rome Statute of the International Criminal Court," Haroon Mutasem, professor of International Criminal Law at the American University of Afghanistan and research fellow at Berlin's Humboldt University, told DW.
The Afghan legal scholar examines how the systematic oppression of women in Afghanistan can be classified and prosecuted under international law.
"This is not just about individual acts of persecution, but about an institutionalised system of oppression and domination of one gender group by another. They have established a system that considers women as secondary and defines a life for them as raising kids, working at home, and also actually satisfying the sexual desires of their men."
Mutasem argues that a failure to respond to the systematic oppression of Afghan women could undermine the universal validity of human and women's rights. He warns that other authoritarian regimes and extremist movements could adopt similar practices.
According to UN estimates, in 2027 more than 4 million children under the age of five, as well as around 1.27 million pregnant and breastfeeding women, will require treatment for acute malnutrition.
Susan Ferguson, the UN's Special Representative in Afghanistan, described the situation in her latest report as "the world's worst women's rights crisis." Afghan women, she said, are now "fighting for their very survival."
Cuts in aid funding have forced humanitarian organisations to make "impossible decisions about which women and girls can receive support and which have to be turned away," Ferguson said.
