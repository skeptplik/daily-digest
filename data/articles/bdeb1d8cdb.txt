A former Murdoch media executive has admitted he said Grace Tame “must be looking to be raped again” at an event, after the former Australian of the year overheard the comment and confronted him.
Tame, a child sexual assault victim survivor who was Australian of the year in 2021 for her advocacy work, revealed in an opinion piece for Crikey that she heard Matt Handbury make the comment to another man at a function last week.
“That’s Grace Tame. Look at her with her tits hanging out. She must be looking to be raped again,” Handbury said, according to Tame’s piece published on Friday.
Tame wrote that she didn’t realise that the man was Handbury until a bystander told her. He introduced himself as “Matt” after she confronted him.
The 77-year-old is Rupert Murdoch’s nephew and the former owner of Murdoch Magazines and Murdoch Books. He is now on the board of The Sydney Retreat, a rehab centre.
Tame said Handbury conceded at the UNSW event – where former military lawyer David McBride was presented with an award – that he had made the comment.
The following day, Handbury sent an email to Tame, seen by Guardian Australia, in which he also admitted he said “she must be looking to be raped again”. 
Tame was 15 when repeatedly sexually abused by her 58-year-old maths teacher, Nicolaas Bester, who was later convicted and jailed. In 2019, Tame won a supreme court exemption which overturned a Tasmanian law that had until then gagged her from speaking about her experiences of assault.
“He [Handbury] didn’t say she must be looking to get raped,” Tame told Guardian Australia. “He said she must be looking to get raped again. Therefore evidencing his prior knowledge of what I have been through.”
Tame said that the comment “activated really deep seated trauma and there are consequences to that”.
“It’s really destabilising and disorienting because your body is essentially your abused self and it is in total survival mode,” she said.
Handbury said in the email to Tame: “I did make reference to your strikingly attractive get-up. It was meant to be in appreciation in a male-to-male exchange. I can see that might be offensive in the context of suggesting a woman is making herself attractive to men in some titillating fashion.”
“I also said: ‘She must be looking to be raped again.’ This was certainly meant for no one’s ears but my friend. However it was in poor taste. In fact in highly objectionable taste and I regretted it as soon as I got out into the fresh air. Thank you for calling me out.
“Some men, obviously including myself, are prone to finding suggestive humour in the wrong places. I deeply regret my letting such bad habits get in the way of common courtesy, respect and decency. Please accept my humblest apologies.
“Thank you for opening my eyes more fully to this flaw in my nature which I will certainly be discussing with my very non-misogynist shrink on Friday. But no one can deny you looked absolutely fabulous that night.”
‘I’m tired of the maltreatment’
Tame wrote in her Crikey piece that “what makes this provocation especially egregious is that Matt didn’t reference an imagined harm, but a very real, criminally tested traumatic experience, which he knew about”.
“It swiftly reawakened deep-seated physical wounds and haunting memories. How is that funny?” she wrote.
“This isn’t about Matt personally; it’s about a broader culture of normalised violence. However, part of that culture is the self-censorship that protects people like him.”
Tame referred to research that suggested 35% of Australian men believe women’s rights have come too far, adding that “research has repeatedly shown that misogynistic attitudes are a broad predictor of violent extremism and prevalent among men of all ages.”
Tame told Guardian Australia she often faced re-traumatising behaviour from men.
“I’m tired. I’m tired of the maltreatment. I’m tired of the gaslighting. I’m tired of being baited and then have people be shocked why I would be angry or re-traumatised,” she said on Friday.
Tame thanked bystanders at the event who supported her following Handbury’s comment.
The chief executive of Full Stop Australia, Karen Bevan, called on people to speak up if they heard similar comments because “we all have the power to shift this”.
“If one of the most recognised survivor advocates in the country can be spoken about like this at an event honouring a whistleblower in a room full of people, you can be assured this behaviour is normalised,” Bevan said.
“Things like this do get said in workplaces, pubs, group chats and boardrooms across the country, where no one is recording, and no one writes about it afterwards.”
Handbury was contacted for comment, as was The Sydney Retreat.
