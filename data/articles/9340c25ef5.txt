Three days ago — an eon in the context of Australian cricket's most embarrassing and avoidable omnishambles — former Australian wicketkeeper Ian Healy offered a reasonable take on Australia's famed bowling group.
"I would say it taints their personal legacies … I don't think it's going to taint their performance legacies," Healy said. "Personally now, they're not as credible as they once were, unless they change something drastically in the next few days."
Healy's personal stake in this is self-evident: his niece is married to Mitchell Starc. And in a fast-evolving crisis, his qualifiers were understandable, although we will get to Josh Hazlewood's version of drastic intervention later.
Healy was right to differentiate between the professional and personal: one need not disparage all the hard work, all the joy, all the winning they have given Australians, by admitting that Australia's bowlers have, in recent times, irreparably damaged their own and Australia's credibility.
The bowlers also surely must concede that the precise and absolute truth of what happened in 2018 no longer matters to most fans. In fact, their carefully-worded prevarications and collective obsession with semantic distinctions project an air of guilt almost comically out of proportion with the original offence.
No matter what they saw or heard, barely anyone believes what they are saying about it.
Even if players have publicly approved of Cricket Australia's "truth forum", the rest of us surely agree it is as likely to establish an agreed and convincing set of facts as it is to locate Jimmy Hoffa.
So, to extend Healy's thought, what is their legacy, exactly?
They can hardly blame us for pondering it now in unflattering light. Because the obsession with the "legacy" of Australia's bowlers predates Usman Khawaja's book.
In the past 12 months, the idea has been fairly rammed down our throats as justification for keeping the gang together for a marathon 12 months of Test engagements against South Africa, New Zealand, India and England. The last hurrah for a golden generation, they said.
It could be argued that the very concept was rendered pointless weeks ago, before Sandpapergate's flare-up, on Australia's disastrous recent tour of South Africa.
It is one of the ironies of the present crisis engulfing the bowlers: it has precluded lengthier analysis of their deep and abject failure in the most recent Cape Town Test.
All the noise coming from within the team, of course, suggested this was a mere setback: South Africa are world champions for a reason, ya know? Geez, I guess we shouldn't have bowled first. Oh well, onward and upward, eh?
Actually, it was proof not just of muddled selection priorities and dwindling returns from old stagers, but a deeper crisis of philosophy and proof that the bowlers, in particular, were not operating in a meritocracy.
Having played out of his skin in the second Test, Scott Boland was dropped for Nathan Lyon, who was in turn out-bowled by Simon Harmer, an off-spinner most Australians had previously thought of as a glorified clubbie.
And Harmer didn't merely take wickets. He publicly called out and dismantled the Australian captain in a manner that should have caused far more alarm than whatever Cummins did or didn't see as a 24-year-old.
To delve into another modern-day obsession — being "aura" — the loss went far deeper than a 2-1 scoreline. Before its present distraction, Australian cricket pretended otherwise, but symbolic damage was done on a scale we will see more clearly in a few years.
Which brings us to Josh Hazlewood. On a very simple level, his claim yesterday that he would "review" his place in the Australian team if Cummins lost the captaincy was an impassioned if artless expression of loyalty and solidarity with the skipper in times of disloyalty.
More so, it smacked of the cart being placed a hell of a long way in front of the horse. It was an expression of entitlement that brought to mind another unflattering scene from a year ago, of an "absolutely filthy" Nathan Lyon sulking away to a sideline TV reporter after being omitted from last December's pink-ball Test. There have been others.
For a long time now — long before Sandpapergate reared up again — there has been an inescapable sense that Australia's senior players see themselves not as members of a team chosen by an independent selection panel, nor as merely a self-selecting elite within that team, but as the self-appointed bosses of Australian Test cricket.
It is hard to escape the feeling that it is this self-absorbed culture of entitlement and the desire to control its own narrative — much more so than anything that happened in 2018 — that now defines what the public thinks of Australia's bowlers.
And yes, it is true that those who've thrown their weight around like this are, by most definitions, indisputably Australia's best. And it is also true that they have sometimes needed to exercise their collective power for the benefit of others, too, as in their pay negotiations.
But what is the average fan — who, by the time the Perth Test rolls around in nine days, will have been subjected to weeks of a melodrama which is only a paternity test and a few exploding boats away from a Home and Away plot — to make of a 35-year-old multi-millionaire threatening to take his ball and go home if his mate's not in charge?
"The cartel". It has always had a faintly sinister ring to it, don't you think? Some of the behaviour is, frankly, gangsterism, albeit in the territory mined by Martin Scorsese and David Chase — the petty spats over matters of honour and respect, the fragile egos and wounded pride.
In this light, we now see Cameron Bancroft as the hapless 'Spider', egged on and then assassinated. Perhaps we suddenly respect Steve Smith a little more for silently doing all that time for crimes he didn't commit.
Which leaves us with how Australia proceeds in the short-term. Cricket Australia, more to blame for the mess than any player, confirmed on Saturday that Cummins would be retained as captain. It was the most predictable and probably the most sensible course of action based on a sober appraisal of all relevant facts, but it did also have the unfortunate effect of validating Hazlewood's veiled threat.
Even granting the need to extinguish a tabloid bin-fire, and acknowledging the overwhelming good Cummins has done for Australian cricket, there is an irony in this: backing him in as leader guarantees him a higher work and stress load than he would ideally be shouldering at this point of his career. In an ideal world, free of this scandal, honest conversations about succession planning would already be happening.
Hazlewood's interjection and the self-conferred gravitas of his Test spot, meanwhile, sit somewhat at odds with the prevailing views of his contemporaries. Only a week ago, players' union boss Paul Marsh admitted there was "decreasing" support among players for the idea of Test cricket as the pinnacle of the sport.
Fans, by extension, are now buffeted by administrators who care too little about Test cricket and a few players who care too much.
In the time since Sandpapergate, Cricket Australia has had six chairmen, four CEOs, three men's head coaches and capped 21 new Test players. That a few mainstays attained an over-mighty status is perhaps natural enough, but it is surely time that senior players are reminded that Test selection is not their birthright.
Australia's next 15 Tests would have been brutally difficult in any context. Against such a draining and unpleasant backdrop, the degree of difficulty for an aging and now embittered group of old hands has only increased.
And that really is the stuff of "legacy" — not just everything in totality, but the aftertaste left by the final few bites.
