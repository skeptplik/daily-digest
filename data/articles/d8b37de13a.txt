In 2022, Ukraine bombed the Kerch bridge linking mainland Russia to the Crimean Peninsula, using explosives hidden in a truck. In 2023, naval drones struck the bridge. In 2025, covert Ukrainian operatives planted bombs on its undersea support pillars. But none of these attacks caused major damage to the structure, which Russian President Vladimir Putin described in 2018 as a “miracle”. But earlier this week, two back-to-back Ukrainian attacks, involving drones and missiles, brought down a crucial span of the road bridge and damaged the parallel rail bridge. Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy hailed it as an “unprecedented operation”. Russian authorities acknowledged the damage, announcing a suspension of road traffic and restrictions on train services.
The 19-km Kerch bridge, across the Kerch Strait, which connects the Black Sea and the Sea of Azov, is the longest bridge in Europe, built at a cost of $3.6 billion. It was opened in 2018 by Russian President Vladimir Putin, four years after Russia annexed Crimea from Ukraine through a contested referendum. Mr. Putin drove an orange truck across its length and said “a miracle has come true”.
The bridge is important for Russia for symbolic, administrative and operational reasons. When Russia swiftly moved to annex Crimea in 2014, after a pro-Russian elected government in Kyiv fell amid West-backed anti-regime protests, there was no direct connectivity between the Russian mainland and the peninsula, which hosts Russia’s Black Sea fleet. Mr. Putin immediately ordered the construction of the bridge. But the bridge remained a weak link as Ukraine grew in military strength, backed by the West, in subsequent years.
When Mr. Putin ordered the invasion of Ukraine on February 24, 2022 (what Russia calls a ‘special military operation’), one of the military objectives, according to experts, was to secure a “land bridge” from southwest Russia to Crimea, where 77% of the local population identified Russian as their native language. Today, Russia has the land bridge extending from northeastern Ukraine through the Donbas and Kherson to Crimea. But the bridge remains a critical logistical supply link for the Russian troops in the south. And for Crimea, which depends on mainland Russia for much of its food, fuel and consumer goods, the bridge is also an economic lifeline.
Symbol of pride vs. symbol of occupation
If Russia sees the bridge as a symbol of pride, Ukraine sees it as a symbol of occupation. Russia also used Crimea as a launchpad to start its 2022 invasion. That’s why Ukrainians have repeatedly targeted the bridge. The context of the latest attack is also significant. The attack came just days ahead of President Putin’s trilateral meeting with U.S. President Donald Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping in Shenzhen.
According to Mr. Zelenskyy, Ukraine carried out a seven-stage operation, disabling Russian radars, air defence and electronic warfare systems protecting the bridge, before launching a two-tier attack with missiles and drones. The damage caused to the bridge, according to Ukraine, is a blow to Mr. Putin. Russia built a myth around it as a symbol of Crimea being ‘Russian forever’,” the Ukrainian military said in a statement after the attack.
While the damage caused to the Kerch bridge and the halt in traffic are clearly a setback to Russia, the impact would be less consequential for Moscow than what it would have been in 2022. Before the war, Russia was almost entirely dependent on the bridge to move people, goods and equipment across the Kerch strait. But today, Russia has a land corridor connecting its southeast with the Crimean Peninsula. Russia has also started ferry services across the strait which could offset the impact of any disruption in traffic on the bridge. Yet, the attack shows Ukraine’s growing capability to strike even Russia’s most fortified infrastructure as both sides continue to exchange fire.
