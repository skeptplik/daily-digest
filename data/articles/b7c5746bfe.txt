The U.K. is granting pardons to women convicted for having abortions
'Today is a historic day,' says a friend of a woman charged in 2020
The U.K. government is granting automatic pardons to all women convicted of having illegal abortions over the last 165 years.
What's more, it says it will expunge the records of those who have been investigated for abortion offences related to their own pregnancies, regardless of whether they were ultimately arrested or charged.
U.K. Labour MP Tonia Antoniazzi, who successfully fought to decriminalize abortion last year, says these clean records will bring justice to women who were "dragged from hospital beds to police cells."
"This trauma cannot be erased," Antoniazzi said. "I hope that this offers some alleviation of the pain these women have endured, enabling them to participate fully in society again, living their lives to the full, working the jobs they've always wanted to."
It started with decriminalization
The U.K.'s Abortion Act of 1967 allows legal abortions up to 24 weeks, under a specific set of criteria agreed upon by two doctors.
Until last year, women in England and Wales could be charged for procuring abortions outside that framework under a Victorian-era abortion ban, which is still on the books and carries a maximum sentence of life imprisonment.
But in April 2025, U.K. Parliament voted to amend its Crime and Policing Bill so that people could not be prosecuted for "an abortion offence relating to their own pregnancy." It recieved royal assent in April of this year, and those amendments came into force on Monday.
The measure was opposed by anti-abortion groups that argued it would open the door to abortion on demand at any stage of pregnancy.
But advocates cited what they described as an alarming increase in women being investigated for abortion-related offences, including several high-profile cases.
Nicola Packer, left, and her friend Helle Tumbridge. Packer was criminally charged in 2020 after she took legally prescribed abortion pills at home, not realizing she was past the legal threshold of 24 weeks. (Submitted by Helle Tumbridge)
Among them was Nicola Packer, who was criminally charged after she took legally prescribed abortion pills during the COVID-19 lockdown in 2020.
Packer, who argued she didn't know she was past the 24-week threshold for legal abortion, was cleared in a jury trial last year.
"It's a bittersweet day for her," Packer's friend and spokesperson, Helle Tumbridge, told As It Happens host Nil Kӧksal.
"She is, of course, pleased that this has happened … but, for her, this really never should have happened in the first place. And she will live with the trauma for the rest of her life."
Tumbridge says Packer experienced what's known as a cryptic pregnancy, which means that she didn't experience the tell-tale signs she was expecting.
As a result, she believed she was only a few weeks pregnant when she procured an over-the-phone prescription for mifepristone and misoprostol. It turned out, however, that she was 26 weeks pregnant, and the medication induced a stillbirth.
She required emergency surgery in hospital and, when she awoke, two police officers were waiting for her in the recovery room.
"Then, when she'd barely recovered, they took her off to the police station, where she was subjected to questioning and appalling treatment for a length of time," Tumbridge said.
"It took five years for her case to come to trial, so she was living all that time with this horrible trauma and stress, her life was on hold. And it was incredibly hard."
Tumbridge says her friend suffers from post-traumatic stress disorder and has authorized her to act as her voice in media interviews so she doesn't have to relive her experiences.
"While today is a historic day, it's an amazing day, and all campaigners and women everywhere are celebrating, I think for Nicola and the women involved, it is also a very difficult day," she said.
Doctors and abortion providers hail decision
The Royal College of Obstetricians and Gynaecologists (RCOG) welcomed the decision in a joint letter with several reproductive rights organizations.
"Today's pardons are an important next step," said Dr. Alison Wright, RCOG's president. "They recognize the injustice of historical convictions and mean women no longer have to live with the consequences of a criminal record for something that should never have been treated as a crime."
The British Pregnancy Advisory Service, a U.K. charity and abortion provider, also welcomed the news.
"For too long, women have faced the devastating consequences of outdated abortion laws," said Heidi Stewart, the organization's chief executive.
The Society for the Protection of Unborn Children (SPUC), a U.K. anti-abortion group that opposed decriminalization, called the pardons an attempt to impose a "barbaric ideology on a past that showed more respect for human life."
In a written statement, SPUC spokesperson Alithea Williams decried the measure as "performative virtue signalling."
The Society for the Protection of Unborn Children organized a rally against the U.K.'s decriminalization measure in March 2026. (Dan Kitwood/Getty Images)
The government has not said how many women will receive pardons or have their records cleared.
But Antoniazzi estimates that more than 100 women were investigated for abortion-related offences in the five years preceding last year's decriminalization vote.
According to the Guardian newspaper, those investigations include women, and at least one teenager, who were later determined to have had natural stillbirths and miscarriages.

The U.K. government says the pardons and record wipings will be automatic, and those affected don't need to take any actions.
Tumbridge, meanwhile, says Packer is calling for an official apology from the government, the police and the Crown Prosecution Service.
"We've had women doing jail time. It's really shocking," she said. "These cases should never have been brought to trial."
