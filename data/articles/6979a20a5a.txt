After decades of being dismissed, a group of particles called paraparticles have been seen emerging inside a quantum simulator. Coming only a year after a mathematical breakthrough in our understanding of paraparticles, this experiment complicates our view of the quantum world.
Quantum particles are classified by what happens when two of them swap places and by how many can have the same quantum state simultaneously. Bosons, for example, can be swapped at no penalty, and infinitely many can share a quantum state. Fermions are their opposite; swapping them incurs a change in their quantum states, and two or more fermions can never occupy the same quantum state.
In the 1950s, researchers theorised other types too, but it seemed impossible to experimentally distinguish them from fermions and bosons. But in 2025, Kaden Hazzard at Rice University in Texas and Zhiyuan Wang at the Perimeter Institute in Canada argued that this doesn’t have to be the case.
Their mathematical work made the case for the possibility of finding signs of paraparticles in experiments. Now, Max Hays at Massachusetts Institute of Technology (MIT) and his colleagues have used a quantum simulator do exactly that.
“They’ve seen emergent paraparticles,” says Hazzard. “They have a system whose dynamics obeys exactly the same mathematical equations that a paraparticle is expected to satisfy.” He says he is surprised by how quickly the team managed to complete this work.
The researchers used 16 qubits, which are the building blocks of quantum computers, made from tiny superconducting circuits and arranged in a grid. By using electromagnetic interactions, the team connected qubits into pairs, like rungs on a ladder. Each rung behaved like a paraparticle.
Three tests confirmed this: the researchers swapped two paraparticles, made them crash into each other and, ultimately, checked how they pile together. It all took a year and lots of careful control and calibration of the qubits, says Cora Barrett, also at MIT, who worked on the experiment.
“This work provides a proof of concept that paraparticles are not merely theoretical curiosities,” says Kristian Hauser Villegas at the University of the Philippines Diliman.
Unlike a simulation on a conventional computer, the new experiment uses the qubits’ quantum characters as building blocks of paraparticles, says Hauser Villegas. It may even be impossible to faithfully reproduce this on a conventional computer.
“In a regular computer, you are writing code and it’s very abstract; there’s no way in which these things are actually really existing in the computer,” says Hays. “In this case, you can say the particle is right at this spot physically.”
Going forward, the team wants to expand their experiment to include paraparticles that interact with each other, as well as paraparticles in more complicated geometrical configurations, says Hays.
Many open questions about paraparticles remain, and these more complex experiments will stand a chance of answering some of them, says Wang. “This is the just the beginning of an exciting journey,” he says.
