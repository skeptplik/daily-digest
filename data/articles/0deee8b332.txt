COP31 climate talks collapse after member states reject deal for not mentioning fossil fuels
First time since 2009 that UN's annual climate summit ended without an agreement
Climate talks collapsed on Sunday at COP31 in Turkey, ending the longest-ever United Nations climate summit with no deal to show for it.
For more than two weeks, representatives from nearly 200 countries, scientists, businesses and more gathered in Antalya, Turkey, for this year's COP31 conference.
The annual UN Climate Change Conference brings together member states to negotiate — and ideally commit to — climate policies that aim to curb global warming. It's the main vehicle through which countries co-ordinate climate action.
But this year, no agreement was reached — despite extending heavily into overtime this weekend following Friday's deadline — after the European Union and others rejected the draft put forward by Turkey and Australia.
"There is nothing to do, we did all that we could. Since there are 196 countries, they will make the decision and we will respect their decision," Turkey's COP31 president, Murat Kurum, said. "There will be no new text from now on."
The lack of a deal means there will be no new action on climate disaster and adaptation financing for developing countries hardest hit by the impacts of climate change as the world is expected to overshoot the 1.5 C target set by the 2015 Paris Agreement, nor any further attempts to rein in the main human drivers of the climate crisis.
It's the first time UN climate talks have failed since 2009, when the conference was held in Copenhagen for COP15.
Fossil fuels weren't mentioned in final draft
Multiple members said the proposed agreement didn't go far enough to match the gravity of the climate crisis — in part because there was no mention of fossil fuels anywhere in the text, despite scientific consensus that they drive global warming.
"At a point where the world urgently needs action to avert disastrous levels of global warming, the draft cover decision falls short," said a joint European Union statement, delivered by Ireland's Environment Minister Darragh O'Brien and Wopke Hoekstra, European commissioner for climate, net zero and clean growth.
"We will not endorse a cover decision that does not even mention fossil fuels, the driver of climate change.... And we will not accept a COP that greenwashes fossil fuels."
Kurum voiced his disagreement with the decision, saying "you should not hurt people in the world who really expect something out of this by just being hung up on one word or phrase."
Oil-producing nations like Saudi Arabia reportedly lobbied to leave out mentions of fossil fuels and instead pushed for the deal to focus on carbon capture and storage technologies.
A representative for the Saudi delegation told the summit he was surprised that member states voiced resistance to the technology, which is heavily pushed by industry lobbyists.
"If indeed the climate challenge, as some have mentioned today, is so important, it makes no sense that solutions, concrete solutions ... will be taken off the table," he said.
Environmentalists have long voiced concerns about overreliance on the tech, saying it could prolong global reliance on fossil fuels. Experts have also warned that carbon capture, while important, can't roll back global warming on its own.
Russia was also against mentioning fossil fuels in the agreement.
Panama said it rejected the deal because it failed to acknowledge the scale and urgency of the climate crisis, while Norway's Climate Minister Sigrun Gjerløw Aasland said the draft "takes us backwards."
Canada leaves disappointed
Jeanne-Marie Huddleston, Canada's chief climate negotiator, expressed "deep disappointment" that a consensus could not be reached.
Environment Minister Julie Dabrusin said in a statement late Sunday afternoon that the outcome was "not what we hoped" but added that Canada's "commitment to the Paris Agreement, multilateralism and international institutions remains steadfast."
Former environment minister Catherine McKenna told CBC News Network following the conference that there is "no way" to have a COP agreement that doesn't mention fossil fuels.
"You need to mention the problem if you're going to actually get to solutions," she said.
Climate finance another sticking point
Developing countries, many of which are already struggling with the worst effects of global warming, said the deal didn't allocate enough money to adequately respond to the climate crisis.
"Climate finance must be fully mobilized to drive needed scale and speed in mitigation, adaptation, technology transfer, capacity building and loss and damage," the Philippines delegation said. "A target without means is a promise without a pathway."
Kenya echoed the concern and said Africa wasn't adequately represented.
"Africa is not named. The decision rightly speaks of least developed countries and small island states, but not once of Africa's special needs and circumstances," Kenya's delegation said. "Those who took the lead in emissions must take the lead in support. We ask our partners to bring the finance that makes this ambition possible."
Reactions from others trickling in
Reactions from officials, activists and experts already started flooding the internet early Sunday, with many supporting the EU's decision to reject the deal.
"Countries from across regions were right to reject the colossal betrayal that the COP31 co-presidencies tried to pass off as climate action," wrote Amnesty International climate justice policy adviser Ann Harrison. "They refused to accept a deeply unjust text that failed even to name fossil fuels while opening the door to risky technologies that could prolong their use."
The UN Secretary General António Guterres wrote on X that he's "deeply disappointed" that COP31 ended without consensus.
"I share the very profound frustration of all those who expected governments to rise to the moment the climate crisis demands. But the setback does not change the facts," he wrote. "The take-home message from COP31 is clear: The world must do much more to confront the climate crisis."
This year, the stakes were high
Notably, this year's summit was the first to take place after the UN's environmental body in October confirmed the rise in global temperature was set to exceed 1.5 C — a threshold countries agreed at Paris climate talks over a decade ago to try not to exceed, and beyond which extreme weather impacts intensify, including through heat waves, floods and droughts.
But it's also marked by competing priorities that threaten to sideline climate action. The collapse this time is against the backdrop of competing crises, including wars and public outcry over the cost of living, that have distracted political attention from the problems of a warming climate largely caused by human activity.
What's next for COP31?
That's it for COP31. The annual summit requires consensus when finalizing agreements. That means: no consensus, no deal. Existing frameworks and agreements established in previous years are still in effect.
The next UN climate summit will take place next year in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia.
