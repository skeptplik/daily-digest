Three French police officers who beat a black music producer they said was not wearing a face mask during the Covid pandemic have been found guilty of intentional, racist violence.
The brutal assault on Michel Zecler, now 47, was caught on security cameras in his building in 2020 and triggered outrage in France over police brutality. The trial this month was considered a turning point for the recognition of racist violence.
The officers were sentenced to between one and two years in prison, with additional years suspended. They were banned for life from serving in the police.
The officers had stopped Zecler outside his Paris music studio on 21 November 2020, accusing him of failing to wear a mandatory face mask. They pushed him into the entrance of his office and violently beat him in an assault recorded by his studio’s security cameras.
Zecler sustained about 30 punches and truncheon blows to the face and body over a five-minute period, according to the investigation. He was left with craniofacial trauma, a wound to the lip and scalp, and a complete rupture of his biceps tendon, leaving him unable to work for 45 days.
Zecler told the court the officers used a racist slur against him on several occasions during the beating. He said the attack had “completely devastated him” and had left a lasting physical and psychological impact.
The three officers, Aurelien Lapersonne, 29, Pierre Plantier, 37, and Philippe Tondeux, 50, were found guilty of breaking into Zecler’s studio and committing intentional violence in a group. The judges decided racism was an aggravating factor in the attack.
The officers were also found guilty of forgery after they tried to cover up their actions by falsifying an official police report saying Zecler had been violent towards them. The police used that report to accuse Zecler of attacking them and trying to grab one of their weapons, but the video footage did not back up the claims, showing he was not violent.
The state prosecutor said that without the CCTV footage, the false police report on Zecler could have resulted in him being convicted of attacking a police officer in what would have been a serious miscarriage of justice.
The state prosecutor, who asked not to be named in the media, told the court the case had caused “immense harm to Michel Zecler but also to society as a whole”.
She said the officers had “betrayed” their uniform. She told the judges: “I ask you to believe Michel Zecler, to recognise him as a victim of these racist insults.”
The three police officers, who had not realised their attack was being recorded on security cameras, had apologised in court for beating Zecler, but denied racism.
Zecler’s lawyers and supporters said the trial had shone a spotlight on police violence in France, which victims and researchers say is systemic but authorities and law enforcement unions say is limited to isolated cases. Many members of the public who had come to support Zecler and watch the trial said it was an important moment in confronting institutionalised racism.
One of Zecler’s lawyers, Serge Money, said while summing up the case: “A verdict can force a society to really look at an injustice that up until now had been tolerated, minimised, and I dare to use the word: institutionalised.”
He said: “The Zecler trial is the trial of impunity, for all those who before him and after him will experience the same thing.”
Money told the court: “I want you to give voice to all those who didn’t have security cameras, and to all those who were not believed.”
Nine young performers who had been rehearsing in the basement at Zecler’s studio had been violently arrested when they came to assist Zecler.
Their lawyer, Seydi Ba, told the court that France needed to know how “police violence, lies and racism” would be considered by a court.
