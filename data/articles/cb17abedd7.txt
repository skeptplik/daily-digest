You might have heard by now, but Americans are quite lonely, especially if they’re young.
The kind of low-effort, high-frequency hanging out we used to rely on – dropping by for dinner, meeting up at the park, watching each other’s kids – has all but evaporated from our lives, leaving us unmoored from the social fabric that underpins our most basic wellbeing. (For reference, chronic loneliness has a comparable impact on long-term health to smoking.)
And if you’re looking for a culprit, there are many to blame: the dissolution of third spaces and encroachment of car culture, the declining participation in social networks such as churches and unions, and the harsh economic conditions that have people working longer hours for less money with each passing year.
And data shows that sheer disposable income is not as strongly correlated with social life as one might think: we are often completely eschewing the budget-friendly forms of contact that used to be standard in favor of being alone and scrolling on our phones.
But, as someone who literally wrote the book on getting together, and doing so across all income and age brackets, I would like to posit another reason why the American social fabric has so rapidly dissolved.
It’s not just that phones make it more appealing to scroll aimlessly than to have a conversation – it’s that they’ve made us more selfish, less community-minded people. Nearly every time I post a video about hosting, for example, my comments are filled with the same refrain: What’s the point of spending all this time and effort if no one actually shows up?
And the concern that you might go through all the trouble of planning a party just to sit in an empty room surrounded by food and decorations is not irrational.
When I posted this recent video about the psychic damage inflicted on a host by no-showing on an RSVP, it quickly racked up thousands of commenters sharing their experiences with the phenomenon. I am tagged daily in videos of people who did what I firmly believe we all should do: they took the risk of trying to get people together in real life, and they were left feeling humiliated by the realization that their party was simply an inconvenience that could be tossed overboard at the last minute.
And with social media and party apps making it easier than ever to invite anyone you’ve ever met, and to perpetually “maybe” your entire calendar so you can make a game-time call as to what invitation you deem worthy (as this excellent Substack explores), we have entered a dual crisis. Fewer people than ever are willing to host, and when they do, it’s a massive gamble: who will actually show up?
While there are, very occasionally, legitimate reasons to bail on a gathering at the last minute, we’ve also gotten very good at leveraging the language of mental health to lean into our most selfish impulses.
We speak in terms of boundaries and self-care about actions that don’t just devastate the person on the other side of our unreliability, but also sap at our own mental health, as community is arguably more important to it than our own immediate comfort.
The truth is, whether it’s exercising, spending time outside, showing up to a social commitment, or opting for more nutritious food over some quick takeout, it is often in doing the less immediately comfortable or enjoyable thing that we are truly caring for ourselves and those around us.
And when it comes to gathering, the social isolation factor is very real: making and keeping platonic bonds is a skill, and the longer you are out of practice, the harder and more scary it becomes to start again. People are losing friends, and the process can accelerate until the entire concept of socializing feels like a threat.
It has never been easier in those moments to get under a blanket, make up a thin excuse for not showing up to a commitment, and reassure yourself that you have engaged in a powerful, therapeutic exercise by choosing to watch a screen again instead of literally anything else.
We increasingly speak of gathering in terms of self-sacrifice and effort, but, as this meme deftly satirizes, the thing we’re talking about – making friends and then hanging out with them – is supposed to be the fun part. It’s supposed to be the reward for all the work we do, both professionally and in our daily lives.
Instead, the act has slipped so far from our cultural routines in just two generations that we no longer understand it as a leisure activity. (The performance of ultra-perfect hosting and homemaking on social media doesn’t help, but that’s a subject for another day.)
Simply put, we have so radically devalued not just what it means to spend time with people we love, but to show respect for the people who facilitate it, that we have enabled a full-blown flake epidemic. And while I’m not willing to say that being a chronic flaker makes you a bad person, I am willing to say that it should not be acceptable – to you, or to anyone else.
There are so many problems to fully address our hosting and loneliness crises, but none are more immediately solvable on a personal level than holding ourselves accountable. If we say we are going to do something, it is our responsibility to actually show up.
No, it doesn’t matter that you don’t really feel like it, or that it’s raining outside: grab an umbrella, put on a smile (and maybe pick up a nice little hostess gift on the way), and go be a part of the community we all desperately need to rebuild.
- Chelsea Fagan is an author and the co-founder of The Financial Diet
