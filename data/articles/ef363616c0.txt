France aims to build reserves of critical minerals and urges Europe to invest more. Researcher Frédéric Jeannin says such stocks must shield the country from market shocks and prepare its industry for possible armed conflict.
An end-of-term announcement, as France and Europe face tensions that are disrupting global trade and supply chains. Emmanuel Macron is launching the creation of reserves of rare earths and critical metals to reduce the country's dependence and anticipate shortages. Three key sectors are concerned: defence, the car industry and aerospace.
This plan, seen by Euronews, was announced at the start of the week during a meeting at the Élysée called "Protect France", which brought together around fifteen industry bosses.
The task of setting up this mechanism of strategic stocks and "joint purchasing" has been entrusted to the Caisse des dépôts, the state's financial arm.
Companies will have to play their part in diversifying their supplies or risk seeing their public support reduced. Mechanisms of "price guarantees" will also be offered "to give producers visibility".
But many questions remain unanswered. At this stage, the challenge is to define precise storage scenarios, the players involved and which minerals are most urgently needed.
Frédéric Jeannin, a researcher at the IRIS think tank and co-head of the Energy and raw materials programme, stresses the importance of this initial work: "The state needs to draw up a list of what is strategic, which companies we want to supply," he told Euronews in an interview.
Physical or virtual reserves, based on purchase commitments? The expert says "we can have both". He explains that the interest of this "insurance" is to allow the country to face two types of threat: "a security shock or a shock on the market".
"We wonder whether China does not have a better understanding of our industry than we do"
According to Frédéric Jeannin, these stocks must first help to "strengthen our resilience" in the face of international raw materials markets, where access to resources is "less and less certain, at least at an economically acceptable price".
China currently dominates the sector, particularly thanks to its vast refining capacity, the researcher recalls.
While the Élysée presents dependence on supplies as a possible "tool of coercion and threat", Frédéric Jeannin sees in these reserves a "safety cushion". They would help to absorb price fluctuations that could be triggered by decisions from Beijing.
China has a legal arsenal that allows it to block the export of its critical raw materials depending on their end use and end recipient, whenever Beijing considers that these exports run counter to its interests.
Export licences enable it to filter sales on a case-by-case basis, without having to declare a total embargo that would penalise its own mining companies.
The researcher also points to the level of transparency demanded from buyers regarding the processing and value chain of materials imported from China, "up to demanding information that falls under industrial secrecy".
"We wonder whether China does not have a better understanding of our industry than we do," observes Frédéric Jeannin.
Reserves to hold out in case of war
Frédéric Jeannin identifies a second factor: the prospect of an open conflict with Russia.
The objective is first of all very concrete: "It is essential to ensure that raw material will be available on our soil, ideally with physical stocks, to supply the defence industry".
"But there is also a psychological dimension," he adds: it is a way of telling a potential "offensive actor" that France is able to cope with a crisis, "with stocks available, with components available, with raw material available, which would allow us to hold out for a certain period".
For how long remains to be seen. Although the scheme is intended to be long-term, the Élysée says it has already "brought out" nearly 7 billion euros of investment in France.
Even if French and American industries are not comparable in size, the United States offers a point of reference. Last February, Washington announced the creation of its strategic reserve, "Project Vault" ("projet coffre-fort"), endowed with 12 billion dollars. It is meant to cover the equivalent of two months of national consumption.
"Seven billion is a good start. We are talking about amounts that will run into billions of euros," notes Frédéric Jeannin. But it is still essential to know who will benefit from these investments and according to which criteria, he insists.
"What companies need to bear in mind, and I would even say what citizens need to bear in mind, is that this resilience is necessary today, and that it has a cost," the economist stresses.
"Our industries must factor in a resilience cost to guarantee that we have what we need on our territory. It will be more expensive, but it is necessary to protect us in case of crisis," he concludes.
"Europe is making a strategic mistake"
Warnings about the need to build up strategic stocks are not new, including at European level.
The European Union identifies 34 critical raw materials, 17 of them considered "strategic", for which demand is expected to exceed supply in the coming years.
For these strategic materials, dependence on third countries is high. China supplies the EU with 85% of its samarium which, combined with cobalt, forms magnets that are essential for electric motors; Turkey, 99% of its boron, used in particular in ballistic protection; and South Africa, 41% of its manganese, indispensable for steel production, according to a map (source in French) from the European Commission.
In early November, Members of the European Parliament backed (source in French) the creation of a Competitiveness Fund, currently under negotiation as part of the EU budget for 2028-2034. France wants to ring-fence 25 billion euros within the envelope of around 300 billion, in order to secure prices and build up European reserves.
But asserting France's priorities in the European budget is proving difficult. Thursday's extraordinary European summit even turned into a psychodrama, laying bare the divisions among the Twenty-Seven. The trigger was the proposal from the President of the European Council, Antonio Costa, to cut around 200 billion euros from the initial budget draft submitted by the Commission.
Emmanuel Macron condemned what he called a "strategic mistake" in response to the cuts backed in particular by German Chancellor Friedrich Merz.
"We are going to register a drop-off in European public investment compared with China and the United States," the French president said at a press conference in Brussels.
"When it comes to having a budget that matches our ambitions, there is nobody to support us," he lamented.
Emmanuel Macron has threatened to pull out of the budget negotiations if no agreement is in sight by the end of the year. He refuses to let these talks drag on and encroach on the French presidential election, whose first round is scheduled for 18 April 2027.
The fate of France's strategic stocks will also depend on whoever succeeds him at the Élysée.
