Researchers recently identified a first of its kind: A star slightly less massive than the Sun spiraled into and merged with a puffy supergiant star at the outskirts of our neighboring Andromeda galaxy. They nicknamed the newly single, potentially “rejuvenated” star Bao. Their NASA-funded discovery was then bolstered by quick-turn observations from NASA’s James Webb Space Telescope that were carefully cross-referenced with extensive data from telescopes in space and on the ground.
“Stellar mergers are very, very difficult to observe in real time because they are hard to predict,” said Viraj Karambelkar, the first author of a new paper in Nature published Wednesday and a NASA Hubble Fellow at Columbia University in New York. “However, we noticed something unusual was going on in 2023 and continued to monitor the system to see what it would do. Then, in 2025, it erupted! Webb observed Bao only 47 days after the stars’ eruption peaked, the earliest ever follow-up of an event like this. The telescope’s data helped us characterize what the system looked like before, during, and immediately after the merger, which is absolutely mind-blowing.”

Before their transformation, the two stars spent millennia placidly orbiting one another as a binary pair. But once the smaller star came into contact with the surface of the giant star, their “unification” happened after only seven orbits (about seven years). Other known binary star mergers, featuring two smaller stars, occurred after thousands of orbits.
“We thought an outburst from a such an extended star would take years,” said co-author Nadia Blagorodnova, an associate professor at the University of Barcelona in Spain. “One day, its brightness started rising and within a week, it peaked!”
“When a companion star begins to interact with the outer surface of the primary star, it produces a subtle effect — a slow brightening, which we clearly saw in Bao’s data,” said co-author Kishalay De, an assistant professor at Columbia University in New York. “As the companion continues its orbit, it keeps peeling off the primary star’s outer layers, and that gas flows away into the surrounding space.”
This is the beginning of a high-stakes showdown some binary stars go through, known as a common envelope evolution. The gas between the stars at this stage is known as a common envelope because its outer layer is shared between them. In this system, the primary star engulfed the smaller star and the companion plummeted, spiraling through the envelope as it was robbed of orbital energy by drag. While Webb’s data proved the stars merged, a merger wasn’t the only possible outcome for this system.
In other systems, both stars survive the process — shedding a lot of mass along the way, but remaining independent in tight orbits. Over time, such surviving pairs can transform into neutron stars and black holes, explode as supernovae, produce ripples in space-time known as gravitational waves, and create millisecond pulsars.
Hitting “replay”
The team reconstructed what happened to Bao by updating their ongoing simulations. They found the model that matched Webb’s mid-infrared observations, which detailed the dust shed by the more massive primary star over centuries.
The team concluded that prior to the eruption, Bao’s primary star dwarfed the Sun: It was seven times as massive, 350 times the radius, and 18,000 times brighter — making “Big Bao” an appropriate nickname.
“It was an absolute beast of a star, completely outshining its companion in the data,” said co-author Karel Temmink, a Ph.D. student at Radboud University in Nijmegen, Netherlands, who led the simulations.
Their modeling resulted in the first mass estimate for the smaller, now demolished, companion. Compared to our Sun, they found that “Little Bao” was likely less massive (about 0.7 solar masses), with a slightly smaller radius and fainter.
After revealing the masses of the pair, the model showed there might be leftover energy for Little Bao to “flee” the scene. However, further analysis determined that Little Bao was dragged into Big Bao's core. Their encounter was “almost like a bomb exploding inside a living star,” De said.
What does the new star — now simply Bao — look like today? Only future observations will identify how its “heft” settles.

Reconstructing what happened
While Webb’s data allowed the team to measure how much mass was ejected during this process and how efficient the process of common envelope evolution was, Karambelkar’s team also used observations that span ultraviolet, visible, and near-infrared light from several telescopes to watch the events, often as they unfolded. (In fact, they published their first paper right before the eruption in 2025.)
Bao first caught the team’s attention in February 2023, when they saw an unusual variable star in data from NASA’s retired NEOWISE (Near-Earth Object Wide-Field Infrared Survey Explorer) mission. Before 2018, the object had a consistent luminosity. Observations from the Wide-field Infrared Transient Explorer (WINTER) in September 2024 showed that Bao was not only beginning to brighten, but also spew dust.
When NASA’s SPHEREx (Spectro-Photometer for the History of the Universe, Epoch of Reionization and Ices Explorer) serendipitously saw Bao fade rapidly in visible light and brighten in infrared light during a routine survey in July 2025, the researchers knew it was an indicator of unusual activity. The Zwicky Transient Facility detected the eruption on Oct. 17, 2025, watching the stars rapidly brighten and radically heat up.
In November 2025, the team received fast-turn observations from NASA’s now-retired Neil Gehrels Swift Observatory and NOIRLab’s Gemini North telescope in Hawaii. Webb’s observations were taken on Dec. 24, 2025, as part of a just-in-time Director’s Discretionary Time program.
New path for future research
The team’s analysis of Bao, formally known as AT 2025abao, offers an approach to reconstructing the events: They conducted their analysis and made observations of the stars’ merger in real time. That allowed them to measure how efficient common envelope evolution is. In other cases, astronomers have worked to carefully reverse-engineer events that may have happened hundreds or even millions of years ago.
The new finding comes about just as NASA’s recently launched Nancy Grace Roman Space Telescope is about to conduct vast publicly available surveys. Its Galactic Bulge Time-Domain Survey will yield a huge number of new transient events that could benefit from similar analyses. “There’s going to be a large influx of similar discoveries just ahead,” Karambelkar said.
