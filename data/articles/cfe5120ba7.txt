For some patients, psychedelic treatment brings new troubles in its aftermath
Psychedelics could transform mental health care, but some advocates and researchers say there needs to be more focus on the minority of people with persistent troubles after using these drugs.
SACHA PFEIFFER, HOST:
Next year, the Food and Drug Administration could approve psychedelics like psilocybin for mental health treatment, and other drugs are in the pipeline. When people are under the influence, doctors monitor them closely. But as NPR's Will Stone reports, some worry there's not enough attention to how people fare in the aftermath.
WILL STONE, BYLINE: In theory, Sin knew what to expect before she took a nasal spray of the fast-acting psychedelic 5-MeO-DMT. She'd enrolled in a clinical trial testing the drug for treatment-resistant depression.
SIN: Honestly, whatever you tell people before they actually experience it, it's not going to make any sense. You'll feel like you'll be tied to a rocket ship, for example, means nothing to me.
STONE: Sin (ph) asked NPR not to use her full name because of concerns that sharing her experience could affect her employment.
SIN: I, at some point, had, like, nothing. So it was all white. OK, have I died? Is this what death is like?
STONE: When she came back to reality, she stepped out of the clinic and was soon riding the underground in London during rush hour. It was jarring, and in the months that followed, readjusting to life was hard. She felt raw.
SIN: Like a very thin skin almost to your emotions.
STONE: The persistent depression she'd lived with began to lift. In its place, though, came intense anxiety and panic attacks. At one point, she wondered to her therapist...
SIN: Did I do something wrong in choosing to do this trial?
STONE: Laura Zam has asked a similar question for years about her husband, Kurt. He was in remission from pancreatic cancer when the two of them enrolled in an MDMA trial for cancer survivors and their caregivers. Zam says, initially, her husband was elated with the experience, but soon she became concerned.
LAURA ZAM: His brain, I noticed, just worked a little bit differently. He stopped sleeping, couldn't turn off this rumination and worry.
STONE: Eventually, the situation spiraled.
ZAM: To such a degree that he was institutionalized five times over the course of the next six months. He kept having to go back to the hospital because of this suicidal ideation.
STONE: She says those involved in his care didn't seem to know enough about psychedelics to say whether that could have been responsible and what to do. It took years to find him the right help.
ZAM: He's still pretty sick, but he is coming back little by little.
STONE: Psychedelics are being heralded for their potential to treat devastating mental illnesses. But advocates like Pedram Dara worry this other side is being overlooked - that some people can end up destabilized, with persistent problems and maybe worse off months later.
PEDRAM DARA: Like, a safe session or safe dosing session doesn't mean safe outcome.
STONE: Dara went through his own challenges after MDMA-assisted therapy in a trial in Canada. The patient-led initiative he started called Psychedelic Lived Experiences is holding a summit exploring what happens to people after taking these drugs. They also run a peer support group.
DARA: Whether it worked for you or didn't, there was this need to connect with others who had gone through it just so that you could feel understood.
STONE: For him and others, the aftermath can be mixed.
DARA: It's not so binary, like, oh, this was a miracle cure or treatment or completely harmed the individual.
ROMAN PALITSKY: We hear these kinds of stories many times a week.
STONE: That's Roman Palitsky, a research psychologist at Emory University School of Medicine.
PALITSKY: These are very effective therapies, and they can help a lot of people. Talking about adverse effects is not contra to their effectiveness, and we just need to be candid about it.
STONE: Clinical trials do track adverse events. Serious ones include suicidality, prolonged psychotic episodes and medical emergencies that require hospitalization. One major review suggests these are very rare in clinical trials but that they are also underreported. Palitsky agrees there are big blind spots. For example, people aren't followed long enough, and the full array of troubles aren't being captured - everything from existential distress to anxiety to feeling disassociated.
PALITSKY: Psychedelics just have this broad-spectrum, robust effect on so many domains of people's experience. And so our assessment of adverse effects have to keep up.
STONE: His team at Emory is running a study tracking people with these problems for a year to get a more detailed picture. Jules Evans directs the Challenging Psychedelic Experiences Project, a nonprofit that researches these issues.
JULES EVANS: Many people, even who offer psychedelic treatments, legal psychedelic treatments, have no idea that people can get into these difficulties for weeks or months or years afterwards. And so when it occurs, they're bewildered.
STONE: While there aren't evidence-based treatments for many of these post-psychedelic challenges, Evans says therapy does seem to help. For Sin, who at one point said she was worried she might have broken her brain, it did slowly get better. She's grateful for how the psychedelic treatment helped her depression, but she was unprepared for the monthslong, sometimes agonizing recovery process.
SIN: When you read the news, obviously, it's all about, oh, it treats depression. Great. But then what does it leave you with? It's just the unpredictability of it, I think, is not necessarily appreciated.
STONE: She hopes the risks don't get lost in the rush to make these drugs more available. Will Stone, NPR News.
