A lex Prager, known for her theatrical photography, stumbled upon “YearQuil,” a fake twist on NyQuil that lets you “sleep until 2021.” This doomscroll discovery stuck with Prager and her sister, Vanessa, also an artist, who then spent the rest of the Covid lockdown writing a screenplay based on that idea. The two imagined a device designed to help an unhappy woman escape everyday life, and the result is her feature-film debut, DreamQuil.
“I really wanted to direct movies,” Prager tells Bazaar on a video call from her Los Angeles home. “We just dove into this idea of a woman deciding to be willingly put to sleep so she doesn’t have to experience the uncomfortable sides of life.… We loved the idea of taking the easy way and the problems that come with that, and this cautionary tale.”
DreamQuil chronicles the story of one family living in a hyperpolluted near future where daily stressors feel insurmountable. Carol (Elizabeth Banks) spends much of her time hiding in her egg-shaped virtual-reality pod, not only escaping career pressures and the murky smog outside but also ignoring her strained relationships with her husband, Gary (John C. Reilly), and son (Toby Larsen). Demoralizing advertisements flood Carol’s screens (everywhere in this digital dystopia), eventually persuading her to enroll in a virtual wellness treatment program: the “DreamQuil,” where she gives away her life rights for temporary reprieve. As part of the program, the company appropriates her likeness to create a chipper AI doppelgänger (Carol Two), which gradually tries to usurp her life entirely. The film, which premiered at South by Southwest in Austin last March before its theatrical release in late October, leverages Prager’s opulent visual style to confront pressing contemporary issues, especially privacy and corporatism.
Prager, now 47, dropped out of high school and learned photography without formal training. She turned her camera on people living in her hometown neighborhood, Los Feliz. The artist channeled her love for film, particularly the golden age of Hollywood and film noir, into sweeping mise-en-scène photographs imbued with a distinctly California sensibility. Some images feature tender close-ups with kaleidoscopic color palettes; others capture hyperpopulated streets littered with uncanny and surreal treasures. She soon began exhibiting her work at galleries worldwide, and she’s currently preparing for a solo show at Lehmann Maupin in New York for March 2027.
This command over composition and color carries directly into her film work. The artist’s short films like Despair (2010), starring Bryce Dallas Howard, and Uncanny Valley (2018), starring Cate Blanchett, show Prager’s deft understanding of the screen and her ability to stage illusory, suspenseful scenes that convey emotion to the audience. This hypersensory storytelling technique fuels DreamQuil’s 84-minute run time, which Prager meticulously composed. “I storyboarded every frame of the movie before I even brought my cinematographer on,” she says, adding that she drew visual inspiration from Douglas Sirk melodramas and Technicolor classics, including a lush field of poppies referencing The Wizard of Oz. Each scene pops with Technicolor palettes, but beyond the decadent surface, Prager shows how color in her work is intentionally symbolic.
“Color can communicate so much,” Prager says. “The red [of Carol’s dress] was intended as symbolic for her humanity—blood red. Then the cold blue [dress] of Carol Two that she wears is representative of these tech companies and technology really, and how it’s unfeeling—the opposite of being human.”
The luscious color palette in her photography and cinematic work often means to “soften the blow” and invite people into difficult conversations about the state of the world. “I’ve always done that with my photography,” she says. “It’s been a way to talk about things that are really challenging for me or things that are going on in the world that are hard to comprehend because they’re so dark and terrifying. It brings color and beauty; it adds a layer of delight and softness, and underneath you can unpack the layers depending on who you are and where you’re at in your life.”
The issues she examines in DreamQuil are indeed thorny. Like The Stepford Wives (2004), Carol’s story wrestles with familiar themes: the demands of femininity and marriage and control in a patriarchal society. But the Prager sisters narrowed in on a more incisive critique of corporatism and AI. The advertisements, in particular, visually assault Carol with tailor-made pleas from businesses preying on her insecurities. “It seemed like this idea of being very far in the future—now, it seems like it’s happening already,” she says. At every juncture, the villain stems from what lies behind the callous Carol Two robot, a sinister system creeping up to steal our humanity.
Prager wanted to specifically target data privacy when writing this script. To prepare, she read extensively, citing books such as Shoshana Zuboff’s The Age of Surveillance Capitalism (2018) and B.F. Skinner’s Beyond Freedom and Dignity (1971). “[Skinner’s] philosophy was first put to physical use in Las Vegas with gambling, like ‘bing, bing, bing, bing.’ All the sounds and music and lights—it works,” Prager explains. “What’s happened with the internet is it’s been gamified. It’s really addictive. The conversation about data privacy … is uncomfortable for people to confront because they do it on a daily basis. They’re giving their privacy to these unknown entities that are uncaring to the individual, and they’re allowing themselves to be sold.” The DreamQuil company in the film seems harmless, almost a full-on altruistic savior for the stressed. But this marketing persuades a broken Carol, who then nearly loses everything. DreamQuil is, above all, about exploitation.
“It’s more about data privacy and using people’s data to exploit them,” she says. “It’s very insidious because we’re our own worst enemy and we’re giving our privacy away. Most of us are like, ‘Well, I don’t have anything to hide, so I’m just going to accept it,’ but in doing that, you’re actually exploiting yourself and allowing yourself to be the commodity for these huge tech companies that have no face and are very much like, there’s no real interest in our human expression and humanity.”
Prager fixates on our shared humanity in everything she does. She often examines the thin line between artificiality and reality through saturated, populous portraits. The themes coursing through her body of work all coalesce in DreamQuil, a critique of the changing world around us. Her next film is more personal. “I grew up in Los Angeles. I was born and raised in Los Feliz, and I shoot everything within around a 10-block radius of there,” she says. “This will be a sinister love letter to Los Angeles.”
