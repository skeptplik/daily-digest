Senator-elect Troy Jackson backs Van Hollen over Schumer for Senate majority leader
Senator-elect Troy Jackson, of Maine, has endorsed Chris Van Hollen for Senate majority leader, a blow to the current minority leader, Chuck Schumer, ahead of a vote among Senate Democrats next Tuesday.
The Maryland senator has successfully cast himself as a “change” candidate following Democrats’ sweeping success in the midterms, whereas Schumer, who has led Senate Democrats for the last decade, is seen as the “establishment” figure in the race, representing the status quo.
Van Hollen and the argument for a new generation of leadership has received backing from Bernie Sanders, incoming senators Abdul El-Sayed and Peggy Flanagan and others, as well as some celebrity backing from the likes of Robert De Niro, Jane Fonda, and others.
“I’ve talked with Mainers about what they need from the Senate, and I believe it’s time for a change in leadership. I’ll be voting for Senator Van Hollen,” Jackson said in a statement. “When we spoke, I told him Maine needs a seat at the table when the caucus decides what gets funded and what we’re going to fight for. I was impressed by his vision for the caucus, and I came away confident he’ll take Maine’s priorities seriously.
“From one end of Maine to the other, I heard the same thing. People are working harder, falling further behind, and getting tired of being told to wait. Senator Van Hollen and I agree that it’s time to end the filibuster, pass Medicare for All, and give working people a raise by increasing the minimum wage. I want a leader who’ll bring those fights to the floor and make every senator say where they stand.
“Mainers voted for change in November, and they ought to get it. Whoever leads this caucus, I’ll fight like hell to make sure the Senate takes on the problems Mainers sent me to Washington to solve.”

Trump administration tries to blame Obama for explosives in Iowa US army was concerned about in 2004
Faced with criticism for the US army’s potentially hazardous detonation on Wednesday of a stockpile of the bomb-making material lead azide in Middleton, Iowa, the Trump administration made a concerted effort on Wednesday to place the blame on former president Barack Obama.
In public comments, Donald Trump, JD Vance and Stephen Miller, all claimed, without evidence, that the hazardous explosives were improperly stored at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant (IAAAP) in 2009.
They made this claim despite the fact that the US army said in a 2014 publication still available online that a project to move away from the use of lead azide “started in 2004 in response to the Department of Defense’s concerns over a 40-year-old stockpile of lead azide deteriorating at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown, Iowa.”
In 2004, Obama was an Illinois state lawmaker running for the US Senate and George W Bush was president.
The White House did not reply to a request to explain how it came to the conclusion that a problem identified in 2004 could be called “a dangerous mess the Obama Administration left behind,” or say what action the first Trump administration took to address the problem between 2017 and 2021.
The dangers of the lead azide at the Iowa plant were noted decades earlier, in a report from the US army’s toxic and hazardous materials agency, published in January 1980, when Jimmy Carter was president and Obama was a freshman in college. The report explained that, between 1966 and 1970, 4,700 canisters containing lead azide were destroyed there.
Nonetheless, Rob Cekada, the director of the US Bureau of Alcohol, Tobacco, Firearms and Explosives claimed on Wednesday, “we were handed a problem frankly that started in 2009”.
“We learned that these lead azide explosives were brought here from a stockpile outside of the Middleton area and transported here to the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant and placed in the earthen-covered magazines, H2 and H3,” Cekada added, without offering any explanation of what his assertion was abased on.
“At the time those records showed that these were already old, degraded lead azide explosives and were already a concern for the military at that time,” he went on, again failing to explain what records he was referring to. “Unfortunately, under that administration, nothing was done to handle that issue.”
“This administration came together quickly in March of 2026, developed a plan to properly mitigate the threat caused by these explosives,” Cekada, a former police officer with no military background said.
A day earlier, Cekada told reporters: “from what we’ve learned, in about 2009 the nation’s stockpile was moved here to the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant. That stockpile contained approximately 10,000 pounds. Some of that lead azide was already 60-plus years old, and in many cases it was noted that there was some deterioration, in 2009.”
Earthjustice, a nonprofit public interest environmental law group that challenged the detonation permit given to the army by the Environmental Protection Agency, told the Guardian that the same claim was made in a sworn declaration filed in court on Monday by a senior military officer. In that declaration, Major General Eric P. Shirley told the court: “The lead azide has been stored at IAAAP since 2009”.
Senator Chris Van Hollen calls out Trump administration 'lies' about Kilmar Ábrego Garcia
Senator Chris Van Hollen, the Maryland Democrat who played a central role in helping his undocumented constituent Kilmar Ábrego Garcia fight his unlawful deportation to El Salvador last year, denounced the Department of Homeland Security for making unsubstantiated allegations about Ábrego on social media on Wednesday.
Ábrego voluntarily left the United States on a flight for Spain on Tuesday night, bringing an end to the 20-month legal battle that followed his wrongful deportation to El Salvador, in violation of a court order.

Trump's efforts to rollback birthright citizenship face latest setback
A federal judge has barred the Trump administration from restricting the birthright citizenship of babies born in the US to foreign government workers.
“Children born in the United States and subject to its jurisdiction are American citizens from birth. That rule, enshrined in the Fourteenth Amendment’s Citizenship Clause and codified in the Immigration and Nationality Act, derives from the English common law and is deeply rooted in our legal tradition,” US district judge Christopher Cooper wrote in a Wednesday opinion.
The ruling comes months after the Supreme Court blocked an executive order, where Trump sought to deny automatic citizenship for US newborns, whose parents were not citizens.
The president signed new executive orders in August with a narrowed scope, targeting the infants of foreign diplomats and members of “foreign terrorist organizations.”
That directive was also aimed at people engaged in birth tourism, a practice where people travel solely to the US for the purpose of giving birth and securing citizenship for their child.
Although birth tourism represents a small portion of annual births, it has been touted often in Trump’s case against automatic citizenship.
Former vice-president Al Gore, who started his career as a journalist, is returning to the industry as a newspaper owner in his Tennessee town.
The Lake family, which has operated the Carthage Courier for five decades, said yesterday that it will sell the paper to Gore in the coming weeks. The weekly paper serves Smith County in central Tennessee, which has roughly 20,000 residents and sits about 55 miles east of Nashville.
As the beleaguered news industry struggles with newspaper closures and growing news deserts, Gore characterized the purchase as a move that preserves an independent local voice in Carthage and Smith County.
“Having spent my early years as a military reporter in Vietnam and later at the [Nashville] Tennessean, I am a firm believer that a free, independent local press is irreplaceable for the enduring health of our community,” Gore said in a statement to the Associated Press.
Before entering politics, Gore spent six months as a military reporter in the US army during the Vietnam War. He returned to Tennessee and worked for a few years as an investigative reporter at The Tennessean before running for Congress in 1976.
Gore, a Democrat, represented Tennessee in both chambers of Congress, served as Bill Clinton’s vice-president from 1993 to 2001, and ran for president twice. After losing the 2000 presidential election to George W Bush, he went on to win the Nobel Peace Prize for his climate activism. He also narrated and was the subject of the Academy Award-winning documentary film “An Inconvenient Truth”.

Trump administration approves Elon Musk’s plan for datacenters in space
Edward Helmore
US federal regulators approved a constellation of datacenters to process data in space today, saying that they would “support America’s booming super intelligence economy”.
The Federal Communications Commission (FCC) said in a statement that regulators had approved 1m Starmind orbital satellites, or compute modules, built by Elon Musk’s SpaceX. It is the first such authorization in US history.
The FCC said that the authorization “promises to bring the human race closer to a Kardashev II civilization – one that can harness the full power of the sun”.
“America is leading the world into the era of super intelligence,” said the FCC chair, Brendan Carr.
Boosters of the plan have argued that datacenters in space are essential as the AI industry grows and their cost on Earth on the power grid and water supply are high.
“By directly harnessing near-constant solar power with little operating or maintenance costs, these satellites will achieve transformative cost and energy efficiency while significantly reducing the environmental impact associated with terrestrial datacenters,” SpaceX had argued to the FCC.
But critics say the technology for such space datacenters is still being developed, and their deployment is a long way off.
Under the license approved today, SpaceX must begin deployment no later than November 2033, deploy 10% of the maximum number – or 100,000 – no later than November 2035 and deploy 50% by November 2038, the FCC said.
Vance says he was assured Jensen Huang was not under investigation before reinstatement as Nvidia CEO
JD Vance has said that he was assured by the US Department of Justice that Jensen Huang “was under no investigation” ahead of his surprise reinstatement as Nvidia CEO yesterday.
Speaking to reporters at Wright-Patterson Air Force Base in Ohio, the vice-president said:
We received assurances that he was under no investigation.
“It was ultimately the board’s decision to bring Jensen Huang back,” he added, a day after Donald Trump took credit for “convincing” Nvidia’s board of directors to reinstall Huang as chief executive.
Trump earlier today expressed he’d be open to pardoning Huang for potential crimes but didn’t think it would be necessary. Asked about that, Vance replied, “Well, I don’t think he needs a pardon at all,” as, he said, there was no evidence that Huang committed a crime.
