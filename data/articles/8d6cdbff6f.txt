Freya looks nervous as she walks into a psychiatric clinic in Sweden for an ADHD assessment.
Countries around the world have seen a huge increase in ADHD diagnoses over recent decades. For many people this can be life changing, helping them understand themselves better and access support.
But Freya (not her real name) is not attending a long-awaited appointment to secure a diagnosis.
This government-funded clinic offers autism and ADHD diagnosis reassessments - its doctors believe it is the first in the world dedicated solely to this purpose.
At 12, Freya was struggling with anxiety at school and sought out professional help. "They thought I had ADHD," she says.
But as she grew up, she felt that she did not experience ADHD symptoms. "I don't think I had it in the first place," she now says.
Her doubts about the diagnosis became more pressing as a teenager, when she discovered that in Sweden, it could be a barrier to pursuing her dream career of joining the police.
"I want to have every option open," she tells BBC World Service.
If the doctors at the clinic decide Freya does not meet the ADHD criteria, her medical records will be updated, and it will be easier for her to apply for a job with the police.
The clinic, known as Mottagning Neuropsykiatri, has opened at a time when rates of ADHD and autism diagnoses are under the spotlight. Many say this is because of greater awareness of the symptoms, and that previously neglected groups - particularly women and girls - are finally getting the diagnoses they need, while others argue that the growing numbers stem from overdiagnosis.
The existence of the clinic is controversial. Clinical guidance from UK governing bodies and the World Health Organization describe autism as a lifelong condition and ADHD as a neurodevelopmental condition that can persist into adulthood. The medical consensus is that both diagnoses last a lifetime, even if symptoms fluctuate.
And some neurodiversity advocates warn that a dedicated reassessment service could reinforce the idea that being diagnosed with ADHD or autism is undesirable â and deepen the stigma surrounding both conditions.
Despite this, hundreds of people have approached the clinic about reassessment.
Barred from jobs
Like Freya, other young adults have been prompted to reconsider their diagnosis after they felt it had become a barrier.
This is because historically, Swedes with ADHD or autism were barred from certain jobs, including joining the military, and becoming police officers, train drivers and pilots. This is despite research suggesting some neurodivergent traits can be strengths in certain roles. No such bans exist in the UK, although rules differ globally.
Since 2022, people with ADHD have been able to join the Swedish military if they are medication-free. And since 2021, people with ADHD or autism have been able to apply to join the police, but must submit a medical certificate, which can cost around 15,000 SEK (Â£1,100) privately.
When Freya was referred to the clinic, she says she was relieved to find out she could get her diagnosis reassessed there for free.
Johanna, 24, was diagnosed with ADHD at 14 after what she describes as a period of bad behaviour at school. She found the diagnosis helpful at first and received additional support from teachers.
But as she grew into adulthood, she says she stopped feeling that she needed extra support. And after discovering it was a barrier to becoming a police officer, she began questioning whether it was even accurate.
Johanna says she has some traits of ADHD, such as a short attention span, "but not enough to have an ADHD diagnosis," she believes.
Johanna was reassessed aged 21 during an early pilot of the reassessment clinic and was found not to meet its diagnostic criteria for ADHD. She now works for her father's company, and does not plan on becoming a police officer.
What is an ADHD or autism reassessment?
Both ADHD and autism are neurodevelopmental conditions that affect how people think and interact with the world. They are diagnosed through detailed assessments, which can include questionnaires and interviews, rather than one single clinical test.
The doctors at Mottagning Neuropsykiatri say their approach is different from traditional assessments, as it focuses less on someone's history, and more on the symptoms they are experiencing in the present.
"This is not de-diagnosing, this is a renewed assessment of your functioning today," says Sebastian LundstrÃ¶m, the clinic's lead psychologist.
The doctors say they do not judge whether the original assessment was correct, so rather than wiping the original diagnosis from patients' medical records, they add a note of the new assessment.
LundstrÃ¶m says that some people who once had a diagnosis but are found to not meet the criteria at the clinic may have been misdiagnosed. But he also believes that for a small minority, ADHD and autism are not lifelong conditions and individuals may stop experiencing symptoms as they grow older.
But updating a person's diagnosis because they are not currently showing symptoms is a contested approach.
"We have to remember that these are lifelong conditions," says Navid Ghannad, chair of Autism SkÃ¥ne, a local Swedish non-profit for autistic people and their families.
"Because at a certain point in their adulthood during a clinical setting they have had their diagnosis removed, it doesn't necessarily mean that the ADHD or the autism has disappeared," he adds.
Symptoms could reappear later in life, he says. "The problem is once they resurface, that individual which doesn't have a diagnosis any longer, they will not be able to get the timely and correct support."
LundstrÃ¶m says he does not consider it a big risk that people could get reassessed and find themselves without necessary support.
During the pilot he encountered people who "really wanted" to get rid of their diagnosis but the psychologist said, "we still feel that you meet the criteria".
'I was stuck'
Vilda, 22, sought reassessment of her autism diagnosis which was initially given after she experienced a trauma.
"I didn't recognise myself in the description of autism," she says. "I was stuck. I know that this is not me, but I can't do anything about it."
It was requests from people like Johanna and Vilda that led to the establishment of the clinic, LundstrÃ¶m explains.
"It gained momentum by itself and the word spread around. A lot of people contacted us and we felt that there was a justification for starting up," he says. It is only open one day a month.
Before opening the clinic, LundstrÃ¶m conducted a small peer-reviewed study - of the 72 individuals who took part, roughly 80% did not meet the criteria for ADHD or autism. All the participants already wanted to lose their diagnosis, rather than being a randomly selected group.
"It's important to stress that this patient population is not necessarily representative for all with autism and ADHD," he adds.
During the first year of the pilot some 400 to 500 people spoke to LundstrÃ¶m, who is also associate professor of child and adolescent psychiatric sciences at the University of Gothenburg, about getting a reassessment.
The project also has the backing of senior figures in Swedish psychiatry, including Sophia Eberhard, a senior consultant specialising in child and adolescent psychiatry.
"It's psychiatry who in the beginning made those diagnoses," she says. "I believe we have a responsibility to help these young adults on their way."
In September, the BBC was given permission to join Freya as she became the first patient to attend the newly opened clinic, which serves both men and women.
During her appointment, Freya takes part in questionnaires and verbal assessments to see if she meets the ADHD criteria. The process is the same for both ADHD and autism, although the questions differ.
Freya says she is hopeful that she will be able to "get rid" of her ADHD diagnosis after the appointment.
But the doctors are adamant that they do not just give attendees the outcome they want. They conduct other checks such as assessing body language and communication skills, speaking to younger patients' parents, and checking medical files for ongoing treatment.
Fears about stigma
The opening of the clinic has drawn criticism from those who fear it sends the wrong message about autism and ADHD.
"The clinic presents neurodiversity and autistic people as a bad thing, like we are a problem that needs to be fixed - we are not," says Jenny Hedin, from charity Organiserade Autister VÃ¤sternorrland, an advocacy organisation run by and for autistic adults.
Hedin, who is autistic and has ADHD, warns that people who can successfully "mask" or mimic neurotypical behaviour might feel under pressure to lose their diagnosis in order to be better accepted by society.
"That is especially a problem with women and girls, because girls are usually great at masking," she adds.
Others say the focus should be on changing the rules that make it difficult for people with ADHD or autism to pursue certain careers.
Eric Donell, the chairman of RiksfÃ¶rbundet Attention, a national Swedish charity, lobbies to remove employment restrictions affecting neurodivergent people, which he says are unfair.
He stresses that for many people, getting a diagnosis is extremely important. "Instead of asking people to get rid of their diagnosis, sometimes we should just ask whether the rules themselves are justified," he says.
Reassessments risk becoming "a way of adapting the individual to society instead of adapting society to human differences," he adds.
'Magic spell'
People from the UK, Denmark, Norway and Germany have also been in touch for reassessment, says LundstrÃ¶m, but they have been turned away, as the clinic only serves locals.
Georgina, 35, from the UK, contacted the clinic because she wants to have the autism diagnosis she received at 25 "removed".
"It felt like a magic spell, that I'd get this label and the world would be more understanding," she says.
Instead, she says, her colleagues belittled her.
Georgina has since been offered an NHS reassessment. The process is the same as the original assessment she went through, and can result in patients receiving more diagnoses, fewer diagnoses or no change.
The Swedish reassessment clinic says they generally would not apply new or additional diagnoses to people - something Georgina was attracted to.
"The situation where you can go in and then come out with other labels... that's really off-putting," she says. For now, she has decided not to undergo an NHS reassessment. Instead, she is trying to find out how often a diagnosis is reconsidered, before deciding whether to go ahead.
Back in Sweden, a few hours after Freya entered the clinic, she re-emerges from a consultation room. The doctors here have found that, right now, she does not meet the criteria for an ADHD diagnosis.
She says it is "confirmation" that she was right to question her diagnosis.
"Now, finally, I have the same opportunity as everyone else."
