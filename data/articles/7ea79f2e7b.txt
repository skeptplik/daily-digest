Comedian and TV presenter Paul Whitehouse has called for the government to help save an at-risk fish species present in England's largest lake since the end of the last Ice Age.
Windermere, in the Lake District, has been home to Arctic charr for more than 10,000 years, but campaigners warn extinction beckons as the fish's habitat is harmed by sewage pollution, invasive species competing for food and rising temperatures caused by global warming.
Whitehouse urged Prime Minister Andy Burnham to act and said it would be "a crime" if the species were lost.
The Department for Environment, Food and Rural Affairs (Defra) said it shared "the public's determination to protect this iconic lake".
Whitehouse, who fronts the BBC's Mortimer & Whitehouse: Gone Fishing series alongside fellow comedian Bob Mortimer, said the "spawning grounds for this beautiful species are covered in algal sludge that is a direct result of sewage pollution".
"This government needs to act now," he added.
The former Fast Show star is helping environmental campaigner Matt Staniek unveil a 1.3m (5ft 6in) ice sculpture depicting the prime minister outside No 10 North, in Manchester, to highlight the fish's plight.

It has been drawn up by the Only Rainwater In Windermere coalition comprising United Utilities (UU), the Environment Agency, Lake District National Park Authority (LDNPA), the Love Windermere partnership, Save Windermere campaign group and Westmorland and Furness Council.
It comes as new research from the Freshwater Biological Association (FBA) also warns Arctic charr are in serious decline and face extinction in the lake without "urgent action".
The fish, which is genetically distinct from Arctic charr in the region's other lakes, lives in four separate populations in Windermere that spawn at different times of year and different places and do not interbreed.
The FBA said spawning habitat quality had "declined markedly" at Windermere since 2017 with levels of silt and algae increasing while introduced species, such as ruffe, roach and bream, were "on the rise".
Dr Louise Lavictoire, the organisation's head of science, said the research had "shown very clearly that we are at risk of losing Arctic charr from Windermere completely".
"The species is facing multiple pressures and research gives us the evidence, data and sense of urgency to take collective action to protect this ancient fish species before it's too late."
The research was funded by groups including Natural England, the Environment Agency and the LDNPA.
Natural England's Dave Ottewell described Arctic charr as "remarkable fish" and a "living link to the end of the last Ice Age".
Tim Duckmanton, team leader for strategy and environment at the LDNPA, echoed that sentiment and said "thanks to data collected, we now have a better understanding of threats to the species".
