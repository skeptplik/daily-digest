Pregnancy floods the brain with hormones and remodels brain circuits on a scale rivalled only by the neural transformation that occurs during adolescence. Yet there is a shocking lack of information about how a healthy brain changes during pregnancy, because of past worries about the safety of scanning expectant mothers.
Now, researchers are working to fill that void by collecting scans from first- and second-time mothers and analysing thousands of archived brain scans acquired during routine clinical care. The work, presented on 17 November at the Society for Neuroscience annual meeting in Washington DC, could help to explain why some people develop depression during and after pregnancy, and how they develop other maternal mental-health conditions. These conditions are the leading driver of pregnancy-related deaths in the United States.
It wasn’t until 2024 that researchers reported the first detailed map of a human brain during pregnancy1, after performing magnetic resonance imaging (MRI) on one woman at 26 timepoints from 3 weeks before conception until 2 years after delivery. The American College of Radiology endorsed the safety of scanning the brains of pregnant people for research purposes that same year.
“Over 80% of women worldwide will experience pregnancy at least once in their lifetimes,” so it’s “imperative” that physicians spot signs of abnormalities early enough to treat conditions such as postpartum depression, said Hannah Grotzinger, a cognitive neuroscientist at the University of Virginia in Charlottesville, during a presentation at the meeting. (Grotzinger was also a co-author of the 2024 study.)
Pregnancy brain
Building on the earlier mapping study, Grotzinger and her colleagues expanded the effort to see whether those patterns hold across several people. They tracked six first-time mothers, two second-time mothers and five female participants who were not pregnant. From before conception to one year after birth, each participant underwent up to 30 MRI scans and blood draws.
The larger cohort confirmed the shifts first seen in the 2024 map. In first-time mothers, total brain volume had dropped by roughly 3.5% by the time they gave birth. Grey-matter volume was reduced in nearly every region of the brain’s outer layer, or cortex, particularly in areas linked to social cognition. A brain structure called the pallidum, which regulates movement and motivation, was a rare exception: it grew during gestation, when a baby was growing in the womb. The researchers also found that the volume of cerebrospinal fluid, the liquid that surrounds and flows through the brain and spinal cord to provide cushioning and nutrients, increased by 11% during gestation.
Second-time mothers showed a similar pattern, but across only about half of their cortical regions. There was no substantial shift in cerebrospinal-fluid volume, suggesting that a first pregnancy has a bigger impact on the brain, Grotzinger said, although she cautioned that more data are needed to confirm these findings given the small sample size.
Such drastic changes to the brain are part of a typical pregnancy and are “not something to be scared of”, says Liisa Galea, a neuroscientist at the Centre for Addiction and Mental Health in Toronto, Canada.
The researchers also observed between the first and second trimester an increase in blood flow in the brain, which peaked around 21 weeks into pregnancy and tapered off near birth. The exception was a participant who developed pre-eclampsia, a serious pregnancy complication characterized by high blood pressure; the blood flow in their brain remained lower overall throughout gestation, compared with that in other participants, and they had less of a reduction in grey matter.
