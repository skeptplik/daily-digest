A woman who spent 48 hours desperately unwell in an emergency department corridor says bosses at Wales' biggest health board have lost interest in patients.
Jo Hughes, 72, who is in remission from cancer, spent two days in a hospital chair with Covid, pneumonia and a kidney infection.
Another patient, who has been treated at the same hospital for 30 years, said the north Wales health board Betsi Cadwaladr is "completely leaderless" and has declined "beyond redemption".
A report into the health board's culture and leadership is expected later.
After her experience at Ysbyty Glan Clwyd in Denbighshire, Hughes said: "I'm not going back to that after what I've been through."
The review was commissioned by the Welsh government in the summer, after it gave Betsi Cadwaladr a "final chance" to improve following nine years in special measures.
Earlier this month, the health board's chief executive Carol Shillabeer announced she was leaving in the new year to take up an NHS role in England.
Her departure follows a string of other senior leaders who have left the organisation in recent months, including the chief operating officer and executive director of finance.
Some patients blame senior managers for repeated failures.
Hughes, from Abergele, Conwy, was admitted to A&E on her doctor's advice last month.
"I didn't know what was happening to me. I didn't know what I'd got," she said.
"I felt awful. The best way I can describe it - it was a nightmare that I'd like to forget."
As the hours and days passed, she described "looking round to see if there was something spare that you could lie on".
She said: "I think it's the higher-ups. It's not the people on the ground, it's the chief executives, it's the bosses in my opinion. They're not doing their jobs."
Hughes said frontline staff were doing their best, but "the top people have lost interest".
The experience frightened her so much, she said she would refuse to go back to A&E.

She also fears for older patients who might face the same experience, adding: "They wouldn't stand a chance.
"How could you have an 80 or 90-year-old sitting in a chair for 48 hours? It's ridiculous. No blankets, nothing like that. You're just plonked."
John Cooke, 78, a retired assistant chief constable with North Wales Police, was diagnosed with Crohn's disease in 1995, and was first hospitalised with it in 1996.
Regular flare-ups mean he has been admitted to Ysbyty Glan Clwyd two to three times every year over the past three decades. While he says frontline staff continue to do their best, conditions had deteriorated overall.
"I suppose the most obvious thing is the time it takes to be seen, and certainly the time it takes to be allocated to a ward and to a bed," he said.
"When I first started on this road I could certainly be in a ward, in a bed within an hour. Now, more recently, it can be very lengthy waits, despite the pain, and I recently had a 47-hour wait in A&E before I was even seen by a doctor.
"I know of people who have waited almost a week to be allocated a bed, and very seriously ill people are not exempt from these waits at all."
He was once guaranteed quarterly outpatient appointments, but said "the treatment I'm getting now as an outpatient is almost non-existent".
Cooke says he does not believe things will improve without a "complete restructuring of the organisation".
"I blame the management. Not necessarily the board because they don't have direct access to the workforce... but the management, the senior management, the executives, they are to blame. It's completely leaderless."
He said frontline staff "work so hard and continue to labour under these conditions".
