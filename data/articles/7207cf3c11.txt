What an international court's historic genocide ruling could mean for future cases
Court's finding that Myanmar committed genocide provides little change for Rohingya victims
In the days after the world's top court was asked to weigh in on accusations of genocide against Myanmar, legal experts, activists and victims alike hoped the case might, for the first time in decades, open a new pathway for delivering justice on the international stage.
That was back in 2019. The International Court of Justice's finding seven years later that Myanmar committed genocide against its Rohingya Muslim minority was indeed celebrated as recognition of the decades-long suffering of the Rohingya people. But it came at a high price.
"For the first time the world has heard the Rohingya," said John Jonaid, a survivor of the genocide who is now an Ottawa-based Rohingya journalist and human rights advocate.
"But at the same time, it's too late, I would say, for us to get any justice for our suffering."
The Myanmar military conducted "clearance operations" in Rakhine state in 2017, systematically destroying Rohingya villages, perpetrating brutal sexual violence and killing thousands of people.
Most Rohingya now live in the world's largest refugee camp in Bangladesh and beyond, with little prospect of return.
In its judgment, the ICJ demanded that Myanmar provide reparations and allow Rohingya to return to their villages — but most of those villages have been erased, and the country's leadership has long disregarded international scrutiny, rejecting allegations of genocide.
The tension between a legal victory in the case against Myanmar, launched by the Muslim-majority African nation of Gambia, and the reality for Rohingya victims of genocide was inevitable from the start: while the court has the power to hear and rule on cases filed by states against other states, it has no real means with which to implement its rulings.
Gambia's Justice Minister and Attorney General Dawda Jallow, right, with lawyer Arsalan Suleman, speaks to the media after International Court of Justice ruling in The Hague, Netherlands, on Thursday. Gambia launched the proceedings at the court in 2019. (Patrick Post/The Associated Press)
And yet even before the Thursday ruling, the case still had a profound legal impact.
Five new cases involving genocide were subsequently filed at the ICJ, each with its own unique circumstances. They include the highly anticipated case brought forward by South Africa against Israel, accusing it of genocide against Palestinians in Gaza following Hamas's attacks on Oct. 7, 2023.

And now, legal experts say, the court's first ever finding of genocide committed by a state may also help shape how that case and others like it are decided in the future.
Not everyone agrees on exactly how.
At minimum, Thursday's ruling — the first of its kind at the ICJ — has brought the 1948 UN convention on genocide "back to life," said Canadian human rights lawyer Payam Akhavan, one of the attorneys who served as counsel for Gambia in the case.
"This is a landmark judgment of the ICJ, and it comes at a crucial point in the evolution of international law, including unprecedented attacks against both the international criminal court and the ICJ," he said in an interview with CBC.
While Akhavan says the ruling is "grossly inadequate" in addressing the immediate plight of the victims of Myanmar's genocide, he notes that how the court has interpreted the law in this case could still play a role in shaping the outcome of future ones.

Ruling underlines sexual violence is a genocidal act
Significantly, the majority of the judges in the Myanmar case rejected the country's argument that the "clearance operations" it conducted against the Rohingya in 2017 were defensive military operations aimed at neutralizing a threat and therefore precluded genocide.
Priya Pillai, a lawyer and international law specialist, told CBC News the court ruled that "the fact that you have a conflict doesn't preclude the fact that you might have genocidal intent as well."
"The two things can exist at the same time."
Other strands of the ruling, says Akhavan, demonstrated an expansive approach to the genocide convention, which was adopted in the wake of the Holocaust and the murder of millions of Jews during the Second World War.
Crucially, the ICJ judges strongly affirmed that genocidal acts were not limited to murder or displacement, but could also include crimes of sexual violence and attacks on reproductive capability to prevent procreation.
Countless Rohingya women reported a campaign of mutilation and brutal sexual violence at the hands of Myanmar's military forces.
Marianne Vitug, legal advisor for the Global Justice Center, said at a news conference in The Hague Thursday that the court looked at how sexual violence was directed at women and girls of reproductive age and sometimes pregnant women, and how young and unmarried women were targeted with extreme and brutal violence.
"The targeting of reproductive capacity is now very clearly a genocidal act, so that is a significant finding in this case."
Vitug says the court's reliance on evidence gathered by NGOs and fact-finding missions is also "very significant in relation to upcoming cases that will rely on similar findings."
Unlike in other cases, NGOs cannot intervene directly in cases before the ICJ. Only member states can do so, and in Gambia's case, that included Canada, jointly with France, the Netherlands, Denmark, Germany and the U.K., supporting Gambia's effort to end Myanmar's impunity.
Their intervention focused on how the court should interpret the genocide convention, including the role of sexual violence.
In 2018, Parliament voted unanimously to recognize that Myanmar's military actions against Rohingya people constituted genocide.

A 'painfully long' process
Rohingya activists are skeptical that pressure by the international community will do anything to persuade Myanmar's current president, Min Aung Hlaing, who led the military during its operations in Rakhine State, to accept them back.
There is also the question of what they would be returning to.
"Their entire villages have been burned and their history has been erased from Myanmar, as if they never existed [there]," said Jonaid.
"There is no trace."
While they await the right conditions for return, Jonaid says countries like Canada that have taken an interest in the plight of Rohingya must do whatever possible to ensure they thrive — including providing opportunities for education and employment wherever they currently live.
Akhavan suspects meaningful change in the international justice system will take time.
"We have to understand that the fight for universal human rights is a painfully long historical process."
