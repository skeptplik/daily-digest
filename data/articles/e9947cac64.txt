While most titles have fled the release period between the Ocarina of Time remake and this week's GTA 6, Denkiworks opted to release their debut title Tanuki: Pon's Summer right in the middle, rolling it out last week. It is, mostly, a deliberate decision.
The three-person, Kyoto-based studio initially planned a summer release for its cosy open-world adventure. However, the team decided to push it back to complete the final touches as it prepared to ship simultaneously across all platforms.
"All of that means time really does run against you," Denkiworks co-founder and game director Liam Edwards tells GamesIndustry.biz. "While we originally decided on October to launch, due to marketing opportunities with first-party platforms, we decided that the sweet spot would be just clear of GTA 6's initial launch period."
"What I don't think we could have accounted for was Nintendo shadow-dropping the release of Ocarina of Time [on November 5]," notes Edwards. "There's probably a fair bit more of an overlap with Switch 2 Zelda players for Tanuki than there would be for GTA 6."
Edwards explains that, rather than viewing the situation as a setback, the team used it to their advantage by positioning Tanuki as a "lovely little underdog coming in that players should care about" and treating it like any other launch period.
"I don't think you can account for what others do even in such a crazy period," he says. "You know your game's strengths best, and you have to continue to promote it as such. We've worked with the publishing team [Critical Reflex] to find places that Pon would shine and have seen a lot of success with that."
"Hopefully, in the long term, word-of-mouth and the fact that it's been well-received means a long tail; when players are done with Zelda and GTA 6, they'll be looking for what's next and might think, 'Oh, yeah! There was that BMXing Tanuki game!'"
A "love letter" to Japan
The game is based on Pon, a BMX-riding tanuki who works part-time at the Post Office, and uses his earnings from parcel deliveries to repair the local shrine before the end-of-summer festival.
Edwards described the game as a "love letter" to Japan, a place he's called home for 11 years. "It originally started from wanting to make a game about cycling around a Japanese town. I've lived in Japan for 11 years, and I find the layout of the towns and cities here fascinating, especially when you also consider what you're cycling past; shrines, rivers, old houses, statues, and endless cultural sites. So it felt that there was a game to be made here, while also getting to celebrate the country I now call home."
Tanuki was designed as an experience of "seeing Japan through the eyes of a foreigner," created by a team of Japanese and long-term foreign residents, along with collaborators from the UK, Australia, France, Brazil, and Canada.
"Together, we could make something clearly unique, with a strong foundation of understanding Japan and wanting to share different aspects of its culture in a game. Something mundane and normal for someone born and raised in Japan can be fascinating to visitors. We spent a long time researching different places in Japan, landscapes and media."
"When we had more international collaborators join, we could ask them personally what interested them about Japan too. The team's willingness to learn and get it right was very helpful, because when it came time to make certain Shrine objects – or anything that had an important or religious meaning – we would sit down together to make sure we were on the same wavelength about getting it right. Over time, it was also just about making sure we had checks and gave feedback when necessary, with the team always willing and open to make changes."
"The biggest and most heartwarming experience of the whole development was just how well the game has been received by Japanese players, especially on social media."
From prototype to simultaneous shipping
While prototyping Tanuki, Denkiworks were also considering a multiplayer-focused concept for another prototype. They chose Tanuki as it offered the most "realistic chance for us to get signed," leading to a publishing deal with Critical Reflex that included full development and marketing funding.
Denkiworks also secured an early agreement with Xbox to feature Tanuki on Game Pass alongside official support across all consoles and PC.
"Having had experiences with simultaneous shipping before, I was hesitant to tackle all of this at once, as it puts a great deal of strain on the team and becomes a very complicated situation," Edwards recalls.
"Thankfully, with support from our publisher, they found us a great porting team to work with, who took over the Xbox, Switch 1 and PlayStation duties while we solely focused on the PC master and Switch 2 development in-house. Even then, however, it required a lot of back and forth, testing and making sure we stayed in the loop."
Edwards notes that the team's industry experience informed their approach to the game, which was developed in Unity, though they slightly underestimated the tools required to develop a 3D game like Tanuki.
"Naively, there was a 'Well, we've made plenty of games before, we should be fine' attitude. But we actually had to do a lot of learning on this project," Edwards says.
Denkiworks was co-founded in 2023 by Edwards, designer Taku Arioka and programmer Jan de Graaf. The team all worked at Q-Games on Apple Arcade title Pixeljunk Scrappers, and Edwards was also game director on Chuahi Labs' Cursed to Golf.
"Without the tools we built internally, we would not have been able to achieve this at all. I think it's really under-discussed and shameful that we don't champion the institutional knowledge that is integral to video game development more. We were lucky that the people we hired to join our team came so well equipped to be trusted, bringing in their own experiences, knowledge, and tools to the project.
"I think if the quality or experience of our team members hadn't been so high, we might not have been able to achieve this, but they all handled everything so incredibly well, we can't speak highly enough of them."
Managing burnout
Edwards highlights that the final version of Tanuki is "quite scaled down from our initial vision," but this did not prevent inevitable feelings of burnout.
"If you're very deeply attached to the project you're making, it sits with you every minute of every day regardless of if you're at a desk working or not. That certainly was the case for Tanuki," says Edwards, noting that the core team didn't work long hours or on weekends, and the majority of the freelancers worked fixed hours as well.
"Having had bad experiences with crunch previously, I personally think there's nothing worse than being sat at a desk unable to do any work but being forced to be there. It's just so very unproductive and draining."

While Edwards says the team didn't "think much about" burnout during development, looking back on it now previous experiences shaped their approach to maintaining a healthy work-life balance.
"We learned to manage the moments where someone was struggling to be productive or creative by being kinder about the situation and being realistic. There were a lot of times when the stress of writing the game was really hitting me, and I would sit at a desk just trying to force words to come out."
"But towards the end of the project, I knew that if I just got up and went for a walk or finished early, what I needed would randomly come to me during a completely unrelated moment."
