Bruce Ames was remarkable in many ways. A renowned biochemist who continued working into his nineties, he is best known for developing a low-cost screening test for cancer-causing substances. But he devoted the latter stages of his long career to an intriguing idea: that a hitherto-unrecognised class of nutrients plays a key role in promoting healthy ageing. “Many as-yet-unknown longevity vitamins and proteins likely remain to be discovered,” he wrote in one of his last papers, in 2022, just a couple of years before he passed away, aged 95.
But could such nutrients have been hiding in plain sight all along? A number of promising candidates are under investigation, including some found in mushrooms and seaweed. Proving the long-term health impacts of dietary factors in rigorous trials is a huge challenge, and the field of anti-ageing supplements has been plagued by ideas that failed to live up to their promise, but we could still be on the cusp of finding out if Ames was right. If so, what might we one day be dosing up with for the benefit of our future selves?
A healthy diet requires various vitamins and minerals for basic metabolic reactions in the human body – that is, to keep us alive in the immediate future. There are around 30 of these essential micronutrients, including the vitamins from A to K, and minerals such as calcium and iron. A deficiency in these can cause conditions such as scurvy, rickets and beriberi.
But Ames had a compelling idea that inadequate intake of micronutrients also causes insidious, long-term damage. Building on his many years of research investigating metabolism and ageing, in 2006 he proposed that when vitamins and minerals are in short supply, our bodies operate a triage system, allocating them to systems involved in immediate survival and sacrificing other functions that are more important for long-term health, such as DNA repair and maintenance of mitochondria. This could explain why moderate deficiencies in vitamin D, for instance, are associated with certain cancers and cardiovascular disease.
His subsequent studies, working with Joyce McCann at the Children’s Hospital Oakland Research Institute in California, demonstrated that when selenium and vitamin K are scarce, they are indeed preferentially allocated to the biological systems that are most important for short-term survival.
This led Ames to the idea that some dietary compounds are particularly adept at supporting long-term health, and that there may be an entire other class of unrecognised nutrients that might be capable of activating various protective pathways in our bodies. If we consume enough of them, he suggested, they can fine-tune our metabolism and help shield us from insidious, long-term damage.
In that 2022 paper, Ames produced a list of around 40 nutrients – key ingredients for thousands of chemical reactions throughout the body – which he believed to be critical for preventing chronic conditions and promoting longevity.
“I propose that many of the known vitamins and minerals have, besides a ‘survival’ function, aging-delaying (‘longevity’) functions as well. I also propose that other dietary biochemicals, not officially recognized as vitamins or as having age-delaying functions, have a positive age-delaying effect,” he wrote.
Perhaps it shouldn’t be a surprise that some lesser-known substances in our diet may have this impact. There are myriad biochemicals in our food, and we know surprising little about this “nutritional dark matter” or its impacts on the body. But proving the efficacy of any purported longevity nutrients – substances whose effects gradually accumulate over the long term – was always going to be difficult, not least because they would sail under the radar of standard tests for deficiencies.
A recent academic book, Supplements and Drugs for Healthy Longevity, describes more than 250 chemicals that are listed in various databases as potentially being able to alter the ageing process. However, it notes that the evidence mainly comes from experiments on organisms such as yeasts, worms or other animals. “[Strong evidence] on the human species is absolutely missing,” says Suresh Rattan, a biogerontologist at Aarhus University in Denmark, who co-edited the book.
This could change, however, as the results of robust clinical trials begin to roll in, and with new research efforts under way to identify nutrients that may slow the clock.
One of these is a 2025 analysis of 777 generally healthy and active people aged over 70 who took part in the DO-HEALTH randomised controlled trial to investigate whether vitamin D, omega-3 fatty acids and a simple home exercise programme could prevent disease in older age and thereby prolong healthy life expectancy. This measured the biological age of the participants after three years of the trial and found that omega-3 alone moderately slowed the rate of biological ageing, with extra benefits from the addition of vitamin D or exercise.
Although we don’t yet have data about the ultimate impact this has on healthspan – the number of years that we live in good health – the study’s authors noted that “even small changes in biological aging, if sustained, may have relevant effects on population health”.
Another large-scale clinical trial that reported results earlier this year analysed the anti-ageing effect of a daily supplement of cocoa extract (containing high concentrations of substances called flavanols, thought to assist healthy ageing) and a daily multivitamin in 958 people in the US aged over 60. This found that the cocoa extract had no impact, but the multivitamin resulted in a small but significant reduction in the rate of biological ageing, which might be linked to our micronutrient requirements increasing with age.
The negative flavanol finding is interesting, because Ames, too, thought that certain substances in food that aren’t classed as vitamins could have a positive long-term impact on our health. But rather than flavanols, the candidates he proposed were pigments called carotenoids, as well as certain antioxidants produced by bacteria and fungi. Now, initiatives to investigate some of these are under way, with researchers in Singapore at the forefront of these efforts.
It is projected that, by 2050, a third of Singapore’s population will be over 65, with spiralling rates of conditions such as cancer and dementia too. The urgency of the situation has led to large-scale investment in clinical trials exploring new ways to elongate healthspan – and among the nutrients in the crosshairs of researchers there is one highlighted by Ames called ergothioneine. Produced mainly by fungi and bacteria, the best dietary source of it is mushrooms, particularly oyster and shiitake varieties.
A number of studies from the past few years have already shown that eating mushrooms is associated with a lower risk of various chronic conditions and longer life expectancy. Humans have also evolved adaptations that allow our bodies to absorb and retain ergothioneine. A specific transporter molecule for this nutrient in our intestines and other tissues means that when we eat foods such as mushrooms, it is distributed around the body – suggesting that it is important for us. But why?
Research from three decades ago revealed that ergothioneine is a particularly powerful antioxidant, a substance that helps negate damage to cells caused by unstable molecules called free radicals, one of the underlying drivers of ageing. It is up to 30 times better at neutralising free radicals than glutathione, one of the body’s innate antioxidants.
Barry Halliwell, a biochemist recruited by the National University of Singapore to lead anti-ageing trials, suspects that an ergothioneine deficiency could be linked to numerous neurodegenerative conditions. “Age-related diseases of the brain, Parkinson’s, vascular dementia, Alzheimer’s disease, cognitive impairment, are all associated with low ergothioneine levels,” says Halliwell. And research on human brain cells and miniature lab-grown organs known as organoids suggests that consuming sufficient ergothioneine might actively protect the brain.
This evidence has convinced Halliwell that maintaining a minimal level of ergothioneine in the bloodstream may be essential for a healthy brain. “Our hypothesis is that it’s actually a vitamin and you need it,” he says. “But it’s different to, say, vitamin C, where you’ll start to have symptoms within a few months if you don’t get enough. We think that the consequences of ergothioneine deficiency take a long time to manifest.”
In an attempt to find out if that is true, he and his colleagues launched a pilot study a few years ago where people aged over 60 with mild cognitive impairment took ergothioneine supplements for a year. Because the study was interrupted by the covid-19 pandemic, they were only able to recruit 19 people, as opposed to the planned 150, but there were still signs that it improved memory and learning abilities and slowed neuron damage. “We just got funding to redo the whole trial,” says Halliwell. “We need to do a full double-blind placebo-controlled phase II trial, which we are planning.”
Meanwhile, in the UK, a trial is measuring the impact of ergothioneine, as well as another antioxidant called taxifolin (found in onions and fruits such as apples), on people aged 50 to 65 as a possible way of keeping the immune system more resilient through midlife, when it starts to decline. The aim is to see whether this reduces the prevalence of so-called senescent immune cells, white blood cells that have ceased to function normally and instead contribute to chronic levels of inflammation, and hence the ageing process. The results are due soon.
But ergothioneine is just one of the possible longevity nutrients in the spotlight. Other candidates have been pinpointed through studies of the foods common in the diets of people who live well for a long time, such as fucoidan, a chemical found in the cell walls of brown seaweed.
A number of large-scale population studies have highlighted an association between consuming more seaweed and lower levels of both cognitive and physical frailty. Fucoidan, which can activate a gene called SIRT6 that promotes DNA repair, is one possible explanation.
Andrea Maier, director of the National University of Singapore’s Academy for Healthy Longevity and the mastermind behind the country’s various approaches to slowing ageing, is now leading a trial giving 2.4 grams of fucoidan per day to 60 Singaporeans aged 50 to 80 with signs of pre-frailty. The aim is to see if it can slow ageing based on inflammatory and immune signals in their blood, as well as their DNA.
In addition, the team will examine body composition, muscle and sleep quality, cognition and skin health. “In roughly four or five months, we will know if you should eat more brown seaweed,” says Maier.
Elsewhere, other researchers are working to identify overlooked compounds in food that might be important to our health. In 2024, for instance, a team led by Sastia Putri at Osaka University in Japan analysed the chemical footprint of tempeh, a food made from fermented soya beans that is reputed to protect against heart disease. This enabled the researchers to pinpoint a substance called meglutol, a cholesterol-lowering chemical found in soya beans that increases in concentration following fermentation. “It works in a similar way to statins,” says Putri.
This is an important step forward in understanding unrecognised nutrients in our food, but there is still a long way to go to discover any impacts that tempeh and meglutol might have on long-term health. And it is important to remember that there have been plenty of red herrings in relation to longevity nutrients.
For instance, Ames enthused about taurine, an amino acid thought to support the brain, muscle and heart. Studies showed that concentrations of taurine in our blood decline with age, suggesting that deficiencies might be a driver of ageing. However, a major analysis last year by a team at the US National Institute on Aging instead found huge variation in the amount of circulating taurine between adults, and no clear association between taurine levels and health status.
Researchers in Singapore faced their own setback when studying another proposed longevity nutrient called alpha-ketoglutarate, or AKG, which plays critical roles within the body in everything from energy production to kidney function. Animal studies have shown that AKG can have a potent impact on lifespan, and AKG supplements are also used by athletes to aid muscle repair and recovery. However, when Maier and her colleagues trialled a daily supplement of AKG in people aged 40 to 60 who were deemed to be ageing rapidly, based on a biological test assessing changes to their DNA, it failed to offer noticeable health improvements.
Maier suspects that AKG could make more difference in a more vulnerable population, and a new trial taking place in Singapore is examining whether it can help prevent the development of post-operative frailty in older adults after heart surgery.
These findings also raise the question of how longevity nutrients might best be utilised. Rather than standalone supplements, Putri, Rattan and many others think they are more likely to offer health benefits when present in whole foods. In light of this, Putri is now developing ways of increasing the meglutol concentration within tempeh by tweaking the fermentation process. “What I find really interesting about food is that there’s rarely just one interesting compound,” she says. “Tempeh contains many that may have useful effects, and some may work together synergistically.”
More broadly, Rattan isn’t convinced of Ames’s ideas about longevity nutrients, pointing out how the field of ageing has long been dogged by unrealistic claims. He highlights the bursts of hyperbole surrounding the purported benefits of different supplements including NAD+, a substance produced by human cells that helps convert the energy within food into a form that our bodies can use.
Some researchers have described the steady decline of NAD+ levels in our cells through the decades as “the driving force in ageing”, giving rise to a burgeoning industry of companies selling various formulations of NAD+ or nicotinamide mononucleotide (NMN), its precursor molecule.
But while Rattan suggests that boosting NAD+ may be useful for people with certain conditions, he says there is little evidence that it alters the ageing process, describing the supplements as “gimmicks”. “In people with cardiological or even some eye problems, these things can be useful,” he says. “But when we take them as geroprotectors, it’s mostly hype.”
However, while Rattan still isn’t convinced that a single nutrient can slow ageing and extend life, he does expect the research efforts in Singapore to shed new light on the role that different nutritional deficiencies can play in the loss of physical or mental function with age.
Addressing these shortfalls could allow people to retain their independence for longer, and may eventually enable more of us to maintain our vitality into a ripe old age – just like Ames.
