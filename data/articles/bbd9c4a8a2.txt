People struggling with their mental health are being encouraged to connect with nature on their doorstep.
Hope Haven, in Whitehaven, west Cumbria, opened in March as one of six pilot centres across the country dedicated to tackling problems before they escalate.
It has now joined forces with the RSPB to issue "nature prescriptions", which see people encouraged to visit a number of places in the area, such as the local harbour, sand dunes at Ravenglass and Walkmill Community Woodland at Moresby.
Clinical manager Barbara Slater said the scheme was "another tool" to add to the hub's range of interventions.
Visitors to Hope Haven, located in a former bank building in the town centre, are able to discuss their concerns with mental health professionals without needing a referral.
Slater said the nature prescriptions, a series of suggestions for local walks and activities contained in a booklet, were "a great way to gently introduce nature and well-being into people's lives".
"Being close to the Lake District, people often think that to go out in nature you need special equipment and boots," she said.
"We purposefully tailored it to Copeland, putting suggestions in for places that can be reached by public transport and most are free.
"That was really important because we want people to feel they're not excluded."
Other featured locations include Whitehaven's Castle Park, Seascale seafront and St Bees Head, home to a breeding site for guillemots.
'Moment of joy'
Not all suggestions contained in the booklet required travel, however.
"It can be as simple as being outside and breathing the air," Slater said.
"Another is to look at raindrops on a window and watch the water roll down. It's about connecting with something much bigger than ourselves."
Hope Haven is the first organisation in Cumbria to take part in the scheme.

Sarah Walker, nature and well-being manager at RSPB England, said the charity's trials had shown "even taking a moment or two to notice nature can boost mental and physical health".
"So many people face a whole range of pressures in their lives and nature could provide a way to help them through and find a moment of joy in their day," she added.
More than 5,000 people have visited Hope Haven since its opening with that number prompting conflicting emotions, Slater said.
"We're very busy because the community is coming to see us, but there's also a sadness because it shows there are struggles with a range of issues - anxiety, feeling in crisis, debt and alcohol problems.
"We try to help people in those really dark places."
