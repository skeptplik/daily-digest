The New York governor, Kathy Hochul, signed legislation on Wednesday that places a five-year ban on the manufacture and sale of toys powered by AI chatbots, including talking stuffed animals, dolls and robots that use the internet to hold conversations with children.
The recently re-elected governor, who has also placed a one-year moratorium AI datacenter construction in the state, said that parents shopping for children’s toys “should not have to worry that dangerous chatbots disguised as toys could expose their children to inappropriate conversations, manipulate their feelings or put their privacy at risk”.
Hochul said the measure was “deeply personal” and that, as a mother and grandmother, “it’s my job to put New York families first.”
A study by NBC News found that the Pixie Paw, a toy marketed to children as young as three, had shared details about firing squads and massacres. It also found that Pixie Paw Smart Interactive Plush Cat explained gang rape.
The New York attorney general, Letitia James, added that AI chatbots in children’s toys “are exposing young people to inappropriate content and putting their mental health at risk”.
James said that her office would “not hesitate to enforce this law and other measures to stop AI systems from doing harm. We won’t allow our children’s health to become the collateral damage of big tech.”
In a press release, the officials pointed to Trouble in Toyland, a consumer watchdog report from the US Public Interest Research Group Education Fund (PIRG), that tested AI chatbot-enabled toys marketed to children as young as three that “talked in depth about sexually explicit topics, told kids where to likely find matches in the home and acted upset when a child had to leave.”
The governor said that in some cases the toys had few or no parental controls, and the safety limits weakened the longer the conversation continued.
“Additionally, some toys could record a child’s voice or scan their face,” the statement said.
State senator Andrew Gounardes said in a statement that “we don’t have to live in a Twilight Zone episode turned real.” LaVita Tuff with Mothers Against Media Addiction, said that “Parents should be able to feel confident that the toys their children play with are safe, which means that toys containing AI should only be sold if they are safe by design.”
Hochul has emerged as a leading elected campaigner against AI. Last month, she signed “AI Chatbot Restrictions for Minors” legislation prohibiting companies from offering certain unsafe chatbot features to minors, including features that foster emotional dependency, encourage self-harm or engage children in sexually explicit talk.
The New York measure signed on Wednesday follows a similar measure in California that placed a four-year moratorium on the sale and manufacture of toys with AI companions.
Earlier this week, a consortium of more than 155 child safety advocates and technology experts sent a letter to the Amazon CEO, Andy Jassy, and eBay CEO, Jamie Iannone, asking the tech giants to bar AI toys from their platforms.
“Just like we don’t release cars without seat belts and test them to make sure they are safe to be driven, we shouldn’t be releasing toys that are available to the consumer before they’ve been rigorously tested,” Rachel Franz, an advocate for limitations on tech for children and leader of a program at the non-profit advocacy group Fairplay, told NBC News.
An eBay spokesperson told the network: “We continually evaluate our policies, engage with regulators, and comply with applicable laws to help ensure our marketplace remains safe, trusted, and responsive to evolving safety standards.”
An Amazon spokesperson also gave a statement to the outlet: “We require all products sold by third-party sellers in our store to comply with applicable laws and regulations,” an Amazon spokesperson said in a statement. “Amazon is committed to the safety of children and families. We support efforts across the industry and government to put appropriate guardrails in place as this technology develops.”
