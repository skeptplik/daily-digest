One of Grand Theft Auto 6’s strangest random encounters occurs when you’re offering taxi services, and a man with a beard wearing a dark suit and sunglasses climbs into the backseat with a gun. This can happen if you’re playing as either Jason or Lucia, but both scenes play out the same way: The man wants you to run a red light, ignore phone calls, and drive him to Harborside Park in Vice Beach. He also sounds an awful lot like Tom Cruise, with a calm but forceful demeanor that seems almost sociopathic. Several clips of the sequence have already hit the internet, and to many people, it’s an obvious nod to Collateral, Michael Mann’s 2004 thriller about a hitman who takes a cab driver hostage.
Jason or Lucia gets a better deal than Mann’s cabbie in this Easter egg. If you take the stranger to his destination and follow him inside after he takes out some of the criminals there, you can find a ton of valuable loot. Most players seem keen on eliminating the stranger rather than keep driving him around. In Collateral, Jamie Foxx’s character, Max, has to spend the whole night driving Cruise’s Vincent around Los Angeles. If you’ve never seen how that goes for him, Collateral is streaming for free on Pluto TV — and it’s a must-watch, especially if you want a movie that has a vibe as wild and crime-filled as GTA 6’s.
Max is a cab driver who’s been telling himself for years that the job is temporary. He wants to start his own limo company someday, so when a well-dressed passenger named Vincent (Cruise) offers him $600 to drive him around for the night, it seems like the kind of opportunity that could get him a little closer to quitting. Then a body lands on his cab at their first destination. Vincent has five people to kill before the night is over. Max has just agreed to drive him to all five stops — and he’s already an accomplice.
Vincent is one of Cruise’s best roles because so much of what makes him convincing as an action hero makes him downright scary here. The same certainty that lets Ethan Hunt talk somebody into jumping out of a plane makes it seem perfectly reasonable, for about half a second, when Vincent asks Max to help him stuff a body in the trunk. He’s also an obnoxious backseat driver. The longer he spends in the cab, the more interested he becomes in why Max has seemingly wasted so much of his life. Some of his advice is irritatingly solid, yet he’s also a man who could end the conversation with a single gunshot whenever he wanted to.
Despite the plot focusing entirely on Vincent’s assassinations, Foxx emerges as the real heart of Collateral. Early on, he picks up a prosecutor named Annie (Jada Pinkett Smith), and their conversation has the easy rhythm of two strangers who realize they’d like the ride to last a little longer. You get to see how good Max is at putting people at ease as he flirts a little. Then, Vincent hops in the back, and the nightmare begins. Once the hitman is in the car, even the smallest decisions Max makes — like when to look in the rear-view mirror and when to keep his eyes on the road — feel like consequential choices Max must make carefully.
For a movie about a man on a deadline, Collateral is remarkably willing to pull over and look around. Vincent himself comments early on about how much he despises the city’s sprawling, depersonalized atmosphere. Mann often dwells on that atmosphere to build up tension. In the movie's best quiet moment, a coyote crosses the road in front of the cab, and both men stop talking to watch it. Los Angeles feels eerie and sinister, lit by flickering streetlights and bland storefronts. As their night continues, even mundane locations feel like the most dangerous places in Los Angeles.
The story’s arc is also immensely satisfying. We see what this pressure cooker does to Max, forcing him to evolve from a passive bystander in his own life to someone who takes an active role. The evolution is subtle at first as Max resists Vincent’s demands, eventually mustering up the courage to become openly defiant. Vincent spends the night needling Max about all the chances he’s too scared to take — putting a down payment on a limo, calling Annie since she left him her card, etc. — and by the time they inevitably face off, Max is finally willing to take one.
GTA 6 so often positions your character as the Vincent of its story, stealing cars and killing people for the next big score. But forcing you to become something closer to Max, at least for a little while, is nothing short of brilliant. The Grand Theft Auto franchise at large owes a great deal of its overall vibe to Mann’s filmography. GTA 5, in particular, is largely inspired by Mann’s 1995 crime film Heat. So it's only fitting that its direct sequel pays homage to another Michael Mann classic — with a little bit of Miami Vice thrown in too, for good measure.
