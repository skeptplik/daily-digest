Holding the future Queen Elizabeth II when she was a toddler and joining King George V to shoot pheasants aren't stories every Australian rugby union player gets to tell.
Nor is sending British newspapers into a frenzy over a rumoured engagement to marry.
But for Jim and Arnold Tancred, these were some of the tales they shared during their rugby careers a century ago.
Jim, a mobile prop, and younger brother Arnold, an aggressive back-rower, were members of the legendary 1927/28 NSW Waratahs, which captivated the rugby world on their tour of Great Britain, Ireland and France (as well as Sri Lanka and Canada).
One of their older siblings, Harry, also played for NSW during his career.
The trio was posthumously recognised as Australian representatives and awarded Wallaby numbers 192 (Harry), 231 (Jim) and 241 (Arnold) following a decision to grant Test status to the Waratahs' matches against international teams between 1920 and 1928.
To celebrate 100 years since Jim (christened James) played for the Waratahs against the All Blacks, 22 of his direct descendants, along with spouses and friends, attended last month's Bledisloe Cup Test in Sydney.
Among those commemorating the milestone was Jim's granddaughter, former Queensland premier Anna Bligh.
"We had caps made. We all sat together in the stands. It was great," Anna tells ABC Sport.
Anna's brother, Stephen Tancred, adds: "We rounded up the family from Perth, Melbourne, Sydney, Darwin and country Queensland.
"We had three of Jim's daughters there. They're all in their 80s, and we had a great-great-great-grandchild who was 13."
It wasn't the only occasion Jim's descendants came together to remember his career.
Anna and Stephen's mother, Frances, was one of nine children born to Jim and his wife Mary, and a coveted family photo was recreated to coincide with her 90th birthday.
The original image captured Jim and Arnold's family outside their home in the Sydney suburb of Rozelle as they welcomed the brothers back from the Waratahs' tour in early 1928.
"It was such a proud moment for the family," Anna says.
"The photo is quite precious."
Playing for the Waratahs
When writing about the Tancred brothers, Australian rugby historian Jack Pollard noted they were "sometimes described mistakenly as New Zealanders".
Born in Sydney, they spent part of their childhood in New Zealand after parents Thomas and Anna moved the family of 10 children to the Shaky Isles.
Much of their rugby education took place across the Tasman.
Harry even represented New Zealand in rugby league but was permitted to rejoin the union ranks.
Once the family returned to Australia, it was Harry, a robust number eight, who first represented the Waratahs when he twice faced the New Zealand Māori (now Māori All Blacks) in 1923.
Jim, who like Harry and Arnold played for the Glebe-Balmain club in Sydney, made his Waratahs debut in 1926, appearing in two internationals against the All Blacks.
But the highlight of Jim's playing days was his selection alongside Arnold for the 1927/28 Waratahs tour.
The Waratahs, away from Sydney for 252 days, dazzled crowds in Great Britain, Ireland and France with a breathtaking display of "running rugby".
They won three of their five internationals and played more than 30 matches in total.
"It was such a big thing in those days to represent your country and tour internationally," Anna Bligh says.
"It meant being away for many weeks at a time because they travelled by ship."
Jim played the third and final international of his career against France, with the Waratahs winning 11-8 in Paris.
He was headbutted by opposition prop Jean Morère during the match, only for Waratahs teammate Ted "Bluey" Greatorex to violently square up with the Frenchman.
Standing at 5'10" (1.78 metres) and weighing 78 kilograms, Jim would be considered a small front-rower by today's standards.
His modest physique, though, complemented the Waratahs' attacking game.
"The Waratahs' forwards were mobile," says Stephen Tancred, who played for the Stanthorpe Kookaburras during a proud bush rugby career.
"They would have the set-play and then join in with the backs. That was the essence of running rugby."
All three of Arnold's internationals came on tour, against Ireland, Wales and Scotland.
A dislocated shoulder ended his tour early, meaning he and Jim never played an international together.
Arnold's ferocious tackling shocked spectators, especially in England, where rugby was regarded as a "gentlemen's game".
"Even though the Waratahs were known for running rugby, they had some really good defenders," Arnold's grandson Toby Tancred says.
"Dad [Arnold's son John] has told me stories about them getting booed at games because of the hard defence.
"That was Arnold's forte."
Meeting the royals
It wasn't just rugby keeping the Waratahs busy on tour.
While in England, they enjoyed afternoon tea with the Duke (who would become King George VI) and Duchess of York at their Piccadilly residence.
The Waratahs were introduced to the duke and duchess's one-year-old daughter, Elizabeth, who 24 years later would be crowned queen of the United Kingdom.
Jim Tancred was among the players to take a shine to "Baby Betty".
"The family story is he jiggled Elizabeth on his knee," Anna Bligh laughs.
Another meeting with the royal family involved pheasant shooting with King George V at his Sandringham estate.
According to the official tour report, written by manager E Gordon Shaw and captain Johnnie Wallace, the king "gave us each a pheasant from the day's bag".
So what happened to the pheasants?
This was a topic of conversation when Jim's descendants came together at the Sydney Bledisloe Cup Test a month ago.
They were joined by the daughter of star 1927/28 Waratahs centre Cyril Towers, Marguerite Howard, whose husband Jake and son Pat played for the Wallabies.
"Marguerite asked, 'Does your family have the pheasant?'" Anna recalls.
"We said, 'What pheasant?'
"She said, 'The king apparently gave all the players stuffed pheasants in a glass case. My mother claimed it was the ugliest thing she had ever seen.'"
The Waratahs fraternised with more than royals while abroad.
Arnold Tancred sent tongues wagging among the British press when it was reported he was engaged to marry Vera Morgan, whom he met at a dance in Swansea, Wales.
"The marriage will probably take place in a year's time, when Miss Morgan will go out to Australia," the South Wales Argus newspaper reported on January 6, 1928.
Alas, it wasn't to be.
"There is nothing at all in our friendship," Arnold told London's The Evening Standard.
"And there is no likelihood of our becoming engaged."
Business giants
Given rugby was amateur at the time, Jim, Arnold and Harry cut their playing careers short because of their business interests.
As a family of butchers, they helped established Tancred Brothers in the 1920s, quickly becoming giants of Australia's meat industry.
Arnold and Harry remained in Sydney while Jim ran the Queensland arm of the business, which went public in 1955, after moving his family to Toowoomba.
"They had a fair bit of go about them," Stephen Tancred says.
"They pioneered slaughtering in the country and shipping chilled meat to the city."
Arnold managed and coached the Wallabies on their 1947/48 tour of Great Britain, Ireland and France.
Newspaper reporters following the tour suggested he was prickly, and his players considered him tough on the training paddock.
"I think what was more notable than his playing career is he was the coach of the 1947/48 Wallabies," Toby Tancred says.
"He trained them like bastards."
The Tancred name lives on in rugby, with Toby's three sons showing promise.
The eldest, Arnie, was a member of the Waratahs' 2024 preseason squad, George represented Australian Schoolboys last year, while Tomma played in the Riverview first XV, which was named AAGPS co-premiers in August.
A well-known racehorse owner, Harry, who was christened Henry Eugene, served as Sydney Turf Club chairman (1953-59), with the prestigious H E Tancred Stakes named in his honour.
Jim stayed connected to rugby in Toowoomba and, like Harry, racehorses were among his passions.
He was a punter, too, once betting 1,000 pounds on the horse he owned, On Cor, when it won the 1948 February Handicap at Doomben.
"We knew he liked the GGs [horses] and a flutter," Anna Bligh says admiringly.
Tragically, Jim died suddenly of a heart attack at his home in 1965, at the age of 62.
His brothers Arnold and Harry also died from heart-related conditions, aged 59 and 64 respectively.
Anna, who was five at the time of Jim's death, remembers her grandfather as "larger than life" and a family man "taken from them far too young".
She learned more about Jim during her political career, especially his generosity.
A chance meeting with Wallabies great, prominent businessman and ex-lord mayor of Sydney, the late Sir Nicholas Shehadie, left Anna beaming with pride.
"When Shehadie finished his rugby career, he was very keen to go into business and couldn't find a bank that would give him money without someone going guarantor for him," Anna says.
"My grandfather offered to do it.
"Shehadie said, 'I owe my success to your grandfather being prepared to back me', which has always made me feel good about my grandfather.
"It's nice when you hear a story like that."
