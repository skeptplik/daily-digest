Your report on a better way to diagnose obesity is welcome, though it is worth remembering how we came to rely on body mass index (BMI) in the first place (BMI alternative for identifying obesity ‘could prevent misdiagnosis in millions’, 11 November).
The index was devised in the 1830s by the Belgian statistician Adolphe Quetelet, who wanted to describe the “average man” across whole populations. He never intended it as a verdict on any individual’s health, and it was largely forgotten until 1972, when the American physiologist Ancel Keys, most famous for developing the diet-heart hypothesis, revived it and gave it its current name. Again, it was for population studies.
Meanwhile, US life insurers had long priced policies on “build” – weight against height – drawn from their policyholders’ mortality; many now simply use BMI. Medicine absorbed the same logic. Weight relative to height became the yardstick, and BMI, needing only scales and a tape measure, became the standard. It was cheap, quick and showed deviation from the norm at a glance.
We measured what was easy, not what mattered, then overinterpreted individual results. A statistical tool for describing nations ended up deciding whether a muscular rugby player or a slim person carrying dangerous fat around their organs is “healthy”.
The Lancet Commission’s approach, adding waist measurements and tests of organ function, is a step in the right direction. Tellingly, though, it still begins with BMI. Habits formed over nearly two centuries are hard to break. If the aim is to diagnose individuals rather than describe populations, clinicians should start with the patient in front of them, not with Quetelet’s average person.
James Capon
Waterloo, Belgium
