Today's top stories
Democrats who won this month's election are looking to use their influence to block funding for the war in Iran before officially taking charge of Congress. President Trump launched attacks last winter without congressional approval, breaking from the tradition of seeking bipartisan support for wars.
- 🎧 Congress must pass a funding measure to avoid a shutdown by Dec. 11. NPR's Claudia Grisales tells Up First that Democrats hold leverage, since their votes are essential for the Republican-controlled Senate to meet the 60-vote threshold. Senate Democrats argue they should use their authority to end what they call an illegal war. Virginia Sen. Tim Kaine aims to include a provision to end war funding in the stopgap bill. Republicans counter that cutting war funding jeopardizes service members in the region. N.C. Republican Sen. Thom Tillis tells Grisales his party may need to explore alternatives if Democrats push for a shutdown. That could include using reconciliation to pass legislation without Democratic votes.
Trump and Chinese President Xi Jinping met for the third time this year, underscoring a dilemma for Americans who see China as a business opportunity and a national security risk. Nvidia's former CEO, Jensen Huang, convinced Trump to approve several chip sales before his departure. The House Foreign Affairs Committee yesterday, in a highly unusual step, released a list of requests this year from Nvidia and AMD to sell chips directly to Chinese companies.
- 🎧 The Trump administration approved the sale of nearly 900,000 advanced chips to Chinese companies as of May 2026. Some of those approvals included well-known firms such as Alibaba and Tencent, which the Pentagon lists as having ties to China's military, NPR's Greg Myre says. During his second term, Trump shifted his stance on China, partly influenced by his relationship with Jinping. The two may meet again next month in Miami.
The U.S. Army has rescheduled the detonation of around 9,500 pounds of unstable lead azide at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown to Monday, Nov. 23. This decision comes after Iowa lawmakers and two environmental groups pushed for a delay. The plant, which manufactures various types of ammunition, has stored lead azide in a water-ethanol solution in underground tanks for 20 years. Recent inspections found that the tanks were too dry, leading to crystallization and instability. To prevent an accidental explosion, the Army wants to detonate the lead azide under controlled conditions.
- 🎧 Many Iowans were unaware of the situation until earlier this week, prompting the delay, says Eliza Billingham of NPR network station Iowa Public Radio. Acting Army Secretary Adam R. Telle said on social media that he directed Army Materiel Command to postpone the blasts until the "latest possible moment" before a forecasted drop in temperatures. Army spokesperson Justine Barati previously said that cold weather could make the situation more dangerous. Last month, the Army hosted an informational open house for Middletown residents and shared maps predicting the movement of lead dust. Those maps will need to be revised based on the new forecast, Billingham says. If the detonation proceeds, the Army, EPA and the University of Iowa will monitor air quality.
Picture show
Iraqi environmentalist Jassim al-Asadi has dedicated his life to protecting Iraq's ancient marshlands. He even helped reflood areas that Saddam Hussein drained and set fire to decades ago to flush out resistance fighters. In February 2023, an Iran-backed militia kidnapped and tortured Asadi for two weeks on false charges that he was an Israeli spy who had ignited anti-government protests. He endured the trauma by reciting poetry until his Bani Asad tribe mobilized armed tribal fighters to secure his release. Once freed, he refused European asylum so he could continue his work. Today, despite drought and other hardships on the land, Asadi continues to monitor recovering water levels as wildlife slowly starts to return to the marshes.
