Geena Meszaros is in the cat sweatshirt.
Worn by her polarizing character Lisbeth in Netflix’s new Catholic school drama The Body, the white crew-neck pullover Meszaros took from set—and is now sporting on our Zoom call—is a key bit of the show’s iconography. The gray kitten’s big green eyes gaze slightly upward, a single paw emerging from under her chin. It’s the perfect veneer of innocence; she could either beckon you closer or pounce.
Depending on which corner of The Body fandom you find yourself in, Lisbeth is either an innately evil villain or an Ethel Cain–coded antihero. (The singer-songwriter herself recently weighed in on the show via her Tumblr account: “i fear lisbeth is, in fact, my biological little sister.”) At the beginning of actress-turned-director Quinn Shephard’s series, which is set in 2004, we understand Lisbeth immediately as an outsider. She watches the Lions dance team—headed by queen bee Anya, played by Kristina Bogic—float by in their matching track pants with ill-disguised greed. She moves like someone not totally in control of their limbs. She has no eyebrows. (Well, okay, they’ve just been bleached to the gods.)
The actress grew up in Calgary, Alberta, a shy kid obsessed with Dakota Fanning who was too scared to ask to go to the bathroom. “I was really a nervous, hyper-aware-of-everything child,” Meszaros says. “Then, with my friends, I was always really goofy and would put on weird little personalities.” When I ask her age, she demurs. “I don’t know if the world is ready to know. I’m scared…I play young.” She laughs.
Even over Zoom, it’s obvious from her mix of humor, plaintiveness, and clear self-possession why she was cast as Lisbeth. (“I think I’ve embraced the weird a little bit more outwardly,” she says of her own growth since her teenage years.) She almost decided to be a painter instead of an actress. On her Art by Geena Instagram account, she shares self-portraits and thoughtfully bizarre scenes from real life. (In one, her mom’s Italian greyhound gnaws on a Barbie doll.) While filming The Body, she painted a portrait of herself as a skull with lipstick on. “That was a representation of how I felt while I was filming. I was kind of dead, a little bit ill, but having a great time.”
The eyebrows both isolate Lisbeth and imbue her with a sort of strange power. (The show’s makeup artists got the look by bleaching them every two weeks. “I was sure I was going to lose my eyebrows, but then when I dyed them back at the end, they were actually so much fuller than they used to be,” Meszaros reports.) By the end of season one, she has the dance team girls in a spiritual chokehold, weaponizing their Catholic guilt against them. But this is no straightforward revenge arc; she is complicated but also deeply cruel, deftly sussing out weakness.
The show’s rapidly growing audience, needless to say, has had its takes—and Meszaros has seen some of the discourse. That Lisbeth comes from a lower-class background, that she has faced more than her fair share of religious trauma at the hands of her mother, that she is essentially the head of her household after the death of her father, don’t totally seem to justify her behavior in the court of online public opinion.
Yet Meszaros has also recognized an important truth about contemporary cultural criticism by way of fandom. “There’s a lot of people that cannot separate the character from the actor, which is fascinating to me,” she says. “I’m like, ‘Yeah, she does some bad things, but she’s not real. Don’t worry. She’s not out there in the world.’” Still, she loves seeing what people come up with in their analysis. “A lot of people think she’s drugging her mom. I’m like, ‘No, that’s not a thing.’ But I do enjoy all the crazy theories anyway. It’s fun, and I’m just happy that people are debating things and that it left them with questions that they’re having fun putting together.”
Meszaros points to one moment that she herself found tough to take: “That one scene in episode five with Maddie [Shirley Chen], I struggled with that as much as people watching did,” she says, referencing the exorcism of Maddie’s “demon of gluttony”—her eating disorder—which Lisbeth has manipulated her into thinking is a sin. “But I still had that empathy for Lisbeth. I think I had to [in order] to play her, and I do think she’s just gone through so much that you can’t really expect her to be a perfect human being.”
“It’s not her most redeemable scene, but I try and tell myself that people like Maddie might be really triggering to Lisbeth because [Lisbeth] used to be really devout, and I think she sees a version of herself that she is still grappling with,” Meszaros continues. “Her mom speaks to her in the way that she’s speaking to Maddie, and it all just comes out in a desperate moment… trauma gets passed down through generations, and I think it can get passed through friendships, too.”
Meszaros thinks back on her own childhood experiences. “Growing up, I actually used to sort of befriend people who bullied me. I would make myself small if I knew somebody was a threat to me,” she says. “The way that Lisbeth gets power is by observing the girls and molding herself into them. I really feel like she mimics people…she’s making herself not a threat in such an extreme way. It’s gaining trust, I think. It’s kind of the flip of [my experience] almost, where she starts out small, and then she slowly makes herself bigger.”
Throughout her season-long quest for belonging, Lisbeth also repeatedly reminds us she’s just a teenage girl finding her way. In one viral scene, she enjoys a particular queer rite of passage: googling “girls kissing” on the internet. (As my lesbian roommate put it, that’s one step before taking an “am I gay?” quiz.) When I ask if Meszaros thinks Lisbeth is on the queer spectrum, she giggles. “I mean, I think everyone is on a queer spectrum.” On the show or in the world? “In the world.”
“I think she’s definitely curious,” she says. “Her little hookup with Tyler didn’t seem all that buzzy.”
If the show is renewed for a second season—which seems likely—she’d like to see “what relationship Lisbeth will have with sex. Is it going to be a power thing? Is it going to soften her? I don’t know. But yeah, she could totally be somewhere on the spectrum.”
As The Body continues to enjoy its run as the number one show on Netflix, Meszaros is looking forward to actually having a minute to process the success of the show, and her increased visibility. “This role truly was a dream role, and now I have to figure out what my next dream role is going to be,” she says. “I am really excited to just go back and spend time with family and be normal for a little bit.” She also plans to hang out on her sister’s “big plot of land in the middle of nowhere” this winter: “I think it’d be nice to just go and hibernate. Maybe turn off my phone for a while.”
