Suspected North Korean computer programmers did contract work for B.C. government
Recruiting company owner says he had no idea they were North Korean operatives
Four computer programmers who did contract work for the British Columbia government were likely North Korean operatives, CBC's the fifth estate has learned.
Between 2023 and 2025, the four were hired to work on the province’s business registry platform.
As part of a contract worth hundreds of thousands of dollars to supply the province with software developers, an IT staffing firm based in B.C.’s Lower Mainland facilitated the hiring of a man calling himself Kevin Zhang.
"Kevin's resume was ideal for the situation that I was dealing with," said the owner of the staffing firm. He spoke with the fifth estate on the condition that he and his company not be named. He said due to the nature of remote hiring, he was completely unaware he hired likely North Koreans.
Over the last year, the fifth estate has been investigating North Korean IT workers and how they, like Zhang, have been able to infiltrate Canadian businesses and government.
"With Zhang, he's pretty known," said Michael Barnhart, a former U.S. Army intelligence officer who investigates North Korea for the private sector.
"I had tracked him before. There's a couple crypto companies that had tracked him before. A couple of other just different industries that had been tracking him. And everyone had a different piece of the puzzle."
The photo from Kevin Zhang’s website, left, does not match that of the passports saved to his GitHub account, right. (kzdev420/GitHub)
Alongside the confirmation of experts like Barnhart, the fifth estate found multiple online clues that point toward North Korean IT worker tradecraft:
In an online profile Zhang used to do work for the B.C. government, he stored two fake Chinese passports. The place of birth for the holder is the Chinese province of Liaoning, which directly borders North Korea. The U.S. Department of the Treasury has previously sanctioned companies operating IT worker cells in Liaoning.

The names on the passports appear to have been modified, with neither matching the name on the bottom stripe of the document.
A resumé-style website for Zhang contained a headshot that did not match the photo on the passports.
"This is the same guy that also goes by Kevin Chang, too," Barnhart said. "Sometimes he’ll only go by that.
"Chang, Ching or Zhang, just different variations of the beginning or ending name."
As time went on, the B.C. government hired three more remote developers via the same IT staffing firm. The owner of the firm told the fifth estate that each was recommended by Zhang, who said they were former colleagues.
The owner said the B.C. government made the ultimate decision to hire them and would have conducted a 30-minute interview focused on competency.
The fifth estate reached out to multiple people and entities within the B.C. government, but received no response or was told that because of the provincial election, which concluded Oct. 24, the government would not be able to provide a response.
In response to a freedom of information request, the fifth estate was told that the ministry where Zhang worked could not locate an employee by that name, despite having been provided the account details that logged his specific contributions to a ministry software project.
Red flags
Beyond the fake Chinese passports and differing headshots, an investigation of Zhang and his colleagues conducted by the fifth estate revealed numerous other oddities.
For instance, a resumé for Zhang found online, which listed the B.C. government as a prior employer, stated his address was "Main St. E, North Bay, ON," with the street number absent.
In email correspondence provided to the fifth estate by the B.C. staffing firm, Zhang and the other developers can be seen repeatedly changing their banking information.
"In retrospect, there's a highly unusual situation to have somebody change their bank, change their company," the B.C. staffing firm’s owner said. "I think in the years that I've been doing this, right, this happened once before, twice before."
This is a red flag, according to cybersecurity expert Barnhart.
"I’ve had the same bank account at my employer for decades. I’ve never told my company to change it, ever. If I have, it would have been once. If I’d have done it twice, that would have been like: 'What are you doing?’"
At one point last year, Zhang asked to be paid to the bank account for a company called Dev Dragon Inc. Corporate records show Dev Dragon, now defunct, was registered by a James Zhang at a derelict building in Dawson City, Yukon.
The registered address for DevDragon Inc. was a long-abandoned bank building in Dawson City, Yukon. (CBC)
One of the other remote workers, Brandon Galli, had a similarly confusing digital footprint. On the account connected to his position working on B.C. government code, Galli’s profile photo is of an Asian man. However, his closely linked personal website provides a photo of a Caucasian man.
In an email obtained by the fifth estate, Galli asked for his pay to go to the account of an Ontario company called Luk Castle Investments, registered to a home in Markham by a man named Kenny Luk.
According to his LinkedIn, Luk did computer programming jobs for a number of Canadian banks before starting a software development consultancy.
When the fifth estate contacted Luk to ask about Brendan Galli and the money paid to him, he said that "Luk Castle Investments does not participate in media inquiries or respond to unsolicited questions regarding its business activities. Neither I nor anyone associated with the company will be providing comment on this matter."
It is a common practice for North Korean IT workers to use a real person’s identity to commit their fraud. (GitHub/Brandongalli.io)
The fifth estate tracked down the real Brandon Galli in New Jersey. When told that someone using his identity had worked for the B.C. government, he said his identity might have been stolen.
But pressed further, he admitted he gave his identity to someone he was working with online. He said this person continues to use his identity without his permission, causing him a host of problems.

"It was before the pandemic. We were working together on upwork and freelancing platforms and I was receiving small payouts for a short time while I was in college just to pay for food," Galli told the fifth estate in a WhatsApp message.
Barnhart has seen this same pattern play out before.
"You'll see them targeting college students a lot that might need some money," he said.
"Where it's like: 'Hey, I don't want anything from you, I just need — can I use your LinkedIn profile? Can I use your driver's licence to try to get this job one time?’ And then once you [let them use it] one time, they have it forever."
