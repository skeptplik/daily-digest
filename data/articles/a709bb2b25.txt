Like most kids who shouldn’t have been playing Grand Theft Auto III in 2001, Eamonn Swayah imagined a version of Rockstar Games’ groundbreaking criminal sandbox set in his own back yard. In his case, that was a Manchester council estate, complete with local accents, driving on the left, and plenty of ways to hustle.
He didn’t know it, but swapping the game’s facsimile of early 2000s New York City for a different setting was an idea he shared with many watchful game developers. Rockstar’s 3D open world template, it turned out, could be plonked in just about any location, so long as it had people and enough cars to run them over with. After GTA III attracted immense commercial success (and controversy), dozens of copycats and spiritual sequels hit the market throughout the 00s and 2010s. Gamers had a pithy name for them: “GTA clones”.
The list of suspects was fatter than a conspicuous stack of dollar bills: the Driver series; Crackdown; the decades-late game adaptations of The Godfather and Scarface; kid-friendly The Simpsons: Hit and Run; the even more kid friendly Lego City Undercover; hacker-thon Watch Dogs; the Hong Kong-based Sleeping Dogs; and the gang-focused and increasingly daft Saints Row series.
But when Rockstar’s GTA Online started hoovering up players and revenue in the wake of 2013’s Grand Theft Auto V, earning billions over the next 10 years, the clones began to die off – until recently. In a real, “Ah shit, here we go again,” moment, the Rockstar replicas have been returning like a bank thief chasing one last score. Fuelled partly by nostalgia, and partly by the 13-year wait for Grand Theft Auto VI, aspiring developers now have the tools to make their own free-roam stories set where they please.
Swayah doesn’t have to wonder what a GTA in a deprived northern city in England looks like any more – the 30-year-old self-taught game developer decided to make it himself. The Estate allows players to rob betting shops, deal drugs and generally cause chaos on the Brit-core streets.
The game’s low-poly models hark back to the scrappy nature of GTA III, Grand Theft Auto’s first jump to 3D. “I feel that in the PlayStation 2 era they were still learning the technology and hadn’t perfected it,” Swayah says. “I think it really helps that I don’t know what I’m doing and I’m figuring it out as I go, which lends itself to a gritty art style … I’ve only just made a Steam page and that was a learning curve.” Ideas for new features in The Estate often come from the fans he’s garnered thanks to viral promotional posts, but the mechanics are only added if he can learn how to do it.
The solo developer’s first game shares much of GTA’s irreverent satire of its setting. For Swayah’s game, this manifests in egging people’s houses, bouts of knock-a-door-run, and even Mancunian radio chatshows that he recorded with his friends.
Burak Akşahin, who’s 32 and from Turkey is the solo developer behind Alaturka, an open world action game set in 1970s Istanbul. Akşahin teaches people how to make games online, and created his project during live streams to encourage solo developers. “When I saw GTA III as a child I told myself: ‘This is the most graphically [advanced] game we can see. This is the top notch game.’ But if we go like 20 years ahead, we have even better technology,” he says.
After plugging away at Alaturka’s retro graphics, doner kebab minigames and Anatolian soundtrack for just under two years, Akşahin was delighted when the explosive trailer for his game went viral when it was released in September. He hopes that the game can help spread Turkish culture, which is often undersold to western audiences. As a period piece – with its moustaches, lumbering cars and gaudy shirts – it has much in common with the 1980s world of Grand Theft Auto: Vice City.
But the game also shares the rags-to-riches tales of many famed GTA protagonists, from the fresh-off-the-boat Niko Bellic to the reluctant gang member Carl Johnson. “You came to the city not because you want to make huge [amounts of] money,” says Akşahin. “You came to earn enough money to return to your home village to get married. So you start working as a doner apprentice… you find some risky shortcuts. You mess with the mob, you mess with the police.”
Yet another solo developer has brought GTA-flavoured chaos to Finland. In October, Pietari Niinimäki launched Helsinki Online, a browser-based sandbox built from real-world geography data and coded using AI. In it, players can socialise, control a goose, and even traverse the Finnish capital in a flying bathtub.
Niinimäki is not much of a gamer himself, and the 23-year-old’s initial idea of a bird-themed MMOG (massively multiplayer online game) set in digital Helsinki was a far cry from the criminality of GTA. But the important ingredients crept in: missions to complete; an open city to explore; a mini-map. Now, with properties to buy, vehicles to drive and players generally running amok, the game has become a kind of Scandi, hygge version of GTA: Online.
“It sort of makes sense as a genre,” Niinimäki says, “People immediately understand what you mean when you say that you made a Helsinki GTA.” But he is keen to keep his player base acting as upstanding as possible: “I’m intentionally not adding that much crime, violence, drugs, stuff like that.”
Another Finnish developer has added as much of all that as he can. In August, Henri Koskinen’s small studio Lohkare Games Oy released Last Drop, an unashamedly grim look at a fictional Finnish city in the 1990s. Just as GTA III drew its gangster credentials from films such as Goodfellas and The Godfather, Koskinen’s team were inspired by the work of Finnish film-maker Aki Kaurismäki. The game’s story is also shaped by the very real social impact of alcoholism in Finland. “All these stories are in the game and they are very personal. Our neighbours died by alcohol,” he says.
Traditionally, each new Rockstar release has ushered in a new way to think about games – a new standard to be envied, copied, or even challenged. Indie developers might not be able to match the billion-dollar budget of GTA VI, but Rockstar’s detailed depiction of Floridian life can still inspire them. The best approach for a GTA-alike, according to Akşahin, is to take things back to basics: where Rockstar has expanded realism and scope, smaller developers should focus on the chaotic gameplay that players fell in love with in 2001.
“I’m a technical person, so if I try to push cinematic cutscenes, I cannot do them well,” he says. “But that chaotic gameplay is much more immediately appealing for players. When you see two police cars flying over you as they start to shoot, you feel like, I want to play this game.”
