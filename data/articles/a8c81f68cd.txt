Maurits Cornelis Escher didn’t plan on becoming the darling of mathematicians. Born in 1898 in the canal-lined city of Leeuwarden in the Netherlands, Escher struggled with mathematics as a student, once remarking that he “never got a pass mark.” He ended up studying decorative arts and printmaking, and he used his artwork to explore the strange rules of nature that he found operating around him.
Escher saw that nature can sometimes produce impossible-seeming images. All around him, he saw distorted reflections, odd perspectives, and superimposed worlds, like the surface of a pond where you can see images of both the fish below and the trees reflected from above.
In rendering these and other visions, his artwork took on unmistakably mathematical qualities. “By keenly confronting the enigmas that surround us, and by considering and analyzing the observations that I had made, I ended up in the domain of mathematics,” he wrote in his introduction to the book M.C. Escher: The Graphic Work.
His journey among these enigmas is the subject of an exhibition entitled Mathematics and the Art of M.C. Escher at the National Museum of Mathematics (MoMath) in New York City.
Escher’s path toward math started with his love of tilings. After completing his arts education in 1922, Escher visited Granada, Spain, and was taken with the tile patterns that decorated the Alhambra. Inspired, he began to systematically study how to fill a sheet of paper with repeating shapes without leaving any gaps.
Years later, Escher’s half-brother, a geologist named Berend, sent him a paper by a mathematician named George Pólya. The paper categorized all the ways to cover a flat space with tiles, broken down into 17 groups, each with different symmetries. Escher had intuited a mathematical question, and here he had a mathematical answer.
Escher carefully traced the paper’s diagrams and studied them meticulously. Over time, he developed his own system for tessellating space, finding ways to cover a print with irregular shapes that resembled fish, birds, bats, lizards, and so on.
“He had worked out a whole theory that he called his layman’s theory, so he knew which edges had to match which edges,” said Doris Schattschneider, a mathematician and leading authority on Escher, and a curator of the exhibition.
Carolina MacGillavry, a Dutch crystallographer, took notice of Escher’s mastery of color symmetry — the rule ensuring that adjacent shapes in a tessellation are always different colors — and published a book in 1965 analyzing his drawings.
“[MacGillavry] realized that [Escher] knew more than all the crystallographers about color symmetry,” Schattschneider said. “It was something he discovered very intuitively.”
In 1954, his work was exhibited at the International Congress of Mathematicians in Amsterdam. There, he was introduced to the geometer Harold Scott MacDonald (H.S.M.) Coxeter. The two exchanged letters until Escher’s death in 1972, and these exchanges inspired some of his most mathematically complex work.
As Escher’s relationship with math grew, his artworks became more mathematically intricate, even inspiring the research of later mathematicians. But living his double life wasn’t always easy. “I’m starting to speak a language which is understood by very few people. It makes me feel increasingly lonely,” he wrote in a letter to his son in 1959.
