When I was growing up in the US in the 1980s and 90s, fat in foods was demonised. Low-fat yoghurt, low-fat desserts and low-fat margarine were all the rage, with the message that fat was bad for our hearts and arteries. By the early 2000s, concern had moved to added sugar and its apparent links to obesity and type 2 diabetes, driving food reformulations.
Sugar was replaced with artificial sweeteners such as aspartame and acesulfame K, which have their own health impacts. Aspartame is classified by the World Health Organization as possibly carcinogenic in humans, while observational research such as a 2022 study of about 102,000 adults has linked acesulfame K with increased cancer risk. Then carbohydrates came into the spotlight, with pasta, rice and bread becoming the bad guys for our diet and blamed for weight gain. There is a fashion cycle in nutrition, where each food group or ingredient takes its turn being blamed for our poor health.
In recent years, protein has emerged as a nutritional good guy, with social media influencers blaming its deficiency for your fatigue or the fact that you don’t have the body you want. Go into a supermarket and you can find protein yoghurts, protein cereals, protein breads, protein drinks and even protein chocolate bars.
When I spoke about my last book on good health to the Edinburgh international book festival, the first question was how many grams of protein I recommended eating each day, and which supplements and powders to take. The same question was asked of Chris van Tulleken, a physician and leading UK expert on nutrition, when he spoke at the Hay festival.
Yes, protein is essential for our bodies to repair and build muscle, produce enzymes and hormones, and maintain normal bodily functions. Severe protein deficiency can cause conditions such as kwashiorkor, a severe form of malnutrition found in parts of Africa, where some children live with conflict, drought and extreme food scarcity. But those are extreme cases of food deprivation.
In the UK, the recommended daily intake is 0.75g of protein per kg of body weight. So if you weigh 75kg, that’s 56g of protein per day. Requirements differ based on age, pregnancy and how much physical activity – especially strength training and performance sport – someone does.
The evidence suggests that the rest of us are already eating enough protein. Indeed, the British Nutrition Foundation found that average protein consumption in the UK already exceeds these recommendations. They report an average daily intake of 85g for men (above the recommended intake of 56g) and 67g for women (above the recommended intake of 45g). Protein is part of the foods we eat every day: not just in chicken or burgers, but in wholemeal bread, rice, eggs, milk, beans, yoghurt, porridge, pasta and lentils.
Yet the “protein industry” has created the widespread impression that we should all be eating more, and has created a whole line of foods and marketing with “protein added”. Many of these products are ultra-processed (which we know isn’t good for our health) with very high levels of sugar and salt, plus all the additives and preservatives to keep them from going off for years. Simply adding protein doesn’t improve the overall nutritional quality of a food product.
In August 2026, a review of more than 350 studies looked at the relationship between protein intake, metabolism and ageing. The researchers found that for some people who don’t do much exercise, consuming less protein may be better for their metabolic health and longevity. As Prof Dudley Lamming said: “Because most people are relatively sedentary, many people are likely consuming more protein than they actually need, which probably has negative health consequences.”
The researchers suggest several biological mechanisms to explain this, including one focused on the hormone FGF21, which increases in the body when protein intake falls. Experiments in mice have linked increased FGF21 activity with longer lifespans, given that this hormone contributes to regulating energy expenditure, glucose metabolism and metabolic health. As a caveat, much of the evidence linking longevity with eating less protein in the paper comes from testing in animal models such as mice, rats and fruit flies. Nonetheless, the study challenges the idea that more protein is always better for healthy ageing, especially for those who aren’t very active.
What does this mean for all of us, trying to wade through the ever-changing dietary advice on social media and the multitude of products in the supermarket? For me, it’s another reason to avoid treating nutrition like fashion, with trends coming and going. Demonising or idolising individual nutrients and foods doesn’t help. Pasta isn’t the enemy. Protein powder isn’t the hero.
We should be eating a diverse and balanced diet, including a range of food groups such as vegetables, fruits, wholegrains, healthy fats, and plant and fish/meat-based protein sources. My only real rule is about reducing the amount of ultra-processed foods in our diets. This is largely to do with the ways these products are formulated and manufactured (just read the ingredient list and see if you could make it in your kitchen), and the emerging evidence on the harm they can do to our health.
And remember that at its heart, protein has become commercially attractive. Adding it to an existing product increases profit margins and gives consumers the impression that a solution has been found to a problem (I would argue) that never existed. It’s a clever marketing strategy, but not necessarily a nutritional improvement.
- Prof Devi Sridhar is chair of global public health at the University of Edinburgh
