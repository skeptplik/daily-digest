The US Army is set to detonate five tonnes of degraded explosive material in Middletown, Iowa, and have urged locals to vacate the area or stay indoors for their safety.
The detonations will result in an air plume that could send toxic dust, containing lead particles, over eastern Iowa and into neighbouring Illinois, with the potential for contaminants to settle on farmland, playgrounds and other surfaces, the army said.
The two controlled explosions will be powerful enough that they could damage buildings and shatter windows nearby.
On Thursday, environmental groups sued in an effort to stop the detonations, arguing the army failed to consider safer alternatives or give adequate notice.
The detonation was originally scheduled for noon local time (1800 BST) on Friday at the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant (IAAAP) in Middletown - a community of about 360 people about four hours west of Chicago, Illinois.
The plant produces medium- and large-calibre munitions for the US Department of Defense.
On Thursday evening, Acting Secretary of the Army Adam Telle delayed the detonation to Monday, saying that "public safety is the Army's top priority".
The planned detonation will be "the latest possible moment prior to a forecasted temperature drop, which would risk further destabilization of the material", he said on X.
The army said that the detonation is part of "a planned and controlled safety operation" to dispose of degraded lead azide. It added that the detonation has been permitted by the US Environmental Protection Agency (EPA) under conservation and recovery laws.
Residents have been asked to leave their homes for about six hours - between 08:00 and 14:00 local time - while the operation takes place and the dust settles. The army said that "all humans" should avoid outdoor activities within affected areas.
Residents in Middletown will hear a loud noise, feel vibrations from two consecutive, rapid detonations and will see a plume of dust, the advisory said.
According to Iowa Public Radio, external, the IAAAP found earlier this year that the lead azide - a highly volatile compound used as a detonator - stored in underground concrete tanks is degrading, becoming too unstable to safely move.
Military and environmental officials determined that detonating it in place is the safest way to eliminate the explosive safety hazard.
The EPA, which granted an emergency permit to detonate 68 drums of the material, said it did so because the explosion risk "presented an imminent and substantial threat to human health and the environment".
Justine Barati, a public affairs specialist at Army Sustainment Command, said that the substance has been stored at the plant for more than 20 years in earthen-covered containers.
It sits in a bath of ethanol and water to keep the material stable, but a routine check earlier this year found that some of the water had evaporated, Barati told the Associated Press.
The army said the lead azide barrels will be detonated inside earth-covered magazines made of concrete, and surrounded with barriers full of sand. The soil above will be saturated with water in an effort to reduce the lead in the air.
On Thursday, two environmental groups sued to block the detonation, challenging the validity of the EPA's emergency permit, according to the BBC's US media partner CBS News.
"The EPA itself admits that there is no safe level of lead exposure for humans," said Michael Schmidt, General Counsel at the Iowa Environmental Council.
"When our federal government makes decisions that result in a potentially toxic plume of lead dust that could cover much of southeastern Iowa, Iowans deserve to know that every available alternative was considered before choosing this method of disposal."
