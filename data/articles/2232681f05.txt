The prime minister will tell Parliament on Monday the "landmark" deal struck with the EU means there is a "very real prospect" the next decade can be "much better" than the last one.
The deal, which covers British food exports, emissions trading and a youth experience scheme, is a "choice between a broken status quo or change", Andy Burnham will tell MPs.
He will also call it "the single biggest growth measure to be delivered in this parliament" - repeating his claim it could add Â£17bn to the British economy.
But opposition parties have criticised the plan, with the Conservatives branding it unfair as UK farmers would have to follow rules made by Brussels.
The agreements announced on Friday included scrapping most of the checks on British food exports, in return for the UK following some EU regulations, from 2028.
Meanwhile, the new youth experience scheme, which is due to launch no later than January 2030, will allow up to 100,000 18 to 31-year-olds from the EU to travel, work and study in the UK for up to three years. The same number of young Britons will be allowed into Europe.
Burnham believes the agreement reached in Brussels "holds out the very real prospect that the next decade can be a much better one for Britain than the one we have just lived through".
"The ability to draw a line on a decade of drift and division is in our hands and I believe we should take it," he will say in his speech.
He will also paint the move as a choice "between easing pressure on the cost of living or allowing food prices to rise further" and "creating opportunities for young people or denying them".
He believes polling suggests the UK public "want closer ties" with the EU and the deal "delivers that".
Conservative leader Kemi Badenoch will use a speech at the Confederation of British Industry (CBI) annual conference on Monday to criticise Burnham's EU deal.
She will argue the prime minister should be "aiming a lot higher" on driving economic growth.
"Of course, Europe will always remain an important market. but it is not the market of the future," she will say.
Reform UK leader Nigel Farage will tell the same conference that establishing closer ties with the EU would be a "disaster" for British businesses that are trying to compete and innovate.
On Friday, the PM said that over the coming months both sides will "seek to conduct an informal brainstorming process" to look at the options for the UK's "long-term relationship" with the EU.
He was clear that the deal he struck was "probably the limit of what we can achieve in this Parliament, given what needs to be done to implement it".
He added: "But we want to inform the Parliament after this one, so that decisions can be taken about that long-term relationship."
Burnham has previously suggested potential options for the future could include rejoining the customs union, the single market or a fresh referendum on full EU membership.
However, he has ruled out major changes to the UK-EU relationship before the next general election, saying he will stick to Labour's 2024 manifesto pledge not to return to the single market, customs union or freedom of movement.
A number of former prime ministers have warned against reopening the debate over rejoining the EU.
Rishi Sunak told Sky News the issue was "divisive" and "ends up being a huge distraction for government".
Fellow Conservative former PM Baroness Theresa May told the BBC she was "aghast" Burnham could be considering rejoining the EU.
She urged him to "accept Brexit and move on".
Meanwhile, former Labour PM Sir Tony Blair, who was a prominent opponent of leaving the EU, told the Sunday Times Britain should only rejoin the bloc if it reforms, and allows more innovation on technology.
