I’m not sure when it started being not chic to want to look sexy, but it’s definitely a thing and it bothers me as it doesn’t feel truthful to the human experience, if that’s not a lot for the first sentence of a fashion column. Our wardrobes have become shrines to good taste. Well-cut coats and quality knitwear, elegant flat shoes and crisp white shirts. These clothes are excellent at communicating that you have read the briefing notes for the meeting and have some Booker prize chat. They are less good at suggesting you might be up for dancing on a table later.
Why do we have to choose? Clothes have always been partly about how they make us feel physically. We accept that an outfit can make you feel powerful, cool, smart. Why shouldn’t it also make you feel hot and fun?
We get terribly embarrassed about wanting to look attractive. This is sort of universal, but being British doesn’t help. And now we’ve ended up in this ludicrous situation where the only acceptable way to look sexy is by accident. Sex appeal is allowed only if it looks accidental, like you didn’t realise your sweater fell off your shoulder like that. But the point of sexy clothes is not that you are trying to get off with everyone. It is about looking like a warm-blooded human with appetites and desires and hinterlands.
This doesn’t have to mean showing lots of skin. I’m not advocating a return to the 00s, when we wore almost nothing and then got so cold waiting for a taxi outside a club at 2am in low-rise jeans that we almost got kidney failure. Your skirt doesn’t have to call in sick, Bridget. You can turn up the temperature without taking anything off. There are outfits that show a lot of body and then there are outfits that remind people you have a body. I would argue that the second kind are actually sexier. I know we said we’re not trying to get off with anyone, but if we were, it is hotter to give a hint than to draw a floor plan.
Think about a tuxedo. In terms of flesh on display, you might as well be going to court. And yet, soooo sexy. Yves Saint Laurent commandeered the tuxedo for women in the 1960s with his Le Smoking design, taking the most formal codes of the male wardrobe and recutting them around a female body. The body does not disappear inside the tailoring. The severity of the clothes makes you notice the person inside. The strong shoulder makes you notice the neck, and the long trouser makes the waist more interesting. Everything is covered, and yet you are emphatically physically present in a way you are not in a floaty midi dress. The same applies to a black polo neck worn with hair lifted high off the neck, and a red lip. Smoking hot.
You can show skin in a low-stakes way by not showcasing the obvious hits of cleavage and legs. There’s something compelling about the eye having to find the sexy bit. Backs are very underrated: try a V-neck sweater worn back-to-front, so it plunges between the shoulder blades. Collarbones are peerless for elegantly conveying the message that there is a person under this outfit. They are architectural, catch the light, give the face a frame. Audrey Hepburn knew this, which is why the boat-neck she wore in Sabrina ended up named after her character. I am also a fan of a fine chain lying exactly on the bone.
The whole bingo-wings drama has convinced women over 35 the upper arm is a no-go zone that must be covered at all times. But a bare arm is sensual: it is the part of the body you reach out with, put around other people. The point is the gesture, the warmth. Victorians understood this, which is why an ankle could clear a drawing room. Somewhere between them and the Instagram thirst trap, we forgot.
Sexy is just dressing in a way that acknowledges you have a body and enjoy being in it, which is another way of saying, being alive. Not for anyone’s gaze, and not for the algorithm. Just for the pleasure of catching yourself in a shop window and thinking: well, hello. Lovely to have good taste; also nice to remember that you have a pulse.
