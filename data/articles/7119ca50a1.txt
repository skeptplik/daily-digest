With everything going on in the games industry today, it can be easy to spend more time worrying about what's happening outside your studio than inside it. But building a positive company culture remains a priority for any business leader in the games space, and those external worries can have a direct impact on that culture.
"When you grow and contract, when all that stuff happens, it's a lot of turbulence and change," Glowmade studio head and co-founder Jonny Hopper told attendees at this year's GamesIndustry.biz HR Summit. "We have to be mindful about the impact that change has when we're talking about culture and business strategy and how it affects the studio kind of sense memory in the studio as a whole."
He referenced an acronym introduced by the US Army War College in 1987: VUCA, which stands for volatility, uncertainty, complexity and ambiguity.
"It highlights an environment where change is constant, cause and effect is hard to predict, and clear answers are rare," Hopper said.
While it may not be possible to fully remove that uncertainty, there are things leaders can do to cultivate a culture of reassurance, praise, and motivation – something that Hopper, along with Playstack's CEO Harvey Elliott and people operations manager Carmen Martino, discussed during their talks at the summit.
What is company culture?
First, it's important to define what 'company culture' even means: Hopper acknowledged that it is a "weird, woolly thing."
"I think culture is what happens when no one's looking, what the kids do when the parents aren't around," he said. "You can't not have a culture. You absolutely can have a good one or a bad one. You know your culture's thriving when people are doing the right thing because it's the right thing to do, and they know it's the right thing to do."
Continuing on his parenting theme, Hopper likened it to raising a teenager, loosening the reins and letting them learn from their own mistakes.
"You let them have their own victories as well, but you're always there as a guide and a safety net to keep things moving in the right direction. And kind of like parenting, culture is not a neat Venn diagram where everything kind of overlaps. You can't just sit down for an hour and fix or decide your studio culture. There's no such one recipe for it that makes it all work."
Ahead of his talk, he asked the Glowmade team what made up the studio's culture, and the results varied. It was the abundance of parrot emojis on the company Slack channels, and the internet dev tool called Chad (and the fact no one knows why). It's the swing sofa that no one has ever sat in, and the Pastry Wednesday tradition (an 11am break to chat and eat pastries). It's the internal game jams, the Friday Love-In (celebrating people who did particularly good work each week), and the fact Hopper forgets at least one birthday shoutout in the Tuesday meetings.
"Some of these things are active decisions, like the Glow Jams, the Friday Love-In, and Pastry Wednesday," Hopper says. "Some of them just emerge."
Be clear about your values
Company values are far from buzzwords that help you win over potential applicants. They can also be essential in ensuring everyone on your staff is aligned with your projects and processes. Even if you're not completely sure how you're going to realise those values, setting yourself a direction is key.
"You need a purpose, a thing that makes you special," Playstack's Harvey Elliott explained. "We said we're going to reinvent publishing. Publishing didn't necessarily need reinventing and we didn't necessarily know how to reinvent it, but we felt that those two things were important to set out what we wanted to be. It just sets an expectation that when they're coming into business, they know where they are, they know what their purpose is, and actually that cascades down through everything we do."
He continued: "We can all write values, but with everything in a company you start, you push the little snowball across the top of the hill and hope that it rolls down and gains momentum."
Hopper revealed that he shows the same slides about the company values and each project in every Tuesday meeting, emphasising the same game pillars, milestones, and goals each week.
"I'm going to test the team one week by taking out little bits and they've got to tell me what's missing," he said. "I want people to know it so that they intuitively are aligned. Even if they're parroting it, they still know it. And then if they get a crazy idea, you know that it's grounded in the pillars and the one liners and the milestones. You know you can trust they are going in the right direction, because everyone has this consistent and shared vision."
Communicate frequently and clearly
Regular communication can be the bedrock of a company's culture, as it keeps everyone on the same page.
Playstack's Carmen Martino said that all staff are given updates on the overall business, as well as any cool stuff each team is working on, every six weeks. She emphasised that simply talking to employees isn't enough; you also need to convey this information through different channels, such as internal websites or chat platforms, to account for people with different learning patterns.
In his own talk, Hopper offered a few warnings about the nature of communication:
- When you think you're being clear, you're not being clear enough.
- If there is a way someone can get confused about something, they probably will.
- If there is a way someone can take what you said and get worried about it, they probably will.
These principles can be particularly important when staff are on edge about the state of the industry or your company.
"We all think we're saying the right things and we might be, but we need to say it again and again," he said. "We need to assume people will get confused or misunderstand, just because communication is really, really, really hard. And we need to give context: they need to understand why we're making these decisions, why we are telling them things again."
Give staff a voice and trust them
Company culture shouldn't be defined by the leaders alone, it should be informed by everyone. As such, Martino emphasised the need for open feedback.
"For people who don't want to talk to me directly, we have this suggestion box, which is anonymous," she said. "They know who replies – normally me or Harvey or Kevin [Shrapnell, COO] – but we don't know who they are. That's very useful, I get a lot of ideas for new things to action thanks to this."
Hopper, meanwhile, urged leaders to show their staff trust and empowerment, two things he believes are core pillars of a positive company culture.
"As things get tough, there's always a temptation to micromanage, because then we have more control," he said. "This applies to all the people in our teams, because they're on this roller-coaster as well.
"When you're in an uncertain situation and you're trying to minimise risk, your first response to any new idea is going to be, 'Well, these are the problems it's going to cause, here's why it might not work', because you want to protect yourself from further failure or disappointment. But instead of that, we need to be asking 'What if this goes right?' As leaders in our industry, we need to be leading this by example."
Encouraging teams to experiment in this way – within reason, of course, and if they show themselves to be aligned with the project direction – can be a great way of showing them trust. Telling staff you trust them is never as effective as actually giving them the freedom to prove themselves.
Celebrate successes
In tough times such as these, it can be hard not to focus only on the negatives. Take the wins, however small, and celebrate people's good work to improve the atmosphere within your teams.
Elliott said this is emphasised at his firm through the 'One Playstack' principle, explaining: "We had a team that were really successful sitting next to a team whose projects were not working out how they wanted. It's great to be able to say to people who are doing really badly 'that's okay, that's why we're part of One Playstack'. It's so we can take these chances and if we drop the ball or miss something, that's OK because somebody else is going to be doing really well next to me."
Elliott acknowledged that this is one of the "hardest things to juggle and balance", adding that Playstack is now in the habit of celebrating every success with cake for the whole team (even remote workers).
Break the year up
When you're working on a long-term project, such as games development, the end can seem further away than it is. Leaders suggest breaking up the year with informal events, whether it's a team meal or something more extravagant (if you can afford it).
Playstack, for example, now has an annual winter retreat where the team takes a short break together, first to Prague in 2024, and then to Marbella last year. There is still some work involved, with each team giving a presentation on their learnings or other things they can share for the benefit of the company. Nonetheless, Elliott reported this "has become something people really look forward to."
Martino added that the same principle can be used with more formal processes. Rather than making staff wait a whole year for their personal review, Playstack now holds them twice a year.
"We give people feedback, but also the opportunity to stop and think how much they have grown, who has grown the most, and who deserves a promotion," she explained. "It's very good, especially for junior professionals, to see a break within the 12 months."
Take inspiration from elsewhere
You don't need to reinvent the wheel when it comes to building your company culture. Draw on ideas you've seen work elsewhere, and not just at other games companies.
Elliott revealed that Playstack now has an internal initiative called Destination 50, which was inspired by the Destination 13 program run at his kids' school. In that instance, it's about preparing young people for the choices they make at 13 for what they want to do next (e.g. which GSCEs they wish to take). In the case of Destination 50, it's about Playstack leaders considering what it would take for anyone joining the company to earn £50,000 per year.
"We were thinking, 'What's a living salary in London where you could maybe get your own place or have a share in a place?'," Elliott said. "That number goes up with inflation, but Destination 52,375 doesn't sound as good.
"Destination 50 was about making sure we have the steps to get to that, and that's why the biannual reviews became so important. You can't just say 'we'll give you more money', you need them to progress."
You can't please everyone
Another crucial thing to bear in mind is that you cannot mould your company culture to suit every single employee, especially as your workforce begins to grow.
"Don't waste your time trying to make everybody happy," Hopper warned. "Don't waste your time being sad that not everybody's happy. You're never going to make everybody happy.
"As leadership in a studio, you need to be clear what your values are, what your direction is, and just do that, because if we are decent people – and I like to think we're decent people – we are going to be doing what's right throughout for our team."
You don't have to do it all at once
Elliott cracked out the age-old adage: How do you eat an elephant? (One bite at a time, before you start Googling the answer.)
Company culture is not fully formed the moment you start your studio and define your values. It evolves over time, especially as you grow and shrink, and the perks that successful companies offer staff weren't always in place.
Elliott and Martino presented a graph at the summit that showed how its staff benefits were introduced over the first ten years of the company. It started with a private pension, followed by dental care and eye care, a government-funded cycle-to-work scheme, and yes, eventually, the winter retreat.
Reminder: Your games are a reflection of the people who make them
Interesting. Creative. Deep. Weird. Over the top. These are adjectives that Hopper said could just as easily be used to describe Glowmade's developers as the games they make.
Because your people are such an integral part of the games you make or the business you run, it's crucial to think about how you're looking after them, nurturing and encouraging them, and driving them to do better.
"We all know and feel that good studio culture is important, and we feel like it's going to impact the quality of the product at the end," Hopper said. "But I don't think there's a linear progression between 'good studio culture equals a good game'. There's no guarantee that with the best studio, you're gonna get a decent game at the end, and there are ways that you won't get a decent game.
"Bad management, toxic culture, toxic behaviours – all of these things impact the quality of the product in the end, and those things can derail even the most talented team and the most groundbreaking ideas."
