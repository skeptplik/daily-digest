The government is to back legislation aimed at preventing wealthy and powerful individuals using the courts to silence critics and curb free speech.
The justice minister Sarah Sackman told parliament on Friday that Labour would support a private member’s bill sponsored by the Conservative MP Sir John Whittingdale, who is spearheading the latest attempt to bring in anti-Slapp measures.
She told the Guardian: “Our legal system should be a route to justice, not a weapon of intimidation, used to evade scrutiny, suppress reporting or discourage people from speaking the truth.”
The bill attempts to define abusive cases, often referred to as strategic litigation against public participation, or Slapps, so that they can be dismissed early before defendants incur unaffordable legal costs.
However, campaigners have criticised the draft legislation, saying it will not work in its current form because the process of dismissing claims will be too challenging and costly for defendants.
The government’s decision marks the latest acknowledgment by Labour of the need to rebalance a legal system that too often favours powerful individuals or organisations over those who seek to hold them to account, be they victims of abuse, whistleblowers, journalists or academics.
“Speaking out against wrongdoing takes courage, whether you’re a journalist exposing corruption, a whistleblower or a victim finding the strength to talk about abuse,” said Sackman, who announced the government’s support after the bill’s second reading on Friday.
“Too often, wealthy or powerful individuals use the threat of expensive legal action to silence people. Not because they expect to win, but because they know the process itself can be punishing.”
Her intervention follows an announcement by the prime minister, Andy Burnham, earlier this week that the government would bring forward changes to non-disclosure agreements (NDAs) to ensure they cannot be used to hide crimes.
The debate around the use of legal threats against victims has been reopened in the wake of allegations against David Walliams of improper behaviour – which he denies – and the conviction of Russell Brand for rape and sexual assault.
Labour’s support means Whittingdale’s bill now has a better chance of passing in to law, but success is not guaranteed – most private members’ bills are defeated or struggle to secure the time and attention needed.
Even if it passes, campaigners say the bill does not go far enough, and risks sitting on the statute books unused.
The UK Anti-Slapp Coalition said last week it could not endorse the bill. It has concerns about the early dismissal mechanism, which is supposed to allow abusive cases to be dismissed long before reaching trial.
“The legislation has the potential to make a significant difference in people’s lives, but it will only live up to this potential if the mechanism contained in the law works in practice,” the coalition said. “Crucially, as currently drafted the early dismissal mechanism is too complicated, imposes too high a bar to be used, and creates financial risk for Slapp targets which will prevent it from being used.”
The bill sets out three tests for identifying Slapps. Defendants have to show the dispute relates to a matter of public interest, the claimant’s behaviour is restraining their right to free of speech, and is intended to cause them harm through expense, harassment, alarm or distress.
Proving a claimant’s intentions is notoriously difficult, and defendants may in effect be forced to undergo a mini-trial in order to have the case dismissed. The coalition said it was ready to work with ministers to improve the wording.
However, it is understood the government is keen to strike a balance between tackling abusive litigation and ensuring legitimate access to the courts.
An earlier private member’s bill sponsored by the Labour MP Wayne David received cross-party support and the backing of the last Conservative government, but it did not complete its passage through parliament before the 2024 general election. Later that year, the then prime minister, Keir Starmer, promised legislation, but the government did not put forward its own bill.
Some protection exists, thanks to legislation introduced in 2023, but it applies only in cases where litigants are accused of economic crime. The new bill widens the scope to cover sexual abuse, corruption and other wrongdoing.
Whittingdale said he was “delighted to work with the minister on a cross-party basis in order to achieve the widest level of support to stop this abuse”.
