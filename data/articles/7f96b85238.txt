Eight years after a ball-tampering scandal rocked Australian cricket, Usman Khawaja's bombshell autobiography has reopened old wounds.
During a 2018 Test against South Africa in Cape Town, Cameron Bancroft was caught on camera using sandpaper to roughen one side of the ball in an attempt to generate reverse swing.

A Cricket Australia investigation found captain Steve Smith, vice-captain David Warner and Bancroft were involved in the scheme, handing them suspensions.
But Khawaja has now challenged that version of events, alleging the practice extended beyond Cape Town and that the wider squad knew what was happening.
The renewed controversy has prompted Cricket Australia to establish a forum where former players and staff can share their recollections, though emphasising the original investigation will not reopen.
Here's a collection of what players and coaches have said — then and now — about the saga that became widely known as Sandpapergate.
The players
In one chapter of his new autobiography, Sabr, Khawaja provided a detailed account of what he says happened during Australia's 2018 South African tour.
The former batter, who retired this year, alleged sandpaper was used on the ball during the second Test in Port Elizabeth, weeks before Bancroft was caught on camera in Cape Town.
Khawaja described a discussion before the first Test in Durban, alleging a member of the coaching staff told the team they needed to find a way to make the ball move in the air.
"We were all complicit," he wrote.
Khawaja described how Bancroft picked up the ball after a break in play during the Port Elizabeth Test and used sandpaper to roughen one side.
He said the ball was then passed around the fielding team, including to him at gully, and that he noticed how dramatically it was reverse-swinging.
Khawaja recalled one bowler commenting during a huddle:
"Guys, it's going heaps now. Just don't do anything else to it, because it's silly how much it's going."
He alleged a member of the coaching staff later approached Bancroft during the Cape Town Test and encouraged him to do more of what he had been doing to the ball.
Khawaja said that after Bancroft was caught, he argued with team manager Gavin Dovey that everyone should accept responsibility.
"We need to cop this as a team," he wrote.
"We did it as a team, so we cop it as a team."
Khawaja said most players agreed when he raised the issue at a subsequent team meeting, but then-captain Tim Paine knew not everyone supported the idea.
The chapter ends with him saying, "I can only speak for myself, and it's very clear I knew what was going on."
In a subsequent interview with the ABC's 7.30, Khawaja said he wanted to tell his story and stand by the three players who had been made out to be the only people involved.
"It wasn't just them," he told Sarah Ferguson.
"We were all involved in it as a team, as a squad."
He declined to speculate about whether individual bowlers knew sandpaper was being used.
"Twenty-two pages is what sandpaper is," he said.
"I've tried my best in that to explain my version of events."
Warner has strongly endorsed Khawaja's account of how the ball-tampering scheme unfolded.
"It's 100 per cent accurate what you've said," the former vice-captain said in a social media video.
"Now everyone knows the truth."
Warner was suspended from domestic and international cricket as a result of the sandpaper plot, and was also permanently barred from leadership positions.
In the recent social media video, he criticised Cricket Australia's original investigation, describing it as "rushed" and "botched".
In further social media posts, Warner questioned whether the bowlers were properly interviewed, saying he had not been told they had provided statements.
"That stinks of a cover-up," he said, though multiple media outlets, including ABC Sport, have reported the bowlers were interviewed by Cricket Australia.
Warner also criticised Cummins after the Australia captain rejected Khawaja's allegations.
"I feel the captain today threw Ussie [Khawaja] under the bus and I'm very, very disappointed by that," he said.
Warner argued that he and the other two sanctioned players were left to carry the consequences without the full circumstances being made public.
"We've been served a life punishment," he said.
"It was a quick one to get it over and done with. Some of the questions were quite challenging in the way it was being planted on us, and we had to move on and just accept responsibility."
Australian fast bowler Starc has rejected Khawaja's claim the ball-tampering plot was known by all members of the team.
"I won't speak on behalf of the bowlers, but where I stand, my opinion hasn't changed," Starc said on November 10.
"We weren't aware anything was taken on the field to be tampered with."
In 2021, Starc joined fellow bowlers Cummins, Josh Hazlewood and Nathan Lyon in releasing a statement disputing any suggestion they had wider knowledge of the scheme.
"We did not know a foreign substance was taken onto the field to alter the condition of the ball until we saw the images on the big screen at Newlands [in Cape Town]," the 2021 statement said.
Starc also said he knew nothing about Khawaja's claim that sandpaper had been used to tamper with the ball in the Test before Cape Town.
White-ball captain Mitchell Marsh refused to address key aspects of the scandal at a press conference last week, saying it was not time for him to share his story.
"I've personally moved on," he said.
"I respect that others probably haven't, but I have, and I hope that's respected.
"This is my choice to not go back there. Who knows? Maybe I'll write a book one day."
Marsh, who played in all four Tests of Australia's 2018 series against South Africa, said he held no grudges against Khawaja.
"I respect Uzzy for telling his story and for writing his book, and I don't sit here coming from a place of judgement," he said.
"Everyone's got their story. Some will be willing to share it, and others might not, purely on the basis of the fact that they've moved on.
"I think it's really important that at some stage we do."
Cummins, who was a key bowler at the time, has strongly rejected suggestions he knew about or helped organise the ball-tampering scheme.
At a lengthy press conference on November 13, the now-Test captain said Khawaja had been "casual" with people's lives and reputations.
"When he says 'we all knew', if by that he means we knew sandpaper was going out on the field, he's 100 per cent wrong," he said.
"But I think Usman actually says things that are far more serious than that.
"He says that I and others were architects of a scheme to ball tamper with sandpaper; that's complete and utter nonsense."
Cummins said he had consistently maintained for the past eight years that he did not know sandpaper was being used.
He also said he did not remember any conversations around the team taking collective responsibility, as Khawaja claimed in his memoir he had pushed for.
"In his book, Uzzy's been casual with my life and the lives of many others, and I think Australian cricket," he said.
"I support his right to tell a story. I'm really glad that he feels like he can. But we have to be accountable for the things that we say and do."
Following Bancroft's latest allegations, Cummins's agent said his position had not changed:
"Whilst we feel for Cam [Bancroft] and everything he's been through, there's nothing in the statement that hasn't been answered by Pat in his press conference."
Sayers, who was part of Australia's 2018 touring squad, has also backed Khawaja's account.
Speaking to SEN in a radio interview on Sunday, the one-time Test fast bowler said:
"I believe that everyone in that side knew what was going on and what was being used."
Sayers said there had been discussions about the use of sandpaper to tamper the ball and that he was "neutral in all this".
"I'm just trying to tell my side of the story and what I believe happened," he told SEN.
"My recollection isn't as clear as it was obviously eight years ago.
"I just know that there was numerous conversations of it around in the change rooms."
He added that he was relieved the matter was finally being discussed openly after years of players having to remain silent:
"It's been hard because you've obviously got to tip-toe around and not be able to tell the truth."
Bancroft, who was caught on camera using sandpaper during the Cape Town Test in 2018, has made some of the most significant new allegations.
In a lengthy account shared with the media on Wednesday, he said the original public version of events did not reflect what he experienced during the tour.
"It was not just the three of us — Steve Smith, David Warner and I — who knew about me using sandpaper on the ball in South Africa," he writes.
"It was the whole team."
Bancroft said Warner proposed the idea and that he regularly discussed the ball's reverse swing with the bowlers, including Cummins, Mitchell Starc, Josh Hazlewood and Mitchell Marsh.
"How else am I to know if what I'm doing is effective or not if I don't have these conversations with the bowlers bowling with it and the wicketkeeper?" he writes.
"All the bowlers gave me feedback if my work on the ball was working. It was helpful."
Bancroft also named former bowling coach David Saker, claiming he received encouragement from players and coaches about his work on the ball.
He also alleged former head coach Darren Lehmann knew what was happening.
"This event is crystal clear in my mind. Not only for the praise I received, but [because] I also remember a very different reaction," he wrote.
"Our media manager, Kate Hutchison, expressed her objection to what was happening.
"I recall her making it clear that she believed it was wrong, that she wanted nothing to do with it, and walking away from the discussion.
"I will never forget that. Somebody had spoken up."
Bancroft said he withheld important details during the original investigation, including when the sandpaper use began, how long it continued and the involvement or awareness of others.
He said Cummins's recent denial was "the final straw" that prompted him to speak publicly.
"It made me feel angry, betrayed and alone," he wrote.
"What I saw, experienced and lived with — not only him, but everyone else on that tour in South Africa — is not the public account he gave in his press conference and in the years previously when he has spoken on this time."
Bancroft's international career was the worst affected of the sanctioned trio, making only two more appearances for Australia after the test series against South Africa in 2018.
Steve Smith, the captain of the side during the ball-tampering scandal, has been at his New York home while all this has played out, well and truly out of the limelight.
Of course, his tears were among the most defining scenes of the entire scandal.
In an early press conference, Smith admitted "the leadership group knew about" ball tampering, but lied by saying it was not sandpaper but tape with grit on it.
He refused to name the other players involved in hatching the plan, saying that he took responsibility.
"I'm incredibly sorry for trying to bring the game into disrepute the way we did."
At his infamous press conference in Sydney, when he broke down in tears in front of the press, he said that he would "regret this for the rest of my life".
He was banned for 12 months for his part in the plot, but has since returned and re-established himself as one of the premier batters of this generation, if not all time.
Smith has not spoken since Usman Khawaja's book was released, but he has still been at the heart of the coverage.
"I told Smudge [Smith] that I was going to come clean to the media," Khawaja wrote in his book.
"I couldn't believe the penalties he got. But Smudge being the man and leader he was, he talked me out of it.
"He wanted to take the blame. I argued with him, but he was having none of it. That's the first time I try to come out."
Shaun Marsh has said nothing recently about the Sandpapergate scandal.
However, he was asked in 2022 whether he had been interviewed as part of Cricket Australia's investigation into the incident.
"I wasn't [interviewed]," Marsh told The Sydney Morning Herald and The Age.
"I was a little bit [surprised] at the time. That's all I'll say."
Tim Paine became captain of the Australian cricket team following the events of Cape Town.
He was stripped of the captaincy in 2021 following allegations of him sending inappropriate messages to a female colleague.
In his autobiography, Paine said he and the rest of the team were "stunned" at the footage of Bancroft and the sandpaper.
"Everyone out there was shocked when they looked up on the big screen and saw Cameron Bancroft with a piece of sandpaper in his hand," Paine wrote.
"I was stunned. We all were.
"My heart sank, I was thinking, 'What the f***?' A sense of dread came over us all."
Cameron Bancroft does mention Paine in his piece, but does not specifically say whether he spoke to him about the use of sandpaper.
"I also spoke with Tim Paine, who was in a key position behind the stumps to observe how the ball was moving," Bancroft wrote.
"How else am I to know if what I'm doing is effective or not if I don't have these conversations with the bowlers bowling with it and the wicketkeeper?"
Hazlewood has kept his counsel since Usman Khawaja released his book.
However, he is referenced in the pages of Sabr, albeit indirectly.
In Sabr, Khawaja writes that: "One of the bowlers went on [Fox Sports's] The Back Page TV show and was asked point blank if he'd known anything about the ball tampering.
"He flat out denied it. David [Warner] was seething, and understandably so. I remember him texting me and sending the clip.
"I didn't mind the guys staying quiet. Hell, I was doing the same thing. But to come out and say that on live TV really pissed me off. If you aren't willing to tell the whole story, just stay quiet.''
Hazlewood was at least sticking to his guns, having denied all knowledge of the plot along with his fellow bowling unit.
They even released a statement along those lines in 2021, reiterating that they had no idea.
Bancroft said reading that statement was "deeply painful".
"I had memories of conversations on the field in Port Elizabeth and Newlands, feedback about the ball and shared celebrations that made the statement difficult for me to digest."
Like Hazlewood, Nathan Lyon has kept quiet since the revelations started tumbling from the Australian Test team.
He did, alongside Mitch Starc, Pat Cummins and Josh Hazlewood, release a statement in 2021 in response to a Guardian interview that Cam Bancroft gave while he was playing County cricket.
The statement read: "We pride ourselves on our honesty. So it's been disappointing to see that our integrity has been questioned by some journalists and past players in recent days in regard to the Cape Town Test of 2018.
"We have already answered questions many times on this issue, but we feel compelled to put the key facts on the record again:
"We did not know a foreign substance was taken onto the field to alter the condition of the ball until we saw the images on the big screen at Newlands
"And to those who, despite the absence of evidence, insist that 'we must have known' about the use of a foreign substance simply because we are bowlers, we say this: The umpires during that Test match, Nigel Llong and Richard Illingworth, both very respected and experienced umpires, inspected the ball after the images surfaced on the TV coverage and did not change it because there was no sign of damage.
"None of this excuses what happened on the field that day at Newlands. It was wrong and it should never have happened.
"We've all learned valuable lessons and we'd like to think the public can see a change for the better in terms of the way we play, the way we behave and respect the game. Our commitment to improving as people and players will continue.
"We respectfully request an end to the rumour-mongering and innuendo.
"It has gone on too long and it is time to move on."
Nic Maddinson played his five Tests so far either side of the 2018 South Africa series, a decade-long gap in part due to a diagnosis and recovery from testicular cancer.
So he was not on hand to be a part of the scandal in South Africa eight years ago.
It has not stopped him from chipping in since then, though.
"It's obviously disappointing that it keeps coming up," Maddinson told Channel 10 after Sabr was released.
"But it's time of year, isn't it? Where Christmas comes along and people have books to sell, so things come out.
"It's just disappointing. I think the three guys that have been affected the most, it doesn't really help them at all to relive it.
"Especially over the past month, having a great series in South Africa, obviously the crowd wasn't as hostile, maybe, as what people expected. But that stuff's still there every day at airports, and the general public making comments here and there. So I guess over time it would wear them down.
"For it to pop up again, the start of the summer, going into a pretty big six months for the [Australia] team, it's obviously probably disappointing for those guys."
Scott Boland didn't make his Test debut until Boxing Day 2021 against England, so he was nowhere near the sandpaper controversy.
Nevertheless, he was caught in the crossfire of Usman Khawaja's book when Khawaja noted that Victoria was the best at "the dark arts" in Australian domestic cricket.
"I don't know what the 'dark arts' are. You probably have to ask Usman for that," Boland said ahead of Victoria's first Sheffield Shield game since the book was released.
"I don't know — if you went back to club cricket, without cameras, you would probably see a lot of things happen.
"From my point of view, I haven't really seen anything untoward."
The coaches
The coaches have also begun responding to the latest allegations.
Former head coach Darren Lehmann has addressed Cameron Bancroft's claims without taking a stance, while batting coach Graeme Hick has rejected suggestions he knew about the ball tampering.
Former bowling coach David Saker has not commented on the latest revelations, but his role has come under renewed scrutiny.
Lehmann was head coach of the Australian team in Cape Town.
In the immediate aftermath, he specifically denied any knowledge of plans to use sandpaper and was cleared by Cricket Australia's investigation, but resigned from the role after the Cape Town Test.
Usman Khawaja did not specify Lehmann by name in Sabr, but Bancroft sure did, saying that the former coach shouted at Peter Handscomb to run down onto the pitch and "get the f****** sandpaper off Bangers now," he wrote in his West Australian article.
"In my opinion, if he didn't know, he wouldn't have said that.
"And if it wasn't clear enough before, then it was obvious now."
Bancroft also said Lehmann was present when David Warner suggested using sandpaper, but said he could not take it onto the ground because everyone would be looking at him.
Lehmann, who was working as a pundit on ABC Sport during the coverage of Australia's ODI clash with England on Wednesday night, made a short statement on air before leaving the media centre.
"The allegations … I've got to sit back. It's only just come to light," Lehmann said on ABC Sport.
"So I just need to sit back and process all that and work through each allegation. That's what I will be doing over the next few days.
"I need to have a really good read of it, don't I? What's been transpired, speaking to people, and just make sure I'm saying what I want to say and answer the questions you want to ask.
"It's a lot for everyone I presume. Yeah, not just me.
"That's all you can do. The allegations and work through them and then respond accordingly. That would be the way to go. There's no point rushing into anything that's said. Just make sure I'm really clear which way I'm going."
