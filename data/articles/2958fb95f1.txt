Will the men’s grooming market surge due to artificial intelligence?
Given guys’ uptake of the technology, there could well be a swell of males entering the category as well as a rise in purchasing among those already there, industry experts believe.
The men’s grooming market is expected to generate an estimated $68.61 billion this year, up 6.6 percent versus 2025, according to Euromonitor International.
“Although globally many men have embraced beauty and personal care routines, with 36 percent claiming ‘looking my best’ is more important to them than it was five years ago, there remains a significant opportunity to bring male consumers deeper into the category, particularly within skin care,” said Eric Mills, beauty thought leadership consultant at NIQ.
“Men tend to default to brands they already trust, historically creating a narrower set of options,” he added. “What is changing this dynamic today is the widespread use of AI to research and shop for products, an area 52 percent of men are interested in.”
Mills highlighted that AI chatbots allow men to ask questions without perceived judgment as they begin their beauty and personal care journey, which helps reduce the complexity of the category and allows them to build a routine.
“From a statistics perspective, shopping and answer engines like ChatGPT are still dominated by a set of early adopters,” said Emily Pfeiffer, a principal analyst at Forrester. She outlined four demographic features of the cohorts most likely to be turning to answer-engine shopping: those who are younger — Gen Z specifically, higher educated, higher income and identifying as male.
When Forrester recently asked which of the following tech a person has used to search for products, in all categories, they were interested in buying over the past month, respondents identifying as male were 50 percent more likely to be using ChatGPT for that purpose than those identifying as female.
Experts share hypotheses as to why: Maybe women are more likely to chat with each other for advice about what to buy. There could be grooming-related questions men don’t want to ask each other. And then there is the stigma some men feel about entering beauty sellers in real life and seeking advice from store associates.
Having ChatGPT as the preferred option for many men could have wide-reaching effects. “This has the potential to move the male shopper from making the occasional purchase of a basic moisturizer to better understanding the needs of their skin and exploring brands they may not have been exposed to before,” Mills said.
Men are turning to AI-generated apps for advice on style recommendations, too. Beauty.ai launched a year ago as an app designed to help people style their existing clothing wardrobes, get fashion styling tips or buy garments from Amazon and other platforms.
“When I started, I was thinking that mostly we would have women, but now it’s 50-50,” said Beauty.ai founder David Voskanyan, who finds female and male shopping behavior to differ. Women spend more time inside the app making decisions on which outfits would be best.
“For men it’s much faster,” Voskanyan said. “They just open the app, receive recommendations and choose maybe the first option. Men are more likely to shop online. It is definitely a changing industry.”
Voskanyan might integrate some beauty features into his platform. “That’s why I call my project Beauty.ai, because in the future I want to expand it to other industries, as well,” he said.
Cuttr AI and HairGO AI are among the AI-powered grooming and virtual hairstyle apps already designed specifically for men, while Envive is an AI search-optimized guide for men’s grooming brands.
AI is being used to speak with men in a variety of ways, including through advertising. Springboards.ai, an Australia-based start-up, has created a suite of AI tools built for creative jobs, including in agencies, to help people come up with ideas.
“We do that by allowing people to explore more creative territories than they could by themselves,” said Pip Bingemann, a cofounder and chief executive officer of Springboards.ai.
L’Oréal uses Springboards.ai as a creative brainstorming tool in the Asia-Pacific zone. One campaign launched recently was for CeraVe that is focused on men in Australia. The speed to market for bringing this campaign out into the world was ramped up, taking about six weeks rather than 16 weeks.
“In the CeraVe example, they explored 40 different campaign territories, jumping from five or six different insights before they then landed on a core concept that they took through to production,” Bingemann said.
That became sports, with the tag line for the 360-degree campaign being “CeraVe, Your Skin Barrier’s MVP.” It kicked off at the same time as Australia’s major rugby and soccer finals.
“For a very long time, advertising has been obsessed with personalization at scale, and they’ve never had a tool to do that,” Bingemann said. “AI allows you to do that, where you can take a core concept and create multiple variations very quickly, targeting all sorts of different segments — be it males all the way to tiny niche segments.”
AI opens realms of far-reaching possibilities. “We’ve got this new tool, and we’re still trying to work out what’s the best way to harness that,” Bingemann said.
