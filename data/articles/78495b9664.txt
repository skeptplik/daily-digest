Annette Farrington-Moore says a charity became a lifeline after her breast cancer diagnosis, and now the group that helped her is celebrating a major milestone with a royal seal of approval.
Run by volunteers with lived experience of the disease, Breast Friends Solihull provides emotional and practical support with many involved forging lifelong friendships.
"You don't want to trouble family and they don't really understand what you're going through emotionally," she said.
"The kids were there for me but when I found out about Breast Friends it was like the best thing ever."
Those who run the group firmly believe that no-one should go through breast cancer alone.
The organisation has supported thousands of people affected by breast cancer over the last 25 years, and has juar been awarded the King's Award for Voluntary Service.
Annette, 64, was diagnosed with breast cancer last year. Widowed and feeling low, she found solace in attending meetings and events put on by the group.
"Whenever you're ill and you haven't got your husband, there's no one there to look after you like they would," she said.
"So having Breast Friends, you know there's somebody on the end of the phone who can be there for you."
The charity provides services such as gardening and cleaning to allow people to rest during treatment, as well as support with bras, wigs and hospital visits.
It has also raised money to fund hospital equipment.
Chris Pile was diagnosed with breast cancer 20 years ago, when she was a nurse and mother of two young children.
She is now vice-chair of the group and helps lift others when they're down.
"I joined Breast Friends when I finished my chemotherapy and radiotherapy, and I found it really uplifting," she explained.
Everybody was so positive and friendly, and I got involved straight away because that's the kind of person I am.
"I've organised days and nights out, summer suppers and the Christmas dos."
Chair Deborah Douglas has had breast cancer twice, first in 2003 and again earlier this year.
She said she has now been given "the all clear" following treatment.
Her experiences mean she knows first-hand what support to offer, explaining that a diagnosis can be "scary and frightening".
Her first diagnosis led her to seek help from Breast Friends.
"I walked in and I saw women who weren't sad. They were all happy and so uplifting, and they basically supported me from the moment I walked in.
"It was such an uplifting experience, and I always leave Breast Friends smiling," she added.
"We laugh, but we also talk about our issues and how we're feeling.
"That emotional support is so important because the people around you know exactly what you're going through. People don't always want to share those things with their family."
More than 20 years on, she is still involved as the group's chair.
