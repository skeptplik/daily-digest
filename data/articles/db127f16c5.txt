'Brexit means Brexit' no more? Why the U.K. now wants closer ties with the EU
U.K. Prime Minister Andy Burnham has opened the door to a 'Breturn'
Remember that old slogan, "Brexit means Brexit," in the wake of Britain’s decision to leave the European Union after the 2016 referendum pitted leavers against remainers?
It was relentlessly repeated by former British prime minister Theresa May — herself a remainer — as she sought to convince her own governing Conservatives that she wouldn’t allow any backsliding on the decision while negotiating the terms of Britain’s exit from the EU.
Well, it turns out Brexit might not have meant Brexit after all. At least, the door to a "Breturn," as they’re already calling it here, has been nudged open a crack or two by Britain’s Labour Prime Minister Andy Burnham.
He’s been flying a few trial balloons.
"In the decade since Brexit, our standing in the world has diminished," he told a black-tie event at Guildhall in the city of London last week, just days before an EU-U.K. Summit heralded a deal to reduce some red tape on exports and improve student mobility in both directions.
"We treated both allies and institutions with insufficient care."
Perhaps one reason he’s becoming bolder on the issue is because opinion polls are on his side.
Fifty-eight percent of Britons now think leaving the EU was a mistake, according to a recent YouGov poll that surveyed 2,019 people.
"The majority of Brits realized that they were lied to by various politicians … and they have actually woken up to the fact that they’re not better off," said Chris Riddell, a political cartoonist for the Observer newspaper who would like to see the U.K. rejoin the EU.
"I’ve been drawing a Brexit ogre for the last decade. I’m looking forward to drawing maybe a tombstone and he’s been laid to rest, finally."
One of political cartoonist Chris Riddell's depictions of the Brexit ogre. (Chris Riddell)
Among the most toxic debates in recent U.K. history
Still, Burnham is treading carefully, talking about resetting relations before any moves toward rejoining, insisting he’ll never move ahead of British public opinion.
The Brexit debate was perhaps one of the most toxic in recent British history, with 51.9 per cent voting to leave and 48.1 per cent voting to stay. Many of those in favour of leaving argued that Brussels had too much influence over U.K. decision-making, especially on the control of its borders.
Since leaving the EU, the U.K.'s GDP per capita is now six to eight per cent lower than it would have been had the country remained, by one estimate.
In the wake of the EU-U.K. summit, all five of the Conservative prime ministers from the 2016 referendum on have risen like ghosts of Christmases past to warn Burnham against reopening the debate.
David Cameron, who campaigned to remain after calling the referendum and then resigned after the result, told Bloomburg that Britain’s poor economic growth was not, as Burnham argued, because of Brexit.
And Theresa May, of "Brexit means Brexit" fame, told the BBC that she was "aghast" at the talk of rejoining, saying Burnham needed to "move on."
May was eventually felled by divisions within her Conservative Party over how "hard" Britain’s exit from the EU should be and replaced by Boris Johnson, who recently told the Daily Telegraph that Britain would still back Brexit today.
Liz Truss, who became the shortest-serving prime minister in U.K. history after replacing Johnson, said the idea of Britain "reshackling" itself to the EU was "sheer lunacy."

Her successor, Rishi Sunak, said it would be a "huge distraction."
It could well be a cautionary tale for Burnham, given the role that Brexit played in much of the Conservative Party’s infighting. But he said on Monday that he wouldn't be listening to them.
"They left Britain with a bad Brexit deal that has hit growth over the past decade," he told British media. "They’re stuck in the past because they can’t face up to the bad deal that they did."
Pro-Brexit supporters attend the Brexit Day Celebration Party hosted by Leave Means Leave at Parliament Square in January 2020 in London. (Jeff J. Mitchell/Getty Images)
Economist Patrick Minford, who campaigned for Brexit, argues Britain’s sluggish economy isn’t suffering from leaving the EU, but from bad management.
"The EU would have enormous power over us, through its ability to regulate all aspects of our lives," he told CBC News. "And that was the central non-economic issue in the debate."
New decade, new world
But Burnham and other supporters of rejoining the EU argue that the world has changed over the past 10 years — and with it, attitudes toward the European Union.
Labour MP and former U.K. foreign secretary David Lammy puts part of that shift down to the Trump factor.
"Just like Canada is reeling from a different relationship with the United States, so Europe is reeling from a different relationship to the United States," he said.
"Sovereignty disappears very quickly in the current geopolitical environment, unless you are in concert with other shared friends."
He’s not surprised that Canada has been exploring closer ties with the European Union.
"The space between us is so slim we’re better huddled together in the best formation," he said.
"That does not, of course, mean that we do not have the closest of relationships with our friends in the United States."
Reopening old wounds
Even some Britons who voted to remain in the EU worry about the possibility of reopening old wounds.
"So there’s an aspect where you just say, 'Well, hold on a minute. If people have voted, you do have to respect it,'" said Bleue Granada, a retired woman.
"But at the same time, I think we need a much more intelligent debate, more informed debate. And I think that was lacking and I think people are a lot wiser now."
Bleue Granada, a retired woman in the U.K., says the country needs a more informed debate on the issue of Brexit. (Katie Pedersen/CBC)
David Arvidsson, a dual Swedish and British citizen, was studying in England when the U.K. voted to leave the EU.
"[As a] student in Cambridge, you had basically not met a single person in favour of Brexit, so it came as a shock," he said.
"[Pushing to rejoin] will lead to a lot of arguments," he said. "I do think that a majority would be in favour of it. I do feel that we lost something in the U.K. when we left the EU."
Kenneth Robbie, another resident, says he decided how to vote on his way to the polls during the last referendum, eventually casting his ballot to remain.
"I just hope that if it’s going to become a live, red-hot issue again, that we can discuss it calmly and avoid another referendum, because the referendum 10 years ago, I think, was very divisive."
'Unthinkable' to rejoin without referendum
But when pressed on the questions of a referendum in Parliament on Monday, Burnham replied that it would be "unthinkable" for any British prime minister to rejoin the EU without one.
David Lammy, the Labour MP, agrees. He wants his party to fight the next election with a commitment to rejoin the EU.
Brexit continues to be controversial, with this protest in September 2023 showing a man holding an anti-Brexit sign, in central London. (Justin Tallis/AFP/Getty Images)
"We will then have a negotiation. We will have a referendum. And the deal will be done by the election after the next one."
But it is a gamble.
The Brexit genie has never really stayed in the bottle, managing to bedevil British politics before, during and after the referendum.
