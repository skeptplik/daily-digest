The future of Marc Jacobs is being shaped right now as its still-new owners, G-III Apparel Group and WHP Global, reset the brand and try out a new approach.
It’s a delicate matter, deciding what to add and what to subtract from what’s been a cornerstone of the American designer world for a generation.
This much is clear: Marc will still be Marc and host his runway shows, the Marc by Marc Jacobs subbrand will make a comeback and the licensing frontiers will be expanded.
But what exactly is being left behind amid all of this business reinvention?
What was this business with a top-shelf brand that Bernard Arnault’s LVMH Moët Hennessy Louis Vuitton bet big on only to spend the last couple years vaguely shopping it around before settling on buyers and closing the deal in September?
A recent filing with the Securities and Exchange Commission offers up at least some of the answer, laying bare Marc Jacobs’ financial results for last year.
In 2025, Marc Jacobs produced revenues of $509 million.
Taking away $158.6 million in cost of sales, $183.8 million in selling expenses, $48.9 million in marketing expenses and $10.9 million in general expenses, that left operating profit of $106.8 million.
With interest of $35.1 million, mostly for $525 million in loans to parent company LVMH, and other items, the brand’s comprehensive income tallied $71.1 million for 2025, according to the filing.
For all of the operating company and half the brand, G-III paid $468.8 million at closing, although $40 million in restructuring funding from WHP cuts that down. WHP also put up about $425 million for its half of the brand.
That means that of the nearly $900 million paid to buy Marc Jacobs, well over half of it, $525 million, went to pay back loans the business owed to LVMH.
If taken at face value — and not seen instead as a parent company making an investment in a subsidiary that was ultimately paid back — it’s a level of indebtedness that helps explain why a change of business model was in the offing.
While Marc Jacobs is seen as having a strong licensing component, including its Daisy fragrance franchise with Coty, the business is still heavily tilted toward its own products.
Licensing revenues for the year tallied $32.1 million, a take trimmed down by $19.1 million in expenses, making licensing about 12 percent of operating profits.
Morris Goldfarb, chief executive officer and chairman of G-III, sketched out some of the rest of the business on a conference call with analysts in September.
“Marc Jacobs generates approximately two-thirds of its revenue through direct-to-consumer with over 100 company-operated stores and a robust digital platform,” Goldfarb said. “The majority of its stores are in the outlet channel and generate healthy four-wall profitability.”
On the other hand, G-III’s primary strength is in the wholesale trade.
“Marc Jacobs has touched on all categories, but the company is not focused on anything other than handbags, small leather goods and accessories,” Goldfarb said. “That’s basically been their focus, justified by the dominance they created in several handbags, and their focus on retail.
“Their stores are relatively small,” he said. “They house limited product categories. And we have a different headset. We have a template that has worked incredibly well for us, as you see with Karl Lagerfeld and DKNY and now Donna Karan. And prior to that, the monster that we built with Calvin Klein and Tommy Hilfiger that was virtually nonexistent by creating classifications that have multiple exposures in department stores… Long term, we believe Marc Jacobs can generate $1 billion in annual revenue for G-III.”
Now the question is what happens to the brand as the business settles into its new home.
