Bosses at 10 datacentre companies secured almost 120 meetings with premiers and ministers in five states over a three-year period, with experts declaring this is “just the tip of the iceberg”.
Analysis by Transparency International Australia argues these meetings, when combined with political donations, demonstrate “unfettered access” to decision-makers that creates an “uneven playing field”.
The report found donations from major datacentre companies and their contracted lobbying firms nearly tripled over the past three years from $685,000 to $1.8m, coinciding with increased investment across Australia.
The analysis found the Queensland leader, David Crisafulli, was “by far the most frequently accessed state premier”, with public diaries showing he has met datacentre executives 13 times in the past three years.
This included three meetings in as many weeks in August and September with Australia’s largest datacentre operator, NextDC – roughly one month before the company’s expansion in Melbourne was halted after its power provider delayed construction of new power poles due to community backlash.
“Those meetings took place shortly before the Queensland premier announced a dedicated framework to fast-track datacentre approvals by taking them out of the hands of local councils,” the Transparency International report said.
Crisafulli has embraced datacentre investment and flew to the United States in September to secure a $31.9bn deal with the AI giant Anthropic for what could become Australia’s largest datacentre. His office was contacted for comment.
During this three-year period, 119 meetings with ministers or premiers were held in New South Wales, Victoria, Tasmania, Queensland and the ACT. These are the only jurisdictions to publicly release ministerial diaries. The meetings were mostly held in 2026.
“These numbers are just the tip of the iceberg,” said Clancy Moore, the chief executive of Transparency International Australia.
“What we don’t know is how many meetings companies like NextDC, OpenAI and Anthropic and their third-party lobbyists are having with the prime minister and ministers in our federal parliament.
“Australia’s lobbying laws also don’t cover in-house lobbyists or government relations staff so there’s very little oversight of their activities too.”
The report also found Australian company Firmus Technologies secured 10 meetings with Tasmanian ministers and the premier this year alone. During this period, the company’s expansion in the state has faced sustained backlash from local communities who feel they were not properly consulted.
The three companies that secured the most meetings with ministers were Amazon Web Services, Macquarie Technology Group and NextDC.
Last month, Guardian Australia reported on political donations from Amazon and Macquarie to both major parties, closely aligning with the announcement of multimillion-dollar government contracts or planning developments. Both companies say these donations were not aimed at influencing policy and that their timing was coincidental.
A shareholder at NextDC’s annual general meeting on Friday said the company had “a lot of lobbying going on in Canberra” but called on the company to better lobby the public on the benefits of datacentres.
The company’s chair, Stephen Flynn, said he was “not sure we are great at lobbying as an industry or as a company” but the world had now changed.
“We thought we were just going along and [being] invisible, in the way if you think about all telecommunications systems … [it] is kind of invisible. We just take it for granted. But we can’t do that. We can’t do that as we go forward.”
The report has also cited examples of four senior public servants at federal government departments quitting to assume leadership positions at OpenAI, AirTrunk and CDC. Three of these appointments were made this year.
In September, the president of the Australian Professional Government Relations Association, Sally Mackenzie, said the scale of engagement between industry and ministers was a sign of the system working as intended.
“What we’re seeing with AI and datacentres is that dynamic playing out at real pace, because the stakes for industry, government and Australians more broadly are so high,” Mackenzie said.
“Lobbying is an important part of the democratic process. It ensures that all stakeholders have the ability to communicate with government.”
