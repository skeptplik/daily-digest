Pat Cummins labels Usman Khawaja ball-tampering claims 'utter nonsense'
Australia Test cricket captain Pat Cummins has emphatically denied any knowledge of sandpaper being used in the 2018 Test against South Africa in Cape Town.
Former Test batter Usman Khawaja claimed in his new book that 'everyone' involved in that series had knowledge of ball tampering.
Cummins, who bowled in that series, has unequivocally rejected that in a press conference and called the claims 'complete and utter nonsense'.
"When he says 'we all knew', if that he means we knew sandpaper was going out on the field, he's 100 per cent wrong," Cummins said.
"I said that consistently for the last eight years. I did not know that was happening."
"But I think Usman actually says things that are far more serious than that. He says that I and others were architects of a scheme to ball tamper with sandpaper; that's complete and utter nonsense."
Cummins also said he had not, and likely would not, read Khawaja's book in full.

"I've taken a bit of time this week to respond to Usman's allegations. I've been trying to understand exactly what those allegations have been, that's why it's taken me a few days.
"When he says 'we all knew', if by that he means we knew sandpaper was going out on the field, he's 100 per cent wrong. I've said that consistently for the last eight years; I did not know that was happening.
"But I think Usman actually says things that are far more serious than that. He says that I and others were architects of a scheme to ball tamper with sandpaper; that's complete and utter nonsense.
"I was genuinely shocked this week when I found out Usman admitted that he told Cam [Bancroft] to be careful when using sandpaper. "Usman's a friend, but friends aren't casual with each other's lives and reputations. In his book Ussie's been casual with my [life] and the lives of many others, and I think Australian cricket. Cummins was clearly struggling with how to talk about this whole thing without implicating himself or anyone, rejecting some of Khawaja's claims, side-skirting others, and all while not wanting to bad mouth someone he considers a friend.
In saying that, he did refer to any claims that the bowlers were architects of a ball-tampering scheme as "complete and utter nonsense".
Cummins spoke about Khawaja's language being "flippant" and "casual" with his language and that having real impact on people's lives; not just players and people involved, but their families.
"That's something I'm grappling with the last couple of days.
"I played 15 years — my debut for New South Wales and with Australia — Usman was right there and he's been awesome to me and he's a friend.
"But this is people's real lives and this is serious. I'm one of the lesser ones, but … it was traumatic for [Bancroft, Smith and Warner]. For all of us, for Australian cricket, for fans. So to bring it up so flippantly and casually, I think it's irresponsible.
"These are people's lives. It was traumatic for some of them. They have had to work through quite a bit to get out the other side. So again that's why I'm being really disappointed and frustrated this week is how it's been dealt with so flippantly."
Cummins said if Khawaja had been interviewed as part of the investigation at the time, not only could he have shed light on the situation, but he could have exorcised some of these demons earlier.
"It looks like it's been sitting heavy on him for a while. It's eight and a half years ago, he's sitting next to Bancroft, so it sounds like he knew a lot more than others in the changeroom.
"We all took part in that investigation at the time. We told them everything, so in some ways maybe that was for us part of the process to help get over it. Maybe this is Usman's chance and he never really got over it at the time."
Cummins was careful on Khawaja's claims of racism
I couldn't give this the gravity it deserves in the run of that press conference, but I want to make note of how many times Pat Cummins was asked about Usman Khawaja's claims of racism.
Cummins was careful not to completely dismiss Khawaja's lived experience and said he hadn't seen anything in his time as captain, but did admit there had been an "evolution" of some of the language used in years past.
"There might have been times when the person felt like they might have been prejudiced against or did not make them feel good. There's nothing very racist I've seen in the past and it's definitely nothing I've seen in the last eight years."
As far as I know there aren't specific claims relating to the Australian team while Khawaja was playing in it.
Khawaja has also said that when newly minted captain Cummins stopped the team from spraying champagne in order to ensure Khawaja could remain part of the celebrations of their 2021/22 Ashes series win, it was the first time he truly felt like part of the Australian cricket team.
Cummins hailed Khawaja as a trailblazer and a vital figure in Australian cricket history.
"I think he is an important player and has been an important figure in Australian cricket and Australia. I think it's good he feels comfortable that he can share his strong views and he opened the door for a lot people coming through who may be from similar backgrounds or a marginalised group to feel like they can speak openly."

'A huge extrapolation'
Cummins is obviously struggling when he talks about Khawaja.
He clearly views him as a friend and it's hard to imagine this won't affect that.
While Cummins isn't a fan of the way it's all written, he says he believes Khawaja's intentions are "sound" and this has obviously been weighing on him for a long time.
Cummins agrees that Bancroft, Smith and Warner were punished too heavily, but that doesn't mean the entire team was involved.
"My personal opinion was it was way too heavy-handed; way over the top.
"I think that's OK to say, but to then extrapolate that out into 'everyone else knew', that's a huge extrapolation and very different to what I think was injustice."
Cummins on legitimate use of sandpaper on cricket balls
A lot has been said about why sandpaper was in players' kit bags and it's been said that it was used on players' bats, but Cummins says they have also used it on balls in training to simulate match conditions like reverse swing.
"It's actually again quite a normal thing. You face a lot of reverse swing so the batters want to face it and practice in the nets.
"We've scratched balls on concrete, I've seen someone use a Stanley knife, I've seen sandpaper used — all the methods are very normal around training.
"We may have been doing it around training but there is a leap to then think it's OK to take it on the field, but maybe not perhaps as big a leap of some people may think."
