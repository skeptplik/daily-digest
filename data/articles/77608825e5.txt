Tourism, infrastructure challenges and "outdated, undersized and poorly maintained" wastewater systems are contributing to the ecological decline of a popular lake, an environmental group has warned.
Buttermere, in the Lake District, has seen rising nutrient levels in recent years which can lead to excess phosphorus and blankets of algae.
A new report has found farming practices alone "cannot account" for the changes.
West Cumbria Rivers Trust (WCRT), a charity which tracked down the sources of phosphorus for the study, argues "urgent regulatory action and maintenance guidance are needed".
The Buttermere Nutrient Investigations Project was commissioned and funded by the Environment Agency (EA) after the lake was last year found to be failing to meet total phosphorus targets.
WCRT said the investigation showed Buttermere - a designated Site of Special Scientific Interest and Special Area of Conservation - was a "fragile ecosystem under pressure".
'Lack of awareness'
Buttermere is a naturally nutrient-poor lake, the organisation said, which provides a welcoming environment for sensitive species, such as the Arctic charr cold-water fish.
But algae triggered by excess phosphorus can "choke out these rare habitats and degrade the lake's protected status", WCRT added.
Local wastewater infrastructure was identified as a "key area of concern", the organisation said, while agricultural investigations across major landholdings found no evidence of systemic diffuse pollution or excessive nutrient use, with soil management plans broadly appropriate.
As they are not connected to mains drainage, many properties in the area rely on septic tanks or package treatment plants.
However, WCRT said a number of the 11 septic tank systems identified in the catchment were "undersized, poorly maintained or past their effective lifespan, with several suspected to be discharging directly to surface water, which is not compliant with the regulations".
Although most owners were keen to help the environment, the trust said there was a "widespread lack of awareness" about how the private systems worked and what level of maintenance was needed.
Surveys also revealed just over 40% of visitors had dogs with them and many were walked along sensitive lakeshore paths.
Nutrient fertilisation by the animals "could be significant", it was found.
'Hidden' pollution source
WCRT CEO Jodie Mills, said: "Our investigations show that water quality in Buttermere is suffering primarily due to private wastewater systems that are undersized, poorly maintained or overwhelmed during peak seasonal surges.
"Many home and business owners simply didn't realise their tanks weren't performing properly.
"This creates a hidden source of pollution, and urgent regulatory action and maintenance guidance are needed to protect this special lake."
In addition to calling for regulation and enforcement, the trust said it would be providing wastewater guidance for homeowners on its website and engaging with the local parish council and the Lake District Visitor Management Group, which includes the National Trust and the Lake District National Park Authority.
Responsibility for the regulation of discharges belongs to the EA.
Peter Miles, the organisation's nature, monitoring and technical manager, said the report provided "valuable information about the sources of excess nutrients" in Buttermere.
He added: "Sources of pollution are often very complex and we urge everyone living or working in Buttermere, and those visiting, to play their part in reducing the amount of pollution entering the lake, from checking their private sewage systems to clearing up after their dogs."
