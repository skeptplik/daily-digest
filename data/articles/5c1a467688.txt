On Tuesday, voters resoundingly rebuked Donald Trump and his brutal mass deportation campaign that sent military tanks and masked agents though residential streets.
But on Friday, the president doubled down. He named Stephen Miller – the architect of the administration’s most aggressive immigration policies – as his chief of staff.
It was never likely that Trump – who rose to political prominence on the promise that he would expel immigrants en masse from the US – would dampen his agenda, even after voters rejected Republican candidates up and down the ballot, near and far from the southern border, in blue and deep-red states.
But the elevation of Miller – a zealous, anti-immigrant crusader who has been by Trump’s side since the president’s first term – is an ominous signal that the deportation machine that Trump has built up over the past two years will be driving ahead at full speed.
Miller more than any single other person is responsible for unleashing a series of violent immigration raids across US cities this year and last, for sending hordes of masked agents in military gear through residential streets to round up undocumented immigrants. He has pushed the administration to all but shut down legal immigration as well. In his previous roles – as a deputy chief of staff and policy adviser – he has stuck close to Trump’s side and stoked the president’s most extreme instincts.
“The elevation of Stephen Miller to White House chief of staff signals a doubling down on the administration’s cruelest impulses,” said Faisal Al-Juburi, the co-chief executive of Raíces, a Texas-based humanitarian aid and immigration services non-profit. “Miller’s promotion guarantees an acceleration of mass deportations and of brute force unleashed within our neighborhoods.”

The US Department of Justice moved on Friday to dismiss the lawsuit it brought against the state of California, in which it sought to keep roughly 20,000 ballots it alleged went to noncitizens from being counted.
The supreme court ruled against the Trump administration on Monday, after several lower courts sided with California officials, who argued the federal government was seeking to interfere in state-run elections.
The request to dismiss the case comes three days after the final votes were cast in the midterm elections and about a week before a scheduled hearing where a special master would have been asked to evaluate the accuracy of the list provided by the federal government.
California officials argued that the list is out of date and includes legitimate voters — a sentiment that the federal judge presiding over the case echoed in his blistering order denying the federal government’s request.

Reading aloud from prepared remarks to NASA officials at Cape Canaveral in Florida on Friday, Donald Trump ran through a list of what he called evidence that Americans were “the greatest pioneers explorers, inventors and innovators who ever lived.”
“America is the nation that dreamed and created the modern world,” Trump said, peering at a teleprompter from beneath a USA cap. “We laid the railroads, harnessed electricity and invented the lightbulb, the telephone, the airplane, the assembly line, the television, the microchip, the personal computer, the internet, GPS, the smartphone, and almost every other thing- think of it – almost every other thing that’s worth mentioning.”
He then looked out at the audience and veered off-script to comment: “That’s a cool list, though, when you think, huh? How about a little piece of each of them? Wouldn’t that be nice? You wouldn’t be sitting here right now. You’d be someplace else.”
The president then returned to his prepared remarks, looking back at the teleprompter and saying: “We charted the human genome to cure diseases,” but stumbled badly over the word “genome” which he pronounced “guh-nome”.
Despite Trump’s attempt to claim that the US deserves credit for sequencing all the DNA in human chromosomes, the Human Genome Project was, in fact, the work of an international network of geneticists.
The president made the flub minutes after boasting about the fact that his uncle, John Trump, had been a long-tenured professor at MIT. Trump has repeatedly claimed that his uncle’s intelligence and scientific knowledge was passed on to him through his genes, giving him an innate understanding of complex scientific questions, from the novel coronavirus that caused a global pandemic in 2020 to nuclear weapons.
Trump expresses satisfaction at stock market plunge after Republican losses in midterms, deflects blame from himself
Donald Trump, remarkably, gave yet another speech on Friday, delivering remarks at the John F. Kennedy Space Center in Cape Canaveral, Florida in his official capacity as president, which were, nonetheless, threaded through with partisan political talking points.
The president emerged to the theme tune from his rallies, God Bless the USA, and mimed dancing, before starting his speech by celebrating the victories of two Florida Republicans, Byron Donalds, the governor-elect, and senator Ashley Moody.
The pair were the only two candidates Trump rallied for in the past month who won competitive races.
He then praised members of his administration who were present, singling out the US ambassador to the United Nations, Mike Waltz, for “the way you took on, the Cubans”. Waltz was recently engaged in a spat with a Cuban official at the UN which played on heavy rotation on Fox News, the president’s window on the world.
Trump then suggested that he was glad that the US stock market plunged after the Republican wipeout at the ballot box, on fears that companies building AI data centers might not be as free to push ahead with regulation by a Democratic Congress.
“Yesterday, you know, our country lost $3 trillion, all because of an election,” Trump said. “If we were to gain $3 trillion, I probably would have been more unhappy. But you know what that means; that means that they thought somebody else did well in an election.”
“By the way, I was not on the ballot, just in case you had any question,” he added. “Because if we were on the ballot, we would’ve won but we weren’t.”
Senator Chris Murphy discharged from hospital after stroke as doctor says he expects full recovery
Senator Chris Murphy was released from a hospital on Friday following treatment for a stroke and plans to spend several weeks at a rehabilitation center as he works to recover, the Connecticut Democrat said in a statement.
“Thank you for the outpouring of support my family and I have received over the last few days,” Murphy said. “I want to be direct with you, so here is my diagnosis. Last Saturday afternoon, I felt something was not right with my body and called 9-1-1 out of an abundance of caution, and I’m glad I did. I was diagnosed with a small bleed in the thalamus region of my brain.”
“The doctors are very encouraged by the progress I’ve made so far. In order to take my recovery seriously and get back to work on the quickest timeline possible, I will be spending several weeks at a rehabilitation center after being discharged from the hospital today,” he continued.
The senator also released a statement from Dr Jose Suarez, the chief of neurosciences critical care at the Johns Hopkins Hospital. “Senator Murphy experienced a small hemorrhagic stroke. He has shown tremendous progress, and at the current rate of improvement, we expect that he will make a full recovery following rehabilitation,” the doctor said.

Several newly elected Democrats and Senator Bernie Sanders have renewed calls for datacenter moratoriums in the days after the US midterm elections. The sweeping Democratic victory, which was buoyed in part by backlash to the tech industry, has added momentum to efforts to slow down the multi-trillion-dollar buildout of the controversial facilities.
On the social media platform X, Sanders posted a call for an immediate national datacenter moratorium this week. “Americans don’t want Big Tech oligarchs wrecking the environment, gutting jobs and threatening humanity’s future,” Sanders said on Wednesday.
Sanders was joined by several Democrats, including Representative Yassamin Ansari of Arizona – a state where Elon Musk’s SpaceX has proposed a datacenter project that would span more than 700 acres.
“Top of the Democratic agenda on day 1 needs to be a pause on advanced AI development, regulation of this out of control industry, and a ban on artificial superintelligence,” Ansari posted Wednesday on X. Musk mocked her post in a retweet, and the two sparred, with Ansari forecasting congressional hearings, telling the centi-billionaire: “See you soon.”
The pushback to the buildout of datacenters, which tech companies need to power the AI products that have become central to their businesses, was one of the most visible issues of the midterms. Opposition to datacenters figured heavily in campaigns across the US, while voters passed dozens of local ballot measures aimed at restricting datacenter projects in their states.
