Brussels is incorporating military fuel needs into the planning and assessment of national emergency oil stocks, according to a document seen by Euronews.
The European Commission wants EU governments to look beyond emergency oil stockpiles to secure enough diesel, jet fuel and other oil products to keep essential services and military operations running during a prolonged crisis, according to a draft document seen by Euronews.
More than half of EU countries (61.54%) reported trouble ensuring sufficient fuel access when armed forces move across the continent in a 2025 Commission impact assessment, posing a major hurdle for the bloc's plans for military readiness by 2030.
With Russia's war against Ukraine at its border and a series of hybrid attacks sweeping the continent over the past weeks, Defence Commissioner Andrius Kubilius recently warned that the EU needs to prepare for a substantial increase in military fuel consumption during a conflict.
He noted that in the event of a war outbreak, NATO could need to move and sustain 100,000 troops during the first ten days of a crisis, stressing the importance of diesel and jet fuel for military operations.
The EU's new approach to energy security deems it crucial to place oil supplies within the EU's wider defence and strategy preparedness agenda to "prevent or minimise risks" on a range of measures.
According to the draft document, which lays out an overhaul of the Commission's oil security rules, national governments would need to assess how much emergency fuel they hold and whether their refineries, ports, pipelines and distribution networks could withstand major disruptions.
"Inadequacy between the evolution of consumption, including military demand, and the evolution of the fuel supply chain, including along military mobility corridors," reads the paper, referring to assessments expected by EU governments.
Under the draft rules, EU countries would have to factor military demand into their national risk assessments, including the possibility of sudden increases in fuel requirements and simultaneous pressure from civilian and military consumers.
A recent NATO energy security study found that the EU's ability to fuel its armed forces could be undermined by a shrinking refinery industry, growing dependence on foreign suppliers and insufficient jet fuel reserves.
"Jet fuel is precisely the product on which NATO Europe is structurally short. It is also the fuel most likely to retain (or even grow) its demand to 2050, and the product with the least amount of stock cover at 49 days," reads the study.
NATO's study noted that Europe imports between 27% and 30% of its jet fuel from outside the region, leaving it exposed to foreign suppliers.
Dependence is also highly concentrated — 36% of external imports come from one supplier, while three countries account for 65% of all external supplies.
Assessing refinery's capacity
While EU governments are pursuing industrial decarbonisation, strategically important refining capacity is disappearing, raising the question of whether the bloc's refinery industry is becoming a national security liability.
Despite the efforts to accelerate the energy transition, the new energy security framework puts considerable attention on refineries — an industry sector in decline across Europe over the past nearly two decades.
"We need to counter this trend and ensure that we have adequate and flexible refining capacity in Europe. Today and for the future. This is not just a matter of economic competitiveness. It is also a matter of security," the Energy Commissioner Dan Jørgensen said after a dialogue on refineries alongside Defense Commissioner Kubilius.
Even if EU countries have access to crude oil alone, they need sufficient capacity to turn it into the diesel, gasoline and jet fuel required by transport, industry and defence.
According to the draft document, national authorities would have to identify their refineries, assess their production capacity and report major closures, expansions and planned investments.
Brussels also expects capitals to examine the consequences of losing critical infrastructure and becoming overly dependent on a limited number of foreign suppliers.
The document suggests the Commission is asking governments to consider whether allowing domestic refining capacity to disappear could create a strategic vulnerability that can't easily be repaired if a crisis hits.
The draft would also require EU capitals to map their dependence on individual suppliers, including the countries from which they import crude oil, diesel, gasoline and jet fuel.
The goal is to assess how these dependencies could evolve over the next five years and what would happen if a major supplier suddenly stopped deliveries.
EU countries could face greater scrutiny over their emergency preparedness.
The draft document suggests that capitals would need to prepare national risk assessments and preparedness and response plans covering physical attacks, cyber threats, extreme weather, ownership of critical infrastructure and prolonged disruptions affecting several supply routes.
Collision course with clean transition
Environmental campaigners have argued that reviving refineries across the EU could slow down the bloc's goal to cut CO2 emissions at a time when a series of climate policies are being diluted.
The softening of the EU's carbon market and the CO2 emissions car rules, alongside the delay in implementing methane rules, raises questions over whether Brussels will reach its 2040 climate target to ultimately become carbon neutral by 2050.
Despite seeking to boost electrification and reduce oil consumption, Brussels recognises that armed forces will continue to need reliable supplies of diesel and jet fuel, including during sudden increases in military demand.
The Commission is expected to unveil its proposal to revise oil security supply rules on 2 December.
