Climate science is “foundational” to the Paris agreement and worldwide progress on tackling global heating, and attempts to delay new scientific reports threaten global cooperation on tackling the crisis, one of the world’s leading climate scientists has warned.
Johan Rockström, the director of the Potsdam Institute for Climate Impact Research, said the world’s leading body of climate scientists, the Intergovernmental Panel on Climate Change, must be allowed to work as quickly as possible on its next comprehensive assessment of global knowledge of the climate systems.
Some countries are holding up work on the three-part report, in a row over the body’s role and representation of developing countries. One group has argued that racist assumptions underpin some of the body’s recommendations.
But Rockström said the reports were fair. “The IPCC is objective, evidence-based science, and it’s the only thing that we can reliably stand on as a basis for our policymaking,” he said. “The IPCC is the summary of everything done voluntarily by scientists from the entire world, global south and global north, that collectively carries out a synthesis [of established research].”
The IPCC’s work is used to inform the annual climate “conference of the parties” to the UN Framework Convention on Climate Change. Its next assessment, called AR7, the seventh in a series dating back to 1988, is under way, and it was hoped could be completed in 2028. In the autumn of that year, nations will gather for Cop33, at which the second “global stocktake” under the Paris agreement will be thrashed out.
This is the mechanism by which progress towards the core Paris goal, of limiting temperature rises to 1.5C above pre-industrial levels, will be judged. The stocktake is almost certain to find that current pledges to cut greenhouse gas emissions are inadequate and must be improved.
The UN has already found the world to be on track for a 2.8C temperature rise, a level previous IPCC reports have found would result in severe and in some cases irreversible consequences, such as the melting of ice caps and glaciers, sea level rises, coral bleaching and extreme weather.
But some countries – chiefly Russia, the Arab group of countries including Saudi Arabia, and India – want to delay publication of AR7 to 2029, after the global stocktake is completed. They argue, with the backing of some climate campaigners, that developing countries are disadvantaged in trying to submit science to the IPCC. As a result, they say, the work of body is permeated by racist assumptions that guarantee the privilege of developed countries.
Lidy Nacpil, the coordinator of the Asian Peoples’ Movement on Debt and Development, told the Guardian the IPCC’s scenarios for cutting greenhouse gas emissions – known as “mitigation” in UN jargon – were “biased against the interests of the global south”.
For instance, she said one assumption was that cutting emissions should be done where it was most cost-efficient. “And where is it cheap to do mitigation? In the global south.” Changing the assumptions would produce different scenarios, Nacpil said.
Nacpil said singling out Saudi Arabia, “whose behaviour we’re not always sympathetic with”, was “also very racist”. She asked: “Who started the fossil fuel industries in this region? Who enjoyed it for now dozens of decades? Whose multinational companies really grew from the extraction of oil and gas in the Middle East? This is not us taking sides. This is us saying: ‘But the pot is calling the kettle black.’ And they’re using their economic, political, and racial power to do this.”
Rockström rejected this. “The pathways to the future represent different socioeconomic realities around the world,” he told the Guardian, in an interview at Cop31. “This is based on the best evidence we have, and this evidence has no north or south bias. It represents the best peer-reviewed science of our understanding of how to close income gaps in poor countries versus rich countries, demographic changes, health projections, and of course also therefore forecasting socioeconomic development. It’s an objective, evidence-based analysis of the reality.”
He added: “We have to defend that objective science. We should not use science as the arena to fight for justice or human rights or even democracy. That’s not the role of science.”
Many other developing countries, including small islands and the least developed countries, have also called for the IPCC report to be completed without delay.
Raju Pandit Chhetri, a negotiator from Nepal, said calling the IPCC racist was “a political line”. “What we need to do in this process is really respect the science,” he said. “Coming from Nepal, I can tell you the impacts in just a few hours time: losing thousands of lives, let along billions of pounds worth of property. There are countries who think that making stronger science will put pressure on them to [cut emissions].”
The core committees of the IPCC are run by pairs of scientists, one each from developed and developing countries. China, India and Saudi all supply leading scientists to the organisation, but insiders acknowledge that parts of the process are dominated by European researchers.
The IPCC said that out of 664 appointed authors working on the drafts of its three working group reports, 51% came from developing countries and economies in transition – an increase of close to half, compared with its last report in 2021. Each chapter across the three Working Group reports includes at least one developing-country chapter scientist.
A spokesperson for the IPCC said: “IPCC is absolutely committed to promoting diversity and inclusion and has taken multiple steps to do so during its seventh cycle. As the UN body assessing the science related to climate change, we uphold and stand by the core values of integrity, professionalism and respect for diversity and gender. In the current cycle, the IPCC introduced institutional measures that implement equity and inclusivity.”
Rockström told the Guardian that even if AR7 was delayed, the science in AR6 would be enough to show that current pledges, made last year ahead of the Cop30 conference in Brazil, must be vastly improved. “We have all the evidence we need. Even if you based your updates [on emissions pledges] only on AR6, we would still be making enormous progress,” he said.
But others fear a delay to the IPCC could be used by countries to derail the global stocktake, for instance by arguing it too must be delayed or modified. “It’s throwing more sand in the gears,” one expert involved in the process said.
The IPCC row has been going on all year, but is now coming to a head because in early December the body meets to decide its budget. Although the body has reserves of cash to draw on, if a budget cannot be agreed there will be little chance of the IPCC being able to produce its reports ahead of the global stocktake.
Technically, the IPCC issues cannot be solved at Cop because they can only be agreed within the body itself, but as governments are meeting in Turkey, some form of compromise among the warring groups might be possible. The chair of the IPCC, the British scientist Jim Skea, extended his attendance at Cop31 for further talks on the issue.
The first draft of the “cover decision” from Cop31, released on Monday, contains references to the need for IPCC science. Though it is likely to be revised several times before Cop31 ends, the draft currently “recognises the central role of the best available science and of the IPCC” and “recognises the value of a full range of knowledge and participation in the IPCC, including contributions from Indigenous peoples, people of African descent, local communities, non-English speaking regions, and developing countries, and allow sufficient time to prepare comprehensive and credible scientific assessments”.
