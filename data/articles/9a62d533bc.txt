EU Energy chief tells Euronews Brussels will press ahead with its Russian gas ban regardless of the outcome of US-Russia peace talks.
The EU will never return to Russian energy, regardless of the outcome of talks between Washington and Moscow, EU Energy Commissioner Dan Jørgensen has told Euronews.
Speaking to On the Record with Maria Tadeo, Jørgensen described Russia as an enemy of Europe and vowed to press ahead with plans to end Russian energy imports.
"We will never again, if it's up to me, import as much as one molecule from Russia. We do not trust Russia," he said.
His comments underline a growing divergence between Brussels and Washington.
Donald Trump's administration eased restrictions on Russian diesel last month ahead of the November midterms and has indicated it could support Moscow's return as a global energy player if a peace deal is reached in Ukraine.
Washington has even floated the prospect of Russian energy returning to Europe, according to media reports earlier this month.
But Jørgensen insisted that no deal between Washington and Moscow could override EU legislation.
“No matter what happens in these negotiations, European legislation is made in Brussels, proposed by the Commission, made by the Parliament and the Council co-legislators,” he said. "The EU wants to make sure it never has to rely on Russian gas again."
Rather than relying on sanctions, which could be lifted once the war ends, the EU has adopted legislation to permanently phase out Russian gas imports.
"We've actually banned [it], which is stronger than sanctions because sanctions have to be renewed every six months," he said, suggesting that " if there's a peace, then probably the sanctions will fall away" but the legislation will remain in place.
The bloc has already cut its dependence on Russian gas from 45% to around 10% and expects to end imports altogether next year.
“Even if there's peace, we will not trust them, and we do not want to repeat the mistakes of the past,” Jørgensen said.
Difficult winter ahead
Jørgensen also warned consumers to brace for higher fuel and gas prices this winter, despite the recent fall in oil prices.
"We are looking into a winter that will be quite difficult at the pump because gasoline, diesel and gas will be more expensive," he said.
Average gas prices in Europe rose by 163% year on year in October, according to figures cited in the report, with colder weather potentially driving bills even higher.
As governments come under mounting pressure to shield households from rising prices, Jørgensen pushed back against calls for an EU-wide windfall tax on energy companies, arguing that national governments should decide.
"In some member states it probably will be a good idea. I do not think it is a good idea to have an overall European one size does not fit all," he said.
Six EU countries – Austria, Germany, Italy, Poland, Portugal and Spain – have called on Brussels to consider a bloc-wide levy on energy companies' excess profits.
The Commission instead wants governments to cut electricity taxes, arguing that high levies discourage consumers from switching away from fossil fuels. Jørgensen said taxes account for around 29% of the average household electricity bill.
Asked about the prospect of a prolonged closure of the Strait of Hormuz, Jørgensen said Europe should prepare for the worst rather than bank on political promises of an imminent end to conflicts in Ukraine and the Middle East.
"We do prepare for a world where the uncertainties are so big. We don't know what will happen," Jørgensen said, adding that the EU should prepare for the worst rather than rely on political promises that conflicts in Ukraine and the Middle East will soon end.
