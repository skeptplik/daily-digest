David Tennant has long been recognized as one of the greatest actors alive. He’s played scoundrels, cops, serial killers, and even a demon — an actual one, not a member of the Conservative Party, though he’s played one of those too.
If there’s one thing you can’t say about Tennant, it’s that he lacks range. He can play a lover, a fighter, or the scummiest person you know. But his most iconic role as the time-traveling alien known as the Doctor in the sci-fi family show Doctor Who brings all three together.
That might be a shocking statement to process. One of the Doctor’s core moral philosophies, articulated as early as Classic Who in 1963, is that the Time Lord is ultimately a peaceful person. During the Twelfth Doctor’s (Peter Capaldi) regeneration in 2017, one of his very last statements was that the future Doctor should “never be cruel, never be cowardly, and never ever eat pears! Remember, hate is always foolish, but love is always wise. Always try to be nice, but never fail to be kind.”
And yet, Tennant’s Tenth Doctor was the opposite of all those things during the 2009 autumnal Doctor Who special, "The Waters of Mars."
The Time Lord Victorious
There have always been moments when the Doctor has shown that, for every person he has saved over the centuries, there have been just as many whom he has condemned.
However, the most memorable episode in which Whovians see the Doctor at his lowest point morally comes when the Time Lord lands on Mars in 2059 and encounters humanity’s first colony there: Bowie Base One. Led by Captain Adelaide Brooke (Lindsay Duncan), the colonists seem to be doing quite well for themselves, and there is hope that more of humanity will be able to join them in the future. Unfortunately, once the Doctor realizes who they are, another realization follows: it’s a fixed point in history in which everyone on Bowie Base One will die in an unknown cataclysm. Worse, it’s set to happen on the day the Doctor arrives.
The reason is revealed with the arrival of the Flood — an incredibly intelligent, waterborne alien virus. A single drop of the virus infects humans, turning them into zombie-like creatures with one sole aim: to keep spreading. One by one, the personnel of Bowie Base One perish, though one member cleverly destroys all the ships so the Flood cannot spread to Earth.
Throughout it all, the Doctor is racked with indecision. Messing with fixed points in history is usually seen as a big no-no, but he had done so before with his previous companion Donna Noble (Catherine Tate) when they saved a single family during “The Fires of Pompeii.” However, if he saves Adelaide, her granddaughter Susie won’t be inspired to become the first human captain to pilot a lightspeed ship to Proxima Centauri, a major historical event.
It’s a conundrum that arises while the Doctor is traveling alone, still reeling from the loss of his companion, Donna. In several previous episodes, the alien race known as the Ood has also warned him that his time is running out — regeneration is on the way.
This knowledge leaves the Doctor furious. No matter how many he saves, it seems all he does lately is lose. Now that he’s on his own, the fact that time and circumstance take so much away has driven him to the brink. He’s tired of losing; he wants to win.
After revealing to Adelaide her impending doom and her granddaughter’s achievement, the Doctor, in a moment of profound, unbridled hubris, delivers a speech that must be read in full to appreciate just how villainous it sounds:
                        “There are laws of time. Once upon a time there were people in charge of those laws, but they died. They all died. Do you know who that leaves? Me! It's taken me all these years to realize that the laws of time are mine and they will obey me!”
It’s a quote that feels exactly like it would come from the Doctor’s worst enemy, fellow Time Lord, The Master. Unlike the Doctor, the Master has always wanted to dominate others, making the Doctor’s subsequent rescue of Adelaide in the TARDIS feel even worse.
Unfortunately for the Doctor, his victory in giving time itself a big ‘F U’ is short-lived. Back on Earth, Adelaide is shaken by the Doctor’s self-perception as a god over time and berates the Time Lord for thinking he gets to live and choose who lives and who dies. She walks away and kills herself, righting history once more.
The Doctor's overwhelming pride has always been part of him. As much as he loves exploring the universe and often falls in love with humanity, he undoubtedly thinks himself better than most. There have been too many episodes to count in which a character has called the Doctor out for thinking others are stupid.
The Time Lord’s arrogance often leaves it to his companions to reel him back from the brink of destruction by reminding him of his humanity. In “The Waters of Mars,” it is easy to see why: If they don’t, the Doctor can easily become the very monster he so often fights.
