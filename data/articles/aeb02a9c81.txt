First U.S. bird flu death of the year after cattle exposure
A person in Washington state has died from complications of the bird flu, the AP reported yesterday. It’s the first such death this year in the U.S., and the third overall since bird flu became a major public health concern in 2024. (The first two deaths were from the H5N1 strain of the virus. The Washington case stemmed from a rarer version, H5N5.)
It’s another day I wish Helen Branswell was still here. She reported extensively on bird flu over her career at STAT, and her most recent reporting offers a reminder: Cases over the past few years have largely been milder than expected, leaving scientists relieved and puzzled. (Also: Drinking raw milk is always a bad idea, Helen wrote, but especially when bird flu is circulating.)
FDA approves generic HIV prevention shot
The FDA has taken a key step to improve access to a groundbreaking HIV prevention medication for dozens of low- and middle-income countries. The injectable drug, lenacapavir, offers virtually complete protection against the virus with one administration every six months. More than a year after approving the brand name version, sold by Gilead Sciences, the agency gave tentative approval to Dr. Reddy’s Laboratories to make a generic version.
The move comes two years after Gilead reached licensing deals with six generic drugmakers, including Dr. Reddy’s, to produce and distribute a lower-cost version to 120 mostly low- and middle-income countries. Read more from STAT’s Ed Silverman on who the generic can help and how much it will ultimately cost.
Scientists are re-thinking ‘three-parent IVF’
I hope you took some time yesterday to read Megan Molteni’s incredible reporting on IVF clinics around the world pitching older women on a reproductive technique known as “three-parent IVF” as a way for them to have biological children, despite scant evidence that it works. Go read that story if you haven’t yet. Today, Megan and STAT contributor Edward Chen bring us a follow-up on the thorny ethical questions about developing the procedure.
The technique, formally known as mitochondrial replacement technology, was developed to reduce the risk of children inheriting sometimes fatal mitochondrial diseases from their mothers. Pushback against it has mainly focused on its expanding use as an infertility treatment, as Megan wrote. But now, some scientists are rethinking the entire approach due to questions around ethics, safety, and efficacy.
“The general public may have the idea that it is very safe and low risk and that’s just not the case,” said Bert Smeets, a clinical genomics professor who serves on the committee that reviews applications from patients to receive MRT at the sole U.K. clinic that has a license to perform it. 10%
That’s the proportion of minors who went to the ER after an electric bike accident that ultimately had brain injuries, according to an analysis of emergency room data published yesterday in JAMA. That’s double the rate of brain injuries caused by non-motorized bikes.
Researchers assessed data from six medical institutions between 2023 and early 2026, finding that injury rates for e-bikes and e-scooters increased much faster than harms from traditional, non-motorized bikes and scooters over those years. And while kids who get hurt on these motorized vehicles are generally older, 24% of e-bike injuries and 44% of e-scooter trauma occurred in kids younger than 12.
How one doctor got better at his job, overnight
Last fall, ophthalmologist Robert Kinast attended a meditation retreat in the Santa Cruz Mountains, hoping for a respite from his busy medical practice. But when he and the other attendees were asked to stay awake and meditate through dawn, Kinast found himself revisiting the most devastating injuries he’d witnessed as a doctor: Eyes ruptured by knives, fireworks, bungee cords, windshields, beer bottles, sticks, rocks, fish hooks, and more.
“That night taught me how the inner work of meditation and self-inquiry can support the outer work of patient care,” Kinast writes in a new First Opinion essay. Read more on how he’s changed his behavior in clinic to stay grounded and better serve patients.
