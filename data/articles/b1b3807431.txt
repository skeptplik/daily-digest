Trump's team scrambles to calm U.S. fears over tech outages
'The American people should ask us these questions,' intelligence boss tells Capitol Hill hearing
After U.S. President Donald Trump brushed aside the past week's series of tech outages, members of his administration are ramping up their efforts to try to reassure the American public that there is no reason to panic.
Officials are rejecting speculation that there's any link between the IT incident that triggered the grounding of 20,000 flights across the U.S., the two-day shutdown of Microsoft services, trouble with online transactions at U.S. banks or the National Security Agency's classified communications network going offline — all within the space of six days.
Jay Clayton, the director of national intelligence, led the Trump administration's push to calm fears on Wednesday, telling a House committee on homeland security that the outages were neither connected nor the result of cyberattacks.
"I understand the timing of these incidents can raise questions, and it should. The American people should ask us these questions," Clayton told lawmakers in the hearing on worldwide threats, which had been scheduled well before the outages began.
"Based on what we know today, after a great deal of diligence, these are separate service outages associated with maintenance and technical changes. They are not a co-ordinated or state-sponsored attack."
In response to the outages that have disrupted work and travel for hundreds of thousands if not millions of Americans, prompting widespread anxiety about what could be going on, Trump has been mostly silent. When he has spoken out, he has downplayed them.
Asked by a reporter Tuesday if he was concerned about the outages, Trump said, "No, it's life. When you're big and powerful and you have all the money, things like that happen. It's fine."
But Trump's insistence that everything is fine — delivered from Manila where he was attending the Asia-Pacific Economic Co-operation summit — appeared to do little to ease nerves in the U.S.
'People are feeling insecure'
Katherine Clark, the No. 2 Democrat in the House as the party's whip, chastised Trump for his response.
"It is a shocking moment when the American people are feeling insecure, when they are wondering about how they can be sure that their families are going to be safe ... and instead they're getting comments like that from the commander-in-chief," Clark told reporters on Capitol Hill on Wednesday.
Even Trump's former press secretary wasn't thrilled with the message. Now in her role as a pundit on Fox News, Karoline Leavitt urged the White House to be transparent with the American people and try to "calm the temperature."
Operating without a press secretary for the past three months — aside from the short-lived tenure of Katie Zacharias — the White House is relying on members of Trump's cabinet for getting out its message on the technical outages. The results have been mixed.
Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin, who testified at Wednesday's hearing alongside Clayton, faced questions from lawmakers — some Republican — skeptical of the official explanations for the outages.
"Have you ever in all of your years in D.C. seen this many technology outages during this short of a period of time?" asked Republican Congressman Eli Crane of Arizona.
"Absolutely not," Mullin replied, and floated the need for better communication from companies doing upgrades that could trigger such outages.

Crane wasn't satisfied.
"How can you guys, with confidence, sit here and tell us that, 'Hey, these aren't cyberattacks. These are actually just a bunch of patches and old networks'?" Crane asked next.
"We have gone out to each one of those individuals, we've used our tools and we have no evidence that this is a co-ordinated cyberattack," Clayton replied.
Apparently signalling the administration's concern about its response to the outages, Vice-President JD Vance abruptly cancelled his scheduled appearance Wednesday at the New York Times DealBook event because he was needed at the White House for meetings.
There are reports that some U.S. consumers are responding to the outages by stocking up on essentials.
An analyst note from investment bank Mizuho Securities raised its forecast for Costco stock because of a jump in purchases of emergency preparedness products, Bloomberg reported.
Costco says the trend started in late October, before the outages.
The company noticed an increase in purchases of such items as "rice, canned goods, water, paper products, generators, batteries and similar items seemingly in response to a variety of concerns on social media," Costco chief financial officer Gary Millerchip said in an email to Bloomberg.
Jordan Rae Kelly, who served as the National Security Council's director for cyber incident response during Trump's first term, says to some extent it doesn't matter whether the outages are the result of technology failures rather than cyberattacks.
"That really misses the point because the consequences have still been incredibly severe," Kelly told CNN on Wednesday.
She said it's crucial that U.S. officials start talking about what guardrails are needed to try to prevent such significant outages in the future.
"The fact that these failures are getting such widespread attention is going to make those conversations come to the forefront and that's really important," Kelly said.
Alex Hamerstone, a director at U.S. cybersecurity consulting firm TrustedSec, believes these sorts of outages will not only continue to occur, they'll likely accelerate with the growing frequency of tech updates.
"Unfortunately, you see those types of things happen with upgrades," Hamerstone told CBC News. "When you're rushing to update things or patch things, that's where a lot of issues come from."
