What did Xi Jinping get from the bear hug with Trump at the Shenzhen summit?
NPR's Rob Schmitz speaks with Robert Daly, director of the program on US China relations at University of Maryland's School of Public Policy, about President Trump's trip to China this week.
ROB SCHMITZ, HOST:
When the president goes on a big diplomatic trip, it's not always clear what's happening in all those meetings they go to. That was true during President Trump's trip to China this week, but there were intriguing public moments.
(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)
UNIDENTIFIED CHILDREN: (Chanting in non-English language).
SCHMITZ: A group of children chanted, warm welcome, over and over, as Xi Jinping met Trump on the tarmac. Now add a third leader to the mix.
(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)
PRESIDENT DONALD TRUMP: Who is he? He's my friend.
SCHMITZ: That was Vladimir Putin, who's also a friend of Xi Jinping's, though the two may preside over an uneasy alliance. The stakes were high. Trump is looking for a breakthrough on nuclear weapons control. The U.S. and China are in a faceoff over AI, and a deal to end the bloodshed in Ukraine has been elusive.
Now that the summit's over, we're joined by someone who can help make sense of what happened. Robert Daly was a U.S. foreign service officer in China and now directs the program on U.S.-China relations at the University of Maryland School of Public Policy. And he's here with me in the studio. Welcome.
ROBERT DALY: Good to be here. Thank you.
SCHMITZ: So what did Xi Jinping want out of this meeting?
DALY: Xi Jinping - as always, his first goal is performative. It is to have the Chinese people see him as a world leader, beneficent, competent, hosting others who are coming somewhat cap in hand, at least in a very friendly way, to China. There's a very ancient saying in Chinese, (speaking Chinese), which means the 10,000 kingdoms all come to the center here. And Xi has been hosting an awful lot of leaders recently.
SCHMITZ: How'd he do?
DALY: He did very well from the Chinese point of view. I think that the hug of Donald Trump - we tend to see that from an American point of view as being friendly. Now, whether that's performative, friendship or truly felt is only for them to say. But for the Chinese, that's an unusually warm and humanizing move for Xi. Even the Chinese people will complain that their leaders are often too stayed, too in control. And this warmth, I think, also makes him look good, as does all the formal ceremony.
SCHMITZ: We also had Vladimir Putin there, the president of Russia. What was his goal at this meeting?
DALY: Well, this appears to have been his idea. His goal as the head of what is now, at least in many spheres - not in China, but in the West - a rogue nation that has invaded Ukraine - he gets a place of honor with what are unquestionably the world's only two superpowers, but as if perhaps he's in their league, which, other than his possession of nuclear weapons, he really isn't.
SCHMITZ: Was he treated like he was in their league during this meeting?
DALY: Well, this has been controversial within Russia itself. Some Russians on the internet have felt that he was dissed. He didn't get a meeting at the airport the way that Trump did. He didn't...
SCHMITZ: Right.
DALY: ...Get bear hugged this time in public, although he has met with Xi Jinping about 50 times now. So he's been warmly greeted overall, but he didn't get quite the same status.
SCHMITZ: And it's important to point out here that, especially in Russia and in China, people often look for these symbolic gestures because there's not much to go on.
China and Russia have called their partnership unbreakable in the past. Was that alliance meaningful at this summit?
DALY: Yes, and it has been meaningful really ever since the invasion of Ukraine. Tony Binken has said that China is the essential enabler for that invasion, both because it buys a lot of oil and gas, but also because it sells many, many dual-use parts to Russia, which enables it to improve its drones, but it also keeps it afloat economically.
SCHMITZ: I mentioned the small but significant detail that Xi met Trump on the runway. Did you spot any other moments at this summit that also caught your attention?
DALY: After the meeting on the runway, what I was listening for was whether Xi Jinping would describe Trump as a great friend or a great and wise leader - all the things that Trump says about Xi, which are never reciprocated. Xi sort of pockets the compliment. He smiles...
SCHMITZ: (Laughter).
DALY: ...In an oracular way, and on we go. And so I was wondering, would that hug be followed up by protestations of friendship? And I didn't really hear that. And if you look at the Chinese government readout of the three-way meeting, it's just blandishments about the need for peace and friendship.
SCHMITZ: That's interesting. So what you noticed is something that actually didn't happen?
DALY: He doesn't return the compliments.
SCHMITZ: What does that mean for the overall U.S.-China relationship in the eyes of someone like Xi Jinping, who's leading this country forward?
DALY: Well, Xi, as I see it - he's not inclined to discuss his emotions or his feelings about even fellow Chinese ever.
SCHMITZ: Right.
DALY: There's a sense that as a leader of 1.4 billion, he should sort of be above personality...
SCHMITZ: OK.
DALY: ...And not show any of that. So it's part and parcel of that. But also if you compliment me and I don't return the compliment, there's a sense in which I win, right? At least in Chinese - in terms of Chinese political culture, that's true.
SCHMITZ: I sort of feel like this - were three leaders that were just saying, hey, how are you doing? You know, you look great. We're all great partners. And there wasn't really much something substantive coming out of this.
DALY: President Trump did get them to speak publicly about nuclear weapons, and this gives a thin but an important foundation perhaps for later talks. China is nowhere near ready to talk about reducing its nuclear weapons. They continue to build and will until they get parity in about 10 years. But Xi Jinping did agree to talk about reducing nuclear risks. That's left vague, and they're talking about nonproliferation, but it opens the door. It opens the door a crack, and I think that's a good thing.
SCHMITZ: Anything that maybe worried you about this summit?
DALY: Well, the question is, you know, whether President Trump is now setting a precedent for the way that other American leaders will behave vis-a-vis China. When you get these descriptions of friendship - Xi Jinping is a great leader, China is a great country - if the next guy doesn't repeat those phrases, then that will seem like a falling off.
We seem to be going more and more for performance. In fact, all American presidents who have visited China have always been pleasant and respectful. Trump didn't invent this. But they've also described American interests, American values, America's dissatisfaction with China.
And that's been OK. The Chinese don't expect the Americans to be exactly like them. But now Trump, I worry, is setting a precedent that makes it difficult to talk about the real problems we all face, and he doesn't seem to have done that at this meeting.
SCHMITZ: That is Robert Daly. He's the director of the China and United States Program at University of Maryland School of Public Policy. Thanks so much for coming in.
DALY: Thank you.
