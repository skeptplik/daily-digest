In 2012 in Ireland, Dr Savita Halappanavar, who was then 17 weeks pregnant, attended a hospital with back pain. An examination found the pregnancy sac protruding through her womb. Her medical team refused to perform a procedure to end her pregnancy, because in Ireland the law prohibited abortion if a fetal heartbeat was still present. The 31-year-old developed sepsis and subsequently died of a cardiac arrest. During an inquest into her death, a midwife admitted to have said, “We don’t do that [abortion] here. Ireland is a Catholic country.” Halappanavar’s death became a catalyst for the campaign that ultimately led to the legalisation of abortion in Ireland.
This would never happen in Australia in 2026, I hear you say. Except, in Australia, Catholic hospitals provide healthcare based on the same Catholic beliefs as Irish hospitals did in 2012. And this scenario could very easily occur in Australia. In 2022, an ABC journalist interviewed an obstetrician working in an Australian Catholic hospital who described admitting a woman at 17 or 18 weeks whose waters had broken and a fetal heartbeat was still present. As the woman’s condition steadily deteriorated, the doctor was unable to recommend the care she required.
As the Catholic healthcare provider Calvary acquires 14 Healthscope hospitals, the implications for women’s healthcare in Australia become even more concerning. The hospitals in question could cease to provide a range of legal healthcare procedures, the majority of which affect women. Calvary follows the Catholic Health Australia’s code of ethical standards, which bars contraception, termination of pregnancy, IVF and voluntary assisted dying.
The consequences would extend well beyond individual patients, potentially creating significant gaps in healthcare access across metropolitan, regional and rural Australia. The scenarios described above could be repeated across multiple hospitals.
This is not just about abortion care though. In Australia, one out of every 18 babies is conceived by IVF. For mothers older than 35, this number rises to one in 10 babies. Catholic healthcare does not provide IVF services. Nor do they permit contraceptive services to be provided on their premises. These restrictions affect women’s ability to make decisions about their reproductive health and access timely, appropriate care.
In New South Wales, the state health minister has announced that the local public health provider will provide access to its theatres for IVF procedures. Calvary has said it will work with clinicians to provide pathways for treatments no longer offered under the acquisition.
While this may offer a partial solution, it raises more questions than it answers. It is not clear whether IVF procedures will be accommodated within already existing theatre sessions or if additional sessions will be allocated. If public hospitals provide additional services, how will this be funded and staffed? What will the effect on current elective gynaecological waitlists be? Will women in the Hunter Valley wait longer for their gynaecological procedures? And while this may be a viable solution for IVF, what comparable arrangements will ensure access to abortion and contraception care?
More importantly, how do state governments intend to guarantee access to essential healthcare when both public and private healthcare providers in a region operate under Catholic healthcare directives?
This risks creating geographic deserts for abortion, contraception and IVF services, leaving women with fewer options and greater barriers to care. Women might be forced to travel further for essential healthcare, adding travel costs during an already challenging cost-of-living crisis. At the same time, the shrinking number of non-religious healthcare providers will place even greater pressure on the remaining services, increasing waiting times and further restricting access.
The bigger question of religion-based healthcare remains. Individual clinicians have the ability to provide services that align with their personal beliefs, but should healthcare institutions have the same right? Australians should not have to travel hundreds of kilometres, wait longer for treatment or risk their health because the hospital closest to them refuses to provide care.
A hospital operator’s religious beliefs should not decide healthcare options available to Australians. It is time for Australians demand religion-free healthcare from our leaders, regardless of where people live or which hospital they attend.
