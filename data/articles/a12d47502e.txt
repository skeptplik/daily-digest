What the U.S. might learn from Canada's privatized air traffic control
Grounding of U.S. flights days ago has sparked calls to follow Canada's lead
It's not often that commentators from the U.S. political right will look to Canada as a model for how their country should run a particular function.
Yet the grounding of thousands of flights due to a router issue days ago has sparked some renewed calls for the U.S. to privatize its air traffic control system, much like its neighbour to the north did 30 years ago.
A recent editorial in the conservative magazine National Review called on the U.S. to privatize air traffic control and heaped praise on Canada's approach for its lead in technological advancement and the speed at which its able to upgrade its system.
And Canadian aviation experts agree that the American air traffic control system, sorely in need of upgrading, could certainly learn from Canada's own privatized structure.
"We can feel very smug and superior. We've been doing it right for 30 years, and the Americans have finally recognized that. At least some of them," said Anthony Perl, a political science professor and transportation policy specialist at Simon Fraser University.
Nav Canada takes off
Back in the '80s and early '90s, Canada's government-run air traffic control system was outdated, and in need of millions of dollars of investment to update its infrastructure, says John Gradek, a faculty lecturer in supply networks and aviation management at McGill University.
Facing a potentially enormous bill, the government decided to transition its air traffic services to a privatized model, he said.
Nav Canada, a non-profit company, began managing air traffic in 1996. It was a move, according to the Nav Canada website, that made Canada the first country in the world to privatize its civil air navigation system.
That means, instead of being controlled by the government and collecting money through taxes that have to go through Parliament and different ministries, Nav Canada charts its own course. For revenue, it charges user fees, sending bills to airlines using their services.
"It's kind of like your cell phone, if you don't have an unlimited plan, they send bills out based on the volume of use, and the rates are correlated to what it actually costs to run the system, to deliver those services," Perl said.
Perl pointed out that regulation is an essential ingredient in this formula. He said Transport Canada is still a key player in making sure Nav Canada doesn't cut corners "because you have to have strong regulation."
Nav Canada uses business modelling to forecast the costs of what it takes to actually run the system and keep it in a state of good repair, he said.
'Control their own destiny'
Gradek added that this also means that at any point in time Nav Canada knows the state of its finances, its operating expenses and where they are in terms of funding for programs.
"So they control their own destiny in terms of 'We're not going to overspend. We're going to spend money where we think we get the biggest bang for the buck.'"
As an example of Nav Canada's quick ability to modernize, Gradek points to paper-based flight data strips that it replaced with electronic strips that are accessible through touchscreens. Nav Canada made this upgrade in 1998, two years after it began running air traffic control.
Meanwhile, the U.S. has yet to replace that system.
The reason for this is simple, says Gradek.
"Money, money, money, money," he said.
Funding for the U.S. Federal Aviation Administration, the agency overseeing air traffic, is "a political football," he said.
Getting operating funds from U.S. Congress for capital expenditures to improve the system and get projects running quickly and efficiently is a challenge, Gradek said.
In Canada, it's up to Nav Canada to determine the priorities for investment to improve the productivity and efficiency of the system, he said.
Travellers pay
Still, the user-fee structure of Canada's air traffic control system means Canadians are seemingly paying more for flights since the costs of air traffic control in the U.S. are covered by the government.
But Perl said that just means Canadians are actually paying for the costs of the infrastructure instead of underfunding it.
Travellers wait at LaGuardia Airport in New York on Friday after the U.S. Federal Aviation Administration grounded flights nationwide. (Ryan Murphy/The Associated Press)
Aidan Hollis, an economics professor and academic director of the research program in competition and regulation at the University of Calgary, said air traffic control is funded either by taxes or the flying traveller.
"It makes sense to pay for it on the basis of users, rather than someone who's never flown in their life having to pay taxes to support a system, which they don't use."
In 2017, during Donald Trump's first term, the U.S. president urged Congress to approve a privatization plan that he said would increase safety and reduce wait times for passengers.
That plan, however, got stalled. Some U.S. politicians and organizations have expressed doubts about the efficiency of a privatized air traffic control system.
The non-partisan Congressional Research Service issued a report in 2017 that concluded there does not appear to be "conclusive evidence" that a privatized model "is either superior or inferior to others ... including FAA, with respect to productivity, cost-effectiveness, service quality, and safety and security."
Meanwhile, the U.S.-based National Business Aviation Association (NBAA) says privatization in Canada has resulted in funding instability, technological disruptions, flight delays and safety concerns. It cited a 2023 International Civil Aviation Organization report giving Canada a C grade score on oversight and safety.
In an email to CBC News, Maryam Amini, a spokeswoman for Nav Canada, said those claims being made about Canada's system are not supported by facts.
She said the ICAO audit being cited was not an audit of Nav Canada but instead assessed Canada's safety oversight system, which is the responsibility of Transport Canada.
"Canada's air navigation system is among the safest in the world," she said.
