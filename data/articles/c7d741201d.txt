Why Queues?

To begin with, it would just poke a HTTP endpoint on the core app and it would kick off a job. Simple enough. That was a good start, but it had some immediate problems and some of these might feel familiar. We had no ability to smooth the load. We had a whole load of customer workloads configured on the scheduler, and one of those workloads was backups, and we let people pick when they wanted to run their backup. The technology most people land on is OpenTelemetry. It's an open-source protocol, which allows you to do a lot of things, but for the purpose of this talk, we're going to focus on its support for distributed tracing. Distributed tracing, you can think of it a little like structured logs. We all know what a log is, and structured logs do what they say on the tin. They let you pass not just a string, but a structured set of keys and values, which allows you to query and filter the events a bit more easily. Traces are very similar, in that they are also made of a bunch of structured events with arbitrary keys and values. What traces have over structured logs is the ability to assign a hierarchy to those events, even when the events are produced by different parts of your system. The hierarchy allows you to group all of the events that stem from a single operation or customer request.
Logging this would probably be called like a correlation ID, but it also allows you to get a sense of the cause and effect relationship between events. What was the full sequence of events that led to an error or a delay? Which systems did the request pass through before this one? Of course, it's called distributed tracing, because this hierarchy can be preserved and reconstructed, even when different components are omitting different parts of the trace.
This is what a very simple trace might look like. You have a route span, which represents some interaction with an entry point to your system. This might be a customer hitting your API, and a number of child spans, which represent the activities that the system has to do to respond to the request. These spans might represent things like database requests, calls to other systems, or specific functions developers are interested in, and have added their own telemetry for. There are two interesting things here. The spans are connected by three special attributes defined by the OTel standard. There's the trace ID, which means that we can associate the span with the trace it belongs to, the span ID, which uniquely identifies the span itself, and the parent ID, which identifies which span preceded this one.
