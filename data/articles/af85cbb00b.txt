Ministers are meeting today in Downing Street to decide whether to cut legally binding requirements for carmakers to ensure 80% of the vehicles they sell in 2030 are electric.
Jonathan Reynolds, the business secretary, is pushing for the target to be reduced to 50%, but Miatta Fahnbulleh, the energy secretary, wants to keep it as close to 80% as possible.
But as ministers wrangle among each other, City Hall officials have made clear that the London mayor is on Fahnbulleh’s side.
Khan’s position was laid out in a letter from his deputy mayor for transport Seb Dance to Oliver Lord, the UK director of Clean Cities, which has been seen by the Guardian.
Dance wrote:
We and Transport for London share your view that the ZEV [zero-emission vehicle] mandate must not change. We outlined in our submission to the government’s consultation that London supports a strong and credible ZEV mandate.
We do not support any weakening of the current trajectories to 2030 or beyond, which risks damaging the case for investment in the ZEV and charging infrastructure industries, slowing down the pace of change, and the associated reduction in harmful emissions.
Department for Transport officials insist that no decision has yet been made but with the government likely to announce a cut to the 2030 target in the next two
weeks, this is likely to leave Khan opposed to official government policy.

Balls says he advised Miliband not to go ahead with 'ridiculous' interview with Russell Brand during 2015 election
Ed Balls has said that he warned Ed Miliband not to do an interview with Russell Brand during the 2015 election campaign. Miliband was Labour leader at the time, Balls shadow chancellor, and the interview – which was controversial at the time – was quoted by Kemi Badenoch this week in the light of Brand’s conviction for rape as evidence of Miliband’s flawed judgment.
Speaking on his Political Currency podcast, Balls said he had a dim view of Brand at the time of the interview.
I actually only met him once in the green room on the Andrew Marr programme [...] he behaved really weirdly and was sort of trying to come up and kind of prod me and get in my face and I was concentrating on my interview and never engaged.
Balls said Brand had also insulted him during a Channel 4 show.
Describing what happened when he found out that Miliband was going to do an election interview with him, Balls said:
At the time, the feedback from my office back to them was what are you doing with this guy? I mean, he’s weird, and he’s untrustworthy, and he’s already been removed from the BBC for his behaviour. And do we really want this?
But they decided to go ahead because they thought it would solve a political problem.
I ended up having to go on the television, in which I have to defend this because I’m the shadow chancellor. So I say, well, you know, I guess they think it will connect with the young people, and so be it.
But, although he defended the move in public, privately he thought doing the interview was “ridiculous”, he said.

Reform UK is seen as a rightwing party. But that does not mean it is automatically pro-business. In his speech this afternoon, at a rally where the party unveiled its “industrial strongholds” policy (a revamped version of enterprize zones), Robert Jenrick, the party’s Treasury spokesperson, launched a strong attack on the owners of Poundland.
He said:
Take Poundland.
A father and a son. A market stall in this city. One shop by 1990. Six hundred shops today, and twelve thousand people on the payroll.
That is the Black Country story. Start small. Work hard. Build something that lasts.
But what happened to it?
It was sold abroad. Then it was sold again, to an American corporate raider, for a pound. The whole company. For a pound. They don’t even sell the products for that anymore.
Poundland is on course to make eighteen million quid in profit this year. Yet, its owners have filed for administration, despite buyers at the door offering to keep it whole. Why? So they can sell it off for parts and fat fees.
Think about that. A business that makes money. That employs twelve thousand people. That sells what families can actually afford. And the system’s answer is: break it up.
That isn’t capitalism. These aren’t titans of industry. They’re shysters.
These people are organ harvesters masquerading as doctors.
They don’t want the patient to live. They want him on the table, still warm, so they can sell him off a piece at a time.
Capitalism is the father and the son on the stall. This is something else. A model where it pays better to strip a British business than to build one.

Reform UK proposes to set up 'industrial strongholds', starting in Black Country, with tax breaks to boost jobs
Ben Quinn
Ben Quinn is a Guardian political correspondent.
Reform UK has pledged to create “industrial strongholds” that would get tax breaks and other incentives aimed at spurring reindustrialisation, with the Black Country identified by the party as the first area for one.
The West Midlands area encompassing Wolverhampton, Dudley, Sandwell, Walsall and Cannock Chase – all Reform UK targets in any upcoming general election – was identified by the party as its first proposed “stronghold” zone.
Each zone would be a place where employer national insurance on net new hires would be halved, along with “streamlining” of planning policy and 100% first-year tax relief on new brownfield residential buildings and extensions.
Four more areas are to be unveiled by Reform UK in the months ahead, a rally in Wolverhampton was told by Reform’s economics spokesperson, Robert Jenrick.
Gordon Brown’s controversial 2007 pledge to create “British jobs for British workers” was also repurposed by Jenrick in a speech in which he spoke of plans for new visa sponsorship rules. He said:
Under Reform, jobs in Britain go to British workers first. Advertised here, to our people, before an employer can look anywhere else. And no sitting out the clock to fly somebody in on a visa.
Cheaper to hire from Bilston. Dearer to hire from abroad. British jobs for British workers. It’s about time you came first.
In a briefing explaining how the policy would work, Reform UK said:
Each Industrial Stronghold will be a defined geographical area, within which businesses will benefit from significant incentives for growth. These will include:
-Employer national insurance contributions reduced by 50% for the first £50,000 of earnings for net additional employees for five years.
-100% first-year tax relief on new brownfield residential buildings, extensions and substantial improvements.
-10-year exemptions on the extra business rates that result from new investment, from improvements, and from establishing a business in a high street retail unit that has been vacant for more than two years.
-Streamlining planning policy by creating a presumption in favour of development according to an updated local plan, with a strictly limited set of circumstances in which planning applications can be refused.
