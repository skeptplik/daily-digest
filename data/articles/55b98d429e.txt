Australia is nearly on track to reach its 2030 emissions reduction target but will fall massively short of a 2035 target unless the federal government substantially ramps up its climate policies, according to new official projections.
Under existing policies, the country is on track by 2035 to cut climate pollution by only 48% compared with 2005 levels – far below its target reduction of between 62% and 70% by that date, climate department projections suggest.
Australia’s progress towards the 2035 target has not improved since the last projection released a year ago.
In an annual climate statement to parliament on Thursday, the climate change and energy minister, Chris Bowen, said that while the country was “making good progress, we are facing undeniable headwinds”.
Bowen, speaking after a bruising experience over failed climate talks at Cop31, attacked the Queensland Liberal National government’s decision to “abandon its renewable energy targets and extend the operation of coal-fired power plants”, saying it would “significantly impact” national emissions.
“Continuing to sweat intermittent and unreliable coal just isn’t the answer to the question of our future energy security. And walking back previously approved renewable projects creates uncertainty, which then goes on to jeopardise future renewable investments,” he said. “Our system needs certainty. It does not need sovereign risk.”
The projections suggested Australia’s climate pollution was on track to be 42% below 2005 levels by 2030 – “within reach” of its 43% 2030 emissions reduction target.
However, that forecast assumes the government will reach its goal of 82% of electricity coming from renewable energy nationally by 2030. Several experts say that is unlikely to happen due to delays in the rollout of large-scale wind and farms and new transmission lines.
The department’s assessment said if unforeseen delays and supply chain bottlenecks pushed the 82% target back to 2032, Australia would only cut emissions by only 38% by 2030.
A separate report released on Thursday found that climate pollution fell by 1.3% – 6.2m tonnes – in the year to June. Bowen said this represented “a reduction at one-and-a-half times the rate of the last 10 years”. Australia’s total annual emissions were 25.3% below 2005 levels.
Emissions from generating electricity were down 3.6%, reflecting “record renewables generation and the ongoing displacement of coal and gas”. Gas generation fell by 23.2% and coal generation decreased by 3.6% over the year.
A third report, an annual progress assessment by another government agency, the Climate Change Authority, warned the country’s pace of emissions reduction must “accelerate substantially”. It said achieving the 2030 and 2035 targets would require sustained average annual emissions reductions of about 25m to 30m tonnes.
The authority found transport was approaching a “tipping point”, with electric and plug-in hybrid vehicles accounting for 29% of new light vehicle sales in 2026, up from 13% in 2025. The authority said more responsibility for emissions cuts would shift towards other areas, including “industry, resources, agriculture and other harder-to-abate activities”.
It warned additional action was needed to deal with methane – a potent greenhouse that amplifies global heating over a short time period. It recommended more be done to deploy established technologies, such as detecting and repairing leaks at mining sites and minimising avoidable venting and flaring of fossil fuel gas.
In a reply to Bowen’s climate statement, the shadow energy and emissions reduction minister, Dan Tehan, focused on electricity prices and the Cop31 breakdown – and the cost of free coffee at the Australian pavilion at the summit – rather than addressing the climate crisis.
He said national emissions had increased since Labor was elected in 2022, but did not mention that climate pollution was suppressed in 2022 due to economic shutdowns during the Covid-19 pandemic.
The Greens’ deputy leader and climate spokesperson, Steph Hodgins-May, said the government’s rhetoric on climate change “is just so far apart from the action”.
She said the government was basing its climate strategy on further electrification, “while still waving through new coal and gas”.
“They’re telling us they want to take climate action, but [are] simultaneously approving 38 new coal and gas mines,” Hodgins-May said.
The Australian Conservation Foundation’s head of advocacy, Gavan McFadzean, said the government report card showed strong progress in some areas but there was clear room for improvement.
“The renewables build is progressing, but it faces headwinds from uncooperative states and other forces whipping up opposition to wind and solar in rural communities for political purposes,” he said.
McFadzean said Australia’s exported emissions from the burning of coal and gas overseas, totalling 1.1 bn tonnes in 2025-26, “is not in this statement and remains a blind spot”.
“Exported emissions are the biggest handbrake on Australian climate action,” he said. “For Australia to be a positive climate player, we need to phase out exports of coal and gas.”
