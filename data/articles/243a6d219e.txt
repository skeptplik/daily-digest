After years toiling all day at a desk as an aerospace engineer in Istanbul, 26-year-old Mohannad Najjar decided to swap his screen for a degree in nature and theology in Cambridge and a volunteering gig as a scout leader.
He walks in the countryside every weekend and has hiked in wild locations in the Scottish Highlands, which he credits with helping him think clearly.
"I just want to see something that's untouched, to be in nature," he says. "When you come in from a hike or a walk, you just feel different."
Najjar is one of a growing number of young hikers in the UK. Nearly 9% of 16-34 year-olds are regular hill walkers or hikers, according to Sport England, up from 2.3% eight years ago.
Many young walkers' groups from Inverness to Nottinghamshire have seen their membership rise over the past two years, according to the Ramblers walking charity.
Demand has nearly doubled for a course it offers teaching map and compass skills to 18-25 year-olds.
Many who sign up want to ditch their phones and no longer rely on a blue dot to navigate, says Alistair Cooper, who leads the Out There Award programme.
"There's a cohort that missed out on outdoorsy, fun stuff during Covid. They want to get out and explore - but often don't know where to start."
Some 83% of people in the UK live in towns and cities, according to the UN, where research suggests noise, traffic, stress and information overload put huge demands on our brains, affecting our mental health.
"Nature is the environment we are adapted to, and being close to it brings benefits such as improved wellbeing, brain function and physical performance," says Dr Danny Longman, lecturer in human evolutionary physiology from Loughborough University.
"There is a theory that simply paying attention to trees and leaves allows our brains to recover," he says.
'Grounding'
Environmental science student Deborah Odutayo, 24, described going through "an identity crisis".
"I didn't see myself as someone who works behind a desk from 9 to 5," she said.
After solo travelling, Odutayo had dreams of living abroad but didn't know how to finance them. So she decided to change her mindset.
"I just need to be outside, essentially. That's how I started like doing solo hikes and stuff. It feels very therapeutic - mostly for my mental health."
She's since hiked in Kent and the Peak District, among other locations, and camped with an outdoor group in Dorset.
Learning how to get around without using her phone or digital maps is something she is proud of, saying it feels "more intentional and grounding".
Friendship flux
Walking gives people "an instant friend group" when life is in flux, says Miranda Chambers-Reid, who leads a Ramblers' walking group for people in their 20s and 30s in Sheffield.
"They don't want all of their friends to be work friends or old uni friends," she says.
As a mum of a seven-year-old, she made a similar decision when she joined: "I thought, I don't want all of my friends to be mum friends."
Over the last two years, the group has increased from 250 members to 350.
For some it's more about a sense of achievement says Chambers-Reid, who herself covers about 30 miles of walking a week.
"I work in an office and I can't see the sky so whenever possible I will be outside."
For Sej Chauhan, 27, her walking group is about making connections while keeping costs down.
"There's a growing interest in socialising in ways that don't involve drinking alcohol or spending lots of money," she says.
Chauhan set up Girls Who Walk in her home town of Leicester after a lonely year spent studying in Bristol.
To make that experience easier for others, the group's focus is on walking in parks, talking and female companionship. And there's always a stop for coffee.
In two years, the group has grown to more than 300 members - most are 25-35 years old.
"There's no pressure, no barriers with walking," Chauhan says. "Social media can often fill the void outside of work and home, but it doesn't provide the connection that people are craving."
Not everyone can access green space easily or has the time to go hiking because of work or childcare commitments.
Here are Longman's tips for getting your nature fix as close as possible to home:
- Move in nature - an urban park or garden will do
- Listen to the sounds of the elements, like wind, rain, birdsong
- Smell the distinctive scent of pine forests, breathe in the outdoors
- If you are indoors, bring the sounds and imagery of nature into your home using apps
