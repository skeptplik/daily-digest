Nvidia scandal has put Singapore in U.S. sights. What's at stake for the city-state
Megaspeed and other Singapore companies have been accused of diverting AI chips to China, skirting U.S. ban
The Nvidia crisis has put Singapore squarely in Washington's sights amid concerns over its role in China's access to advanced AI chips. Here's what's at stake for the Southeast Asian nation and why it's stepping up efforts to prevent companies from circumventing U.S. export controls by diverting chips through its jurisdiction.
Prior to the emergence of the news that the CEO of chip giant Nvidia had an affair with a former executive of Singapore-based cloud computing company Megaspeed who may have been spying for China, Singapore had positioned itself as a key intermediary in the global AI supply chain.
The city-state benefited from its access to advanced U.S. chips and its established commercial ties with Chinese technology companies.
Singapore accounted for 22 per cent of Nvidia's global revenue last year, according to its financial filings before it stopped disclosing its Singapore sales and revenue. That's despite the fact that the vast majority of the products billed here were shipped elsewhere.
But allegations that Megaspeed diverted advanced AI chips to China have raised a critical question: were businesses here doing enough to verify where the hardware was ultimately going?
Alice Huang, seen in screenshot from a video provided by Taiwanese broadcaster TVBS, is suspected of spying for China while having an affair with Jensen Huang, CEO of Nvidia. Here, they are seen at a restaurant in Taipei, Taiwan, in May 2025. (TVBS/The Associated Press)
"That question was never asked: When you do business, would you ask where the thing goes? said Mark Goh, a professor of analytics and operations at the National University of Singapore's business school.
"When the money comes in and nothing is rocking the boat, you say, 'Fine, we'll do it,'"

Now, Singapore is having to rethink its approach. It has launched an investigation into Megaspeed and said it's co-operating with U.S. authorities, who have been looking into the company's alleged diversion of chips to China since 2024 and added it to its trade blacklist earlier this week.
Singapore's access to U.S. tech at risk
What's at stake isn't just its reputation as a trusted global trading hub but its continued access to advanced U.S. technology and its ambitions to become a leading AI hub.
Earlier this week, Prime Minister Lawrence Wong said Singapore was working closely with Washington to improve information sharing and set up a mechanism allowing U.S. authorities to conduct checks and enforce its own export controls.
Singapore's prime minister, Lawrence Wong, seen in Jakarta, Indonesia, in July 2026, said he is co-operating with U.S. authorities in efforts to clamp down on the diversion of AI chips to China through the Southeast Asian country. (Yasuyoshi Chiba/AFP/Getty Images)
But tighter enforcement will require greater visibility over a complex global supply chain.
One of the biggest challenges is tracking what happens to the chips after the initial sale. While suppliers are required to carry out checks on their customers, those customers may subsequently resell the chips to restricted parties.
"From a legal point of view, the selling company is not liable for the onward resale as long as the proper due diligence was carried out," said Goh Puay Guan, an associate professor specialising in supply chain management at NUS business school.
'They can't open every container'
Preventing the diversion of sensitive technology will likely require more stringent checks on end users.
For businesses in Singapore, that could mean higher compliance costs, longer transaction times and greater risks associated with doing business with Chinese technology companies.
The biggest concern for Singapore, perhaps, is losing its appeal as an intermediary between the world's two largest economies.
Shipping containers are seen at the port in Singapore. Local authorities are under pressure to step up monitoring of AI chips being shipped through the city-state. (Adrian Wyld/The Canadian Press)
"They need to keep the trust with both parties," said Matthias Hendrichs, an independent AI industry adviser.
"They need to step up the investigations and the monitoring they do.... At the same time, there can't be 100 per cent follow-up. They can't open every container that comes into the Singapore harbour."
U.S. moves to restrict cloud computing access
But it's not just the physical movement of chips that Singapore is now under pressure to monitor.
Washington is also moving to close a loophole in its export controls. Until now, Chinese technology companies could access advanced U.S. chips legally by renting computing power from overseas data centres.
That demand has helped fuel Southeast Asia's data-centre boom, with investment in Singapore alone forecast to reach $7 billion US this year, according to research by PwC and Oxford Economics.
Under the new U.S. restrictions, data-centre operators serving major Chinese AI customers have until mid-February to secure licences or risk losing access to advanced chips.
Separately, the Financial Times, citing people familiar with the matter, reported that Nvidia had instructed its manufacturing partners to halt shipments of servers to Singapore, Malaysia, Thailand and Indonesia.
Malaysian customs officers stand next to a seized server containing advanced AI chips last month. The Financial Times reported Wednesday that Nvidia is halting shipments of servers to Malaysia, Singapore, Thailand and Indonesia. (Vincent Thian/The Associated Press)
It is unclear how long the suspension will last, but it adds to the uncertainty facing the region's data-centre industry and risks delaying new projects.
"If projects lose anchor customers or cannot assure lenders that they have a reliable chip supply, financing could be delayed or fall through," said Han-Wei Liu, an associate professor of law at Singapore Management University.

"That could slow the expansion of computing capacity across Southeast Asia and make it harder for local firms and researchers to develop AI."
Higher compliance costs could also eat into the profits of existing operators.
For Singapore, it adds to growing uncertainty over its future in the global AI supply chain.
