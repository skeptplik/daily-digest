I experienced sleep paralysis and hypnagogic hallucinations from childhood and into adulthood, with the episodes only waning in my 30s (Touch of evil: the terrifying truth about sleep paralysis, 11 November). I never spoke about them to anyone.
I experienced hallucinations in the form of severe perspective distortion (oversized bed and rooms) and full hallucinations/dream states, auditory hallucinations (screaming and loud thumping) and, of course, paralysis. I didn’t know at what point I was asleep or if I had woken and could easily slip back into a hypnagogic state. I only became aware of this state as an adult, having been referred to a psychiatrist on an unrelated matter.
Some of the episodes were literally the stuff of nightmares. I have always had exceptionally vivid dreams and rarely do I go through a night without a dream waking me up (or so it seems). Once I understood what was happening, it was less terrifying and more fascinating, though the actual physical state cannot fail to be frightening.
It is such a strange state to experience, and while I am glad that the episodes are much less frequent and severe now, in a strange way I slightly miss the otherworldliness of it, and I would not ask to never have experienced it, even some of the most terrifying episodes. Perhaps most interesting is how quickly the fear dissipates once properly out of the state, though that can take some time.

Sleep paralysis struck me years before I learned, at an exhibition at the Wellcome Institute, that this was not just me being weird in my sleep, but a proper thing with a name. And yes, it is absolutely petrifying. I still remember how relieved I was on the one hand and fascinated on the other by all the evidence of sleep paralysis through the centuries at this exhibition.
Since then, I have only seen one article about it, until now. Thank you, Amy Fleming, for finding words for something that is so difficult to put into words that I have never even tried to mention it to anyone. I find it equally helpful and super-interesting that the “monster” is being studied by neuroscientists. It is comforting to be able to say: “Look, this is not me making something up, this is real.”
Barbara Geier
London

 Your article reminded me of an old custom in the Alpine region: to ward off night-time disturbances caused by the Toggeli (a night spirit), Swiss farmers would fasten a Werchhechel (a tool for combing flax, studded with numerous long, pointed iron needles) to their chests. This served to physically ward off the nocturnal tormentor, which choked the sleeper’s breath and caused severe nightmares or sleep paralysis.
