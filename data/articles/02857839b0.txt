Benjamin Franklin doesn’t get enough credit. Yes, he was a founding father of the United States; he invented the lightning rod and his face is printed on $100 bills. But rarely is he celebrated for his contributions to the most meaningful field of human achievement: wellness influencing.
Centuries before GRWM videos and scientifically dubious podcasts were a dollar-sign-shaped glint in their creators’ eyes, Franklin was doling out life advice. Under the pseudonym Poor Richard, Franklin published an annual pamphlet and minted maxims like “Diligence is the mother of good luck” and “Fish and visitors stink after three days.”
In his autobiography, which he worked on for 20 years and was published posthumously, he detailed his daily routine – a sensible schedule that included “taking the resolution of the day”, “overlooking accounts” and “conversation”. He also enumerated a list of 13 virtues he believed were necessary for a good life. These include temperance, silence, frugality, industry, tranquility, and my favorite: “Humility: Imitate Jesus and Socrates”.
His efforts seem to have paid off. “He lived to 84, which is a good age for the 18th century,” says Joseph Adelman, history professor and co-host of the Ben Franklin’s World podcast. Adelman describes Franklin as an “inveterate improver”, constantly tinkering at the margins of his own habits in an attempt to improve himself.
A perpetual quest for optimization is normal today, but it was considered eccentric at the time, says Adelman. For example, Franklin dabbled with vegetarianism long before it was common. “My refusing to eat flesh occasioned an inconveniency, and I was frequently chided for my singularity,” he recalls in his autobiography.
As a wellness reporter, I was curious to find out how Franklin’s 18th-century advice translates to today. Plus, Franklin and I have a lot in common: we have both worked for newspapers, we have both flown kites, and now that I’m six months pregnant, we have the exact same body shape. So who better to emulate for a few days? Perhaps I would emerge with ideas for a new style of government, or interesting insights into thermodynamics.
Preparing to be Benjamin Franklin
To begin, I buy a copy of Franklin’s autobiography. Franklin referred to the work as his “memoirs”, but when it was released, the publisher called it The Autobiography of Benjamin Franklin, probably because it sounds less annoying. Unfinished projects and an inflated sense of self-importance? Franklin and I are basically twins.
The autobiography is a pleasant read and funnier than I expected. There’s one scene where Franklin somehow acquires too many bread rolls from a baker and runs into his future father-in-law, one roll under each arm and one in his mouth. “I made a most awkward, ridiculous appearance,” he says. What a goof, haha.
In the interest of journalistic integrity, I decide to check whether Franklin was indeed the aspirational lifestyle model he presented himself to be.
“We forget how vital and healthy [Franklin] was into his 50s,” says Rae Katherine Eighmey, food historian and author of Stirring the Pot with Benjamin Franklin: A Founding Father’s Culinary Adventures.
Franklin emphasized the importance of regular physical activity, especially if one had a relatively sedentary job, as he did. Similarly, he was adamant about paying attention to what one ate and drank, and the effects of alcohol. The latter was particularly notable at a time when diets were rich in alcohol and it was considered healthy to take a shot of liquor first thing in the morning, says Sarah Lohman, food historian and author.
Over the years, Franklin suffered from gout, psoriasis, pleurisy and bladder stones. But according to Adelman, “he does seem to have been, all things told, for an 18th-century white man, relatively healthy.”
Good enough for me!
Rise at 5am
Franklin loved an early morning (as Poor Richard, he coined the phrase: Early to bed and early to rise makes a man healthy, wealthy and wise). According to the daily schedule he shared in his autobiography, Franklin awoke most days at 5am. His morning routine lasted about two hours, and consisted of the following:
“Rise, wash, and address Powerful Goodness! [Ed note: his version of God/a higher power] Contrive day’s business, and take the resolution of the day; prosecute the present study, and breakfast.”
I was an early riser for many years. I would wake up before the sun and go to an exercise class, or start writing for work. But then I got pregnant, and a small alien in my uterus began gobbling up my life force. Then, my ideal amount of sleep changed to between 18 and 30 hours a day. It became all I could do to get out of bed and have my one weak cup of coffee.
Still, to honor my friend Ben, I set my alarm for my version of 5am, which is 7am. When it goes off, I roll out of bed with much moaning and groaning, and wash my face. I try to address my own Powerful Goodness, but I’m not great spiritual company because I’m so tired. I settle for saying, “Thanks for, you know, everything,” to the universe.
Air bathe
Franklin enjoyed “air baths”, Eighmey told me. These entailed “sitting naked shortly after arising in front of an open window”. This sounds kind of nice, actually, but unfortunately our windows all look into the apartment of our neighbor, a nice woman in her 60s whose cat was recently diagnosed with cancer. I decided she has enough on her plate without having to see me air bathing, so I skip it.
‘Prosecute the present study’
According to a quick Google search, “to prosecute the present study” means to spend time actively learning or working on a personal project, so I watch TikToks about the Love Is Blind reunion and text my thoughts to my husband – an honest-to-goodness early riser who is already at his office. (I detail why I’m rooting for Jax and Stew; he agrees climbing four volcanoes in a year is not enough to make Captain Blake interesting and that Erika deserves better.)
Breakfast
I have my usual wheat cereal with yogurt and berries, a low-effort meal that has nutrients the alien inside me needs. But I wish I were having Franklin’s favorite breakfast, which Eighemy tells me was fresh bread cubes tossed in melted butter, toasted, then dropped in a bowl of hot wheat cereal and topped with more butter. Yum! I might make this for myself sometime when I’m sad.
Work
From 8am to 11am, Franklin worked. I do this too. Twins! I am mostly coherent on my calls, and make some good jokes in the work Slack (admittedly, one is just pointing out a picture of a guy in a funny hat), so I consider it a successful morning. Franklin once wrote a satirical essay all about farting, so he would have probably had some good Slack bits too.
Lunch
Franklin took a two-hour lunch every day, which feels extremely luxurious. Lunch was the biggest meal of the day in Franklin’s time, said Lohman. It’s unclear what he ate most days, but according to his writings, his favorite foods included potatoes, parmesan and apples. I have some beef and potato stew I made over the weekend, which feels like the sort of heavy, solemn dish early American colonists would have eaten, and follow it up with an apple.
Overlook my accounts
I need a nap after my heavy meal. But first, I must “overlook my accounts”, as Franklin said he did at midday. I open the banking app on my phone, feel slightly depressed at the small number I discover there, then sleep for 30 minutes. Franklin did not usually take naps, but he merely gestated a democracy, not a human child.
Exercise
Physical activity was important to Franklin. I don’t have access to a pool or a stable to go riding – two of his favorite pastimes – but I do have dumbbells, which Franklin once wrote to his son, William, contain “a great quantity of exercise in a handful of minutes”. Franklin said he would do 40 swings with them, gripping one weight in each hand and swinging them back and forth by his sides.
I find a video of a man in a Franklin costume (no wig, sadly) demonstrating the exercise. After laughing for a while because it looks so silly, I try it myself. Although I fear it will cause a shoulder injury, the exercise is now known as “skier swings”, and various fitness blogs recommend it, as long as you keep the weight low.
When I do the exercise, I stop laughing. It’s harder than it looks and I tire out after 30 reps. I wonder whether Franklin had powerful shoulders, and I look up pictures of him, but the little jackets he wore didn’t really do his upper body justice. His calves looked rather shapely though.
Evening routine
Franklin and I both clock out at 6pm. From then until 10pm, Franklin went about his evening routine:
“Put things in their places, supper, music, or diversion, or conversation; examination of the day.”
As I empty the dishwasher, I imagine Franklin arranging all his little knick-knacks. But when I research what his homes were like, I find that a 1998 excavation of his terrace house in London uncovered not just knick-knacks, but a pit full of over 1200 human and animal bones. Apparently William Hewson, the son-in-law of Franklin’s landlady, ran an anatomy school from the house. Franklin seems to have been fine with this – he described the anatomist as an “excellent young man”.
After supping and conversing with my husband, I sit down to determine how my experiment has gone.
My strengths, I decided, were “order” (I basically stuck to Franklin’s schedule and accomplished my work tasks), “frugality” (I didn’t order an expensive sweater I wanted) and “cleanliness” (emptied dishwasher, wiped down kitchen counters). My weaknesses were “temperance” (a virtue Franklin describes as eating “not to dullness”, but I was decidedly dull after lunch), “silence” (I did not avoid “trifling conversation” – in fact, I actively sought it out) and “humility” (I praised myself throughout the day for how well I was being Ben Franklin).
Mixed performance overall. But Franklin struggled too. As he wrote in his autobiography: “I was surprised to find myself so much fuller of faults than I imagined.”
Conclusion
Would I recommend Franklin’s wellness routine? Yes! Being mindful of what you eat, getting enough exercise and focusing on personal growth are as helpful today as they were 250 years ago.
But our understanding of health and the body has progressed significantly since then. Today, instead of wildly swinging around dumbbells, Franklin might be following a customized strength training plan. RIP, Ben Franklin – you would have made an amazing TikTok wellness influencer.
