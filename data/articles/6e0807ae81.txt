Regional restaurant operators are bearing the brunt of Japan’s suspension of new foreign workers under the specified skilled worker program for the food service sector, according to a survey.
The survey, conducted by Mynavi Global, a Tokyo-based company that supports foreign workers seeking employment in Japan, found that 44.1% of restaurant operators outside Tokyo, Kanagawa, Chiba, Saitama, Osaka and Aichi prefectures said the suspension had made a “very significant impact” on their recruitment efforts.
The figure is considerably higher than the 27.8% reported by companies in the six major metropolitan prefectures, highlighting a widening human resource gap between urban and regional areas.
In April, the government paused approvals for foreign workers in the restaurant industry as the number of holders of the Type 1 residency status for foreign workers with designated skills in the category was approaching the upper limit of 50,000.
The survey released Tuesday covered the period between Sept. 24 and Oct. 20 — six months after the suspension of new admissions took effect — and received responses from 113 companies.
Nationwide, nearly 70% of respondents said the suspension had had either a “very significant” or “somewhat significant” impact on recruitment.
Regional restaurant operators also reported that the labor shortage and rising personnel costs had forced them to scale back or alter their business operations. Some said they had to postpone plans to open new restaurants or facilities.
The suspension is believed to have intensified competition for foreign workers, prompting more of them to move to major cities where wages are typically higher.
A restaurant in Kusatsu, Shiga Prefecture, that has two employees from Vietnam and Myanmar currently working in its kitchen, exemplifies the recruitment struggle amid the visa freeze.
Its operator — Kurashiki, Okayama Prefecture-based Excellent — had informally offered positions to two additional foreign workers just before the suspension took effect, but lengthy administrative procedures have prevented them from starting work.
The company plans to hire two Vietnamese workers who are changing jobs by the end of this year.
Hideki Toyota, the company’s president, said, “We couldn’t bring them on board in time for the Bon (summer) holiday season. Foreign workers are no exception in heading to Tokyo, where hourly wages are higher.”
