Doctors keep missing the ‘perfect storm’ for eating disorders
Anorexia, binge eating, and other disorders can emerge before and after childbirth, but the women who struggle with them often slip through the cracks of the U.S. health care system.
Research is limited, but we do know a few things: About 4.3% of pregnant women and more than 9% of new mothers experience eating disorders. Women with a history of eating disorders appear more likely to experience postpartum depression and anxiety and to report problems feeding their newborns. And obstetricians typically receive very little training about these conditions or how to use the few screening tools we have validated for eating disorders in pregnancy.
Medicare’s 2027 physician pay changes spell trouble for specialists
In the Centers for Medicare and Medicaid Services’ recent update to how Medicare pays physicians, the agency cut the payment rate for certain services provided on the same day by 20% when the services are provided by the same physician or practice.
A bit wonky, I know, but it’s part of the agency’s broader shift away from specialist care and the fee-for-service model, and one of many reimbursement challenges that specialists are contending with.
What does this mean? Opponents say the policy change will force patients to return for separate appointments and increase out-of-pocket costs and wait times. People with Parkinson’s disease, for example, could struggle to get outpatient Botox injections that are critical for relieving pain. Read more.

I’m grateful for this dispatch from a big conference of people who want to live forever, or at least create a lot of shareholder value before they die. I’m guilty of gawking at the loudest voices in the longevity movement and occasionally dismissing them as men who say alarming things while being weird with their fluids. Sarah Todd, being a good journalist, meets them where they are (the Bay Area, obviously) and finds that whether they’re pitching full-body scans or plotting to reenact ” Never Let Me Go,” most longevity enthusiasts are just like the rest of us: scared to death of inevitably dying. — Damian Garde, STAT reporter at large, live and feature journalism
“Thankful” is not exactly the best word for how I feel about Megan’s story about scientist Brian Donovan; it’s actually pretty depressing. But it’s stayed with me since April because it so well captured the broad impact of federal grant cancellations, the specific challenges interdisciplinary academic researchers face, and the important work that gets left behind as a result. — Andrew Joseph, STAT Europe correspondent
Great journalism doesn’t only uncover secret injustices or corruption. It also reveals the scourges that are hiding in plain sight. Alcohol is so embedded in our culture and consciousness that we may not realize how harmful it has been and could be. This amazing series doesn’t exist to ban drinks from our family Thanksgivings. But it does a great public service of explaining the scope of alcohol addiction, the influence of the industry, and the failures that got us to this point. — Bob Herman, STAT business of health care reporter
Not a story per se, but I loved getting to debrief the Elizabeth Holmes documentary with my fellow STAT reporters. — Margaret Manto, STAT Washington correspondent

It dug deeper into the lives and motivations of the people leading the company. The reporting led the way in scrutinizing how profiteers have taken advantage of a well-intended policy to ban surprise bills. It was followed by many other outlets and again showed why STAT’s agenda-setting coverage is so vital. — Casey Ross, STAT chief investigative reporter, data and technology
It’s easy to feel pessimistic about what technology is doing to our brains and those of future generations. Which is why I’m grateful for this story from Isa Cueto, which explores creative ways people are working to reclaim kids’ attention spans from compulsive social media scrolling. Rich with rigorous science and insights gleaned from time spent with real teenagers, it offers a welcome antidote to the notion that the attention crisis is a foregone facet of modern life. — Megan Molteni, STAT science writer
We think of human anatomy — organs, muscles, nerves, bones — as a conquered science. Medical students master its intricacies, layer by layer. We can scan the body, palpate it, examine its cells. But anatomy is not history. Subramaniam Krishnan, a cardiac electrophysiologist in California, reminded me that the architecture of our bodies still contains mysteries. Krishnan discovered a pouch within the atrial septum of one of the most studied organs in medical history. He described his finding in his First Opinion essay, “I discovered a new structure in the heart — just by looking closely.” Scientific insight, he wrote, is often limited by “our own assumptions about where to stop looking.” Krishnan nudges us to remember that the frontiers of medicine don’t lie only in AI or genomics — and that curiosity and observation can still spark discovery. — Kathy Burge, STAT contributing editor
