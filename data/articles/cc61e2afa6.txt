Vecna is arguably the archetypical "boss character" in Dungeons & Dragons. For almost 40 years now, Vecna's machinations have endangered D&D's multiverse, only to be opposed and defeated but never permanently destroyed by groups of outmatched adventurers. In his last outing, Vecna: Eve of Ruin, Vecna is designed to be a unique threat who's capable of winning against a party of 20th-level adventurers. On Azeroth, however, he'd just be another face in the crowd.
Dungeons & Dragons: World of Warcraft is officially out in early access. Fans, pros, and content creators have had a few days to break it down, to dig out the winners and losers in Wizards of the Coast's adaptation of WoW's mechanics to D&D 5.5e rules. (The debate is still ongoing at time of writing, but the Discipline Priest currently seems like a hugely powerful subclass for Cleric.)
One thing that isn't really up for debate, however, is that Wizards' writing team made WoW's signature characters into forces to be reckoned with. Only a relative handful of Warcraft's big names got stat blocks in the D&D crossover, but all of them have been designed as major threats in ways that mark a departure from D&D's past design choices.
Putting the 'war' in Warcraft
D&D's World of Warcraft book is set roughly around the time of WoW's 10th expansion, The War Within, with a few nods towards the current expansion Midnight. If you haven't kept up with the story of WoW’s modern servers for a few years, there's a lot in D&D: WoW that may come as a mild shock.
This book only provides stats for the relative handful of major NPCs who are currently center stage, namely Alleria, Anduin, Jaina, and Thrall, as well as fan favorites Illidan and Sylvanas. Arthas snuck in due to being the primary antagonist of the Icecrown Citadel map pack, despite being one of the most thoroughly dead characters in WoW as of Shadowlands.
At first glance, it may seem strange to the casual observer that all of these characters got stat blocks at all, given the ancient wisdom that you shouldn’t give stats to something if you aren’t willing to let the PCs fight and kill it. However, this can be seen as a case of Wizards being faithful to their source material. If a character has a name in World of Warcraft, there's a better than even chance that they'll be a raid or dungeon boss someday. Every signature character in this book has been a loot piñata in WoW at some point. Even Anduin fought players back in the Sepulcher of the First Ones raid, although he was mind-controlled into doing so.
What's more unusual is that every signature character in the WoW book came uniquely loaded for bear. Their stated CRs sit between 20 (Anduin) and 25 (Arthas), but Wizards' team has made a few changes to their usual design approach that give the WoW crew some more gas in the tank than previous books' signature characters.
For example, Vecna in Eve of Ruin is a CR 26 archlich with 272 hit points and no stated bonuses to initiative. He’s got a number of powerful abilities that let him self-heal for “free” and do tremendous amounts of damage, but 272 HP isn’t as much as it sounds like in 5e.
Conversely, every signature character in the WoW book has 430 (Illidan) to 500 HP (Arthas), along with a flat initiative bonus of +11 (Jaina) to +21 (Sylvanas). Those are simple bonuses, but they add up quickly. In any tabletop game, any way to improve the chance that you'll act first in a given round is a powerful asset.
These bonuses come on top of the usual retinue of unique skills that D&D tends to give signature characters in 5e, such as legendary resistances, bonus actions, and spell-like abilities. Alleria has an Aura of Darkness that can inflict the frightened condition on everyone around her, while Arthas constantly radiates bursts of cold that slow and exhaust every character within 30 feet. Anduin is a self-healing machine, Illidan has an actual second phase as he transforms into a demon, and Thrall can throw up to six Lava Bursts every round.
This isn’t to say that the WoW crew are necessarily overpowered. For example, Jaina has a few useful immunities and knows Mind Blank, but any sufficiently devoted D&D nerd will know a few "save or suck" spells that can substantially slow her roll. Even so, Jaina has the tools to ruin most D&D parties' day, like a high chance to petrify up to 3 PCs per round with Ice Prison. Compared to other recent high-CR signature spellcasters like Eve of Ruin's redesigned Alustriel Silverhand, Jaina's simply on another level.
This marks a departure from Wizards' previous practices, which frequently create monsters that are less potent than their stats might indicate. If you’ve ever played with people who mainline Laserllama or DnD Shorts videos in their off-time, you've likely run into this issue yourself: rules-as-written D&D 5th edition, especially after 2024, can be difficult to make into any kind of genuine challenge. Action economy, obscure spells, bad saving throws, and sometimes the dice can all conspire against you as a DM to make your campaign-ending supervillain into a paper tiger.
With the stat blocks in World of Warcraft, however, Wizards has made a genuine effort to make its potential raid bosses more challenging to take down. They've got more health and react faster, and the toughest among them have their own automatic save-or-suck effects. Even if you don't know the first thing about Warcraft, it might be worth taking a look at the new sourcebook for some ideas on how to turbocharge your next high-CR antagonist.
Dungeons & Dragons: World of Warcraft will be released on Nov. 17.
