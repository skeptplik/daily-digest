Drought threatens Iraq's marshes, risking one of the oldest ways of life
The unique marshes of southern Iraq are beginning to revive after five years of devastating drought. But challenges remain for one of the world's oldest ways of life.
A MARTÍNEZ, HOST:
In the marshes of southern Iraq, one of the world's oldest ways of life is under threat. Years of drought decimated the buffalo herds that people have depended on since the dawn of history. Over the last year, rain has revived some marshlands, but there's just not enough to ward off other threats. NPR's Jane Arraf and Fatima Al-Kassab bring us this report from Iraq.
(SOUNDBITE OF BUFFALO GROANING)
JANE ARRAF, BYLINE: It's just after sunrise in Iraq's marshes, about 50 miles from the ancient site of Ur. There are water buffalo wading in the shallow water. We're in a long, narrow boat called a mashuf, the same design used for thousands of years.
(SOUNDBITE OF BIRDS TWEETING)
ARRAF: Birds here include a few migratory species still passing through. Even after years of drought, there's incredible biodiversity here.

ARRAF: There are just, like, five or six of them, little ones. But wild boars just swam across and darted into the reeds.
It was a mother and her piglets, gone in a flash. Our guide, Jassim Al-Asadi, the co-founder of the environmental organization Nature Iraq, points out a white egret.
JASSIM AL-ASADI: You see the buffalo? With a bird - and there is a bird on the buffalo. You see?
ARRAF: Saddam Hussein drained the marshes in the 1990s after an uprising in southern Iraq. Asadi was born in the marshes, literally in a boat. When the U.S. toppled Saddam in 2003, Asadi - an engineer - played a key role in restoring them. His work almost cost him his life. Three years ago, he was kidnapped and tortured by an Iran-backed militia in Iraq. It accused him of being an Israeli spy because he met with foreign journalists. When he was freed, he insisted on returning to his work there.

Families then deserted the marshes, taking their remaining animals to places with more water. Now some of those families are starting to come back. But the future of the marshlands depends partly on the Iraqi government's water policies and those of Turkey and Syria, which share the rivers.
