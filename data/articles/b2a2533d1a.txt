Private jet owners will no longer receive fuel tax subsidies after the Albanese government secured the Greens’ agreement to undo the sweetheart deal in exchange for supporting a controversial minimum tax rate on discretionary trusts.
The government is eager to pass four key pieces of legislation before parliament finishes for the year, including laws to create a gas reservation scheme.
The deal with the Greens to land discretionary trusts followed an earlier announcement that Labor had agreed to carve out pensioners from reductions to private health insurance rebates for over-65s in return for the minor party’s support in the upper house.
The Greens leader, David Shoebridge, said it was a “disgrace” that wealthier individuals with private aircraft only paid a fraction of the fuel excise to jet around the country.
“We can announce that we have forced the government to end the lurk where the ultra-wealthy are paying one-fifteenth of the tax on the fuel that they use to fill up their private jets [compared with] ordinary motorists,” he said.
“Whether it’s Gina Rinehart or Clive Palmer or Pauline Hanson, they’re now going to be paying the same effective rate when they fill up their tanks as ordinary Australians do in a cost-of-living crisis.”
The government excise applied to petrol used by specific aircrafts is below 4 cents a litre while petrol and diesel used in vehicles is 54 cents a litre.
The tax change will apply a minimum 30% tax rate on income received from discretionary trusts from 2029-30 and is estimated to raise $4.5bn in its first year of operation.
The opposition leader, Angus Taylor, accused the government of cutting a “dirty deal” with the Greens in a “war against small businesses”.
‘Realities of the Senate’
The Greens secured another major win early on Wednesday after the government agreed to carve out pensioners from its bill to decrease private health insurance rebates for over-65s.
The legislation, which had received public condemnation, was thrown out of the Senate last month by the Greens, Coalition and crossbench.
Shoebridge said the changes would stop 1.2 million pensioners from being forced to pay more.
“Around 2 million insured older Australians lived on less than $37,000 a year. These cuts would have left people going hungry or missing other essentials with no plan to reinvest in healthcare,” Shoebridge said.
“At a time when the ultra-wealthy are making more and more, it was always wrong for Labor to even consider going after pensioners.”
Labor had promised the $3bn in savings from the budget measure would be spent on more aged care beds. The health minister, Mark Butler, said the government would fund the same amount, despite the carve out.
Butler acknowledged a deal was impossible without the major concession.
“The government also recognises the realities of the Senate, and reaching agreement was necessary to secure passage of the bill and deliver these important investments into aged care,” Butler said in a statement.
The digital duty of care passed on Wednesday afternoon with the Greens also backing it after negotiating for stronger penalties, including “multibillion-dollar” fines and allowing individuals to sue social media companies in court.
The week’s deals with the Greens to pass three pieces of legislation in the dying hours of the parliamentary year has left Labor with just one more hurdle – the gas reservation scheme.
The industry minister, Madeleine King, told News24 it was still the federal government’s intention to get the laws passed this week.
“Each senator will have to decide if they’re backing Aussie gas for Aussies,” King said.
