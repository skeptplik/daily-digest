Winter can be a challenging time, with bad weather, dark evenings, horrible bugs circulating, plus plenty of opportunities for overindulging. How can we avoid getting run down? Doctors share their tips for surviving the season, from saunas to screen blockers, light boxes to apples.
Go for a walk
Dr Ellen Fallows, GP and lifestyle medicine specialist, Northamptonshire
“I make sure I have my flu vaccine as early as I can. I had influenza B last year and it took me three months to recover. The vaccine has been shown to be more effective if you have a healthy gut microbiome, so I’ve been upping my intake of fermented foods.
“I avoid using too much ibuprofen and paracetamol if I’m ill. There is some suggestion that ibuprofen isn’t helpful to our immune system and in adults, we don’t always have to lower a fever; it may even be useful in clearing infections. I take 25 micrograms of vitamin D and omega-3 supplements but I am sceptical about other things. What I will have is small amounts of spices, such as a quarter teaspoon of turmeric, cinnamon and some ground cloves in some coconut or almond milk.
“For me, sweating it out at the gym hasn’t been helpful and makes me feel stressed. There is lots of evidence for light exercise in nature, so I’m doubling down on walking. This has been made easier by getting a dog, which has been associated with increased longevity.
“I have an enforced day of rest. I put my to-do list away on a Sunday, no matter how stressed that initially makes me feel. I will read a book, have a bath or do a bit of gardening. And I mean really resting, not doomscrolling, because it is surprising how stressful that is. I use a screen blocker: I didn’t think that I had a problem with my technology use, but it’s been remarkable how much it has helped my mood not to have a constant stream of bad news.
“I have as many saunas as possible. They’re not studied enough – you can’t have a placebo sauna – but it feels great and forces us to stop and be present.”
Be social
Dr Rebecca Lawrence, consultant psychiatrist, Edinburgh
“Winter is definitely something I struggle with as I have a diagnosis of bipolar disorder and I tend to get depressed during the autumn and winter. Fortunately, this year it’s so far, so good.
“Having a regular lifestyle is really important for me. I prioritise getting up at more or less the same time. I’ve tried seasonal affective disorder lamps and I didn’t find them very effective but I make sure I get outside in the morning and get at least 10 minutes of daylight.
“I am on medication for my diagnosis which makes it easy to put on weight. I have a static bike and do three sessions a week, and have started strength training with my daughter who is a personal trainer. I try not to eat too much bread and carbohydrates, although you can’t do everything correctly all the time: it is OK to have the occasional mince pie.
“I don’t drink too much alcohol, and I particularly try not to drink for stress relief – that is a slippery slope. I work as an alcohol and drugs consultant, so I am very aware of the kind of problems that people can run into.
“It is very easy to roll up in a duvet and not do anything in the winter, but it is important to have contact with people. I play violin in an orchestra, which is a really good way to get out and be sociable.”
Healthy bacteria
Prof Debbie Shawcross, hepatologist, London
“I make sure I eat an apple a day. Apples are rich in pectin, a type of dietary fibre that feeds the beneficial bacteria living in our gut. These bacteria ferment pectin into short-chain fatty acids, which help maintain a healthy gut lining and regulate our immune responses. A healthy, diverse gut microbiome is an important ally in helping our bodies fight infection. Pears, oranges, carrots, plums, cherries, apricots and peaches are also excellent sources of pectin, fibre and beneficial plant compounds.
“I always have an extra helping of brussels sprouts on Christmas Day. They are packed with fibre and plant compounds that support beneficial gut bacteria. They are also rich in vitamins, including vitamin C. Think of them as little green presents for your gut microbiome.
“It’s surprisingly easy to drink more than you realise over the festive period. Alcohol is a toxin, and no level of consumption is completely risk-free. I recommend, and always aim to have, at least two or three alcohol-free days each week, especially over the festive period, and aim to drink no more than 14 units of alcohol per week. If you do choose to drink, avoid binge drinking, pace yourself and have alcohol with food, which slows its absorption. I am particularly careful to avoid fizzy alcoholic drinks on an empty stomach, as they can be absorbed more rapidly.”
Stay active
Prof Scarlett McNally OBE, consultant orthopaedic surgeon, Sussex
“I quite like winter – it is a reminder that any gains we have made to get people active really are hard won. It can feel difficult to keep active when it is dark and slippery outside, however it is much easier to do something if it is a habit. This is why I cycle to work all year round. I have waterproof overtrousers, a fleece snood, snowboarding mittens and charged lights ready to go.
“Activity reduces the feeling of being miserable or cold – I was lead author for a report that lists evidence for 150 minutes per week of exercise reducing the risk of dementia, depression and some cancers by 30%.
“I don’t change what I eat – fruit, vegetables, protein and treats work all year round.”
Take vitamin D
Dr Ajay Verma, consultant gastroenterologist, Northamptonshire
“I’ve been on a bit of a health kick lately, so I’m trying to keep up the good habits. Even though it is dark and cold, I keep my exercise regime going by playing badminton and going to the gym – thankfully I can do this indoors.
“All adults should be taking a vitamin D supplement of 25-60 micrograms a day, from October to March. I choose to take it all year round, which is sensible if you have chronic health conditions, or if you don’t have fair skin.
“At Christmas we all overindulge. For every indulgent day, you should have a day of recovery where maybe you don’t drink alcohol and you don’t eat as much junk. I’m 48 – you can’t have too many Christmas parties once you get older, your body won’t let you do it. One of the things that we do now as a family is try to go for a walk on Christmas Day after lunch, to get some fresh air, but also to help with digestion.”
See the light
Dr Faye Begeti, neurologist, London
“Poor sleep is linked with higher susceptibility to infection, so protecting sleep is one of the best things you can do to avoid bugs. I struggle with my sleep/wake cycle. It is the brain that governs this, and if you commute in the dark, spend all day in a dimly lit room, then go home and have on bright lights and watch TV, your brain can get confused. So I try to have a break in the morning to get a coffee and go for a walk outside.
“I have a light box on my desk that I use when I have back-to-back clinics the whole day. There is good evidence that it can help to regulate our body clock. You need a light box that gives 10,000 lux, positioned about 40-60cm away and off to the side, rather than staring directly at it, and you should use it for at least half an hour.
“I’m not teetotal, but close to it. On special occasions, I’ll have a drink. With chocolates and sweets, I do relax at this time of year. In my job, I see people at some of the most difficult moments of their lives, so I’m very grateful to be at home with my husband and kids, allowing a bit of joy, not being overly strict, and building some memories around fun and food.
“I am strict about my phone use, especially in the evenings. Scrolling takes up a lot of time and can raise cortisol levels. I only go on Instagram twice a day.”
Breathe easy
Mr Edward Noon, consultant ENT surgeon, Manchester
“My preparations for winter begin in the summer. I make the most of being able to run outside and stock up on fresh fruits and have lots of salads.
“If my nose is blocked and it is affecting my sleep, then I will use a decongestant spray. It opens up the nasal passages, gives you that bit of relief for a few hours, and you’re not quite as tired as you would be. But it is really important not to use these for more than five days. I see a nose condition that is specific to overusing these decongestants, where there is significant swelling of the lining of the nose, which can cause irreversible changes. The end result is the opposite of the short term-effects – a severely blocked nose that is difficult to treat.”
Get vaccinated
Dr Philip Barber, pulmonologist, Manchester
“I gave up smoking 35 years ago. Smoking is still a mass killer, and increases your risk of getting a serious chest infection, and greatly prolongs the symptoms. Vaping is not a good idea either, unless it’s absolutely essential to prevent you smoking or to reduce your smoking consumption.
“I have vaccinations against Covid, flu and shingles. These – and the pneumococcal vaccination [for serious illnesses including pneumonia] that is offered to some patients – are very important to improve your resistance to bugs, especially in the winter time when they are much more common anyway.
“Most colds and chest infections don’t need immediate antibiotics, but they are helpful when the symptoms are prolonged by a secondary bacterial infection with, for example, discoloured nasal secretions or phlegm. If I get a bad chest I like to have a hot toddy at night – a bit of whisky, hot water, lemon and sugar – along with plenty of fluids. It is really important to rest and stay in bed if you feel weak and unwell, and take paracetamol if you have a painful throat or a temperature.”
Keep moisturised
Dr Ellie Rashid, consultant dermatologist, London
“With humidity levels much lower in the winter, it takes off a lot of the moisture from the surface of the skin. We also produce less oil in the winter. I’ve got a condition called ichthyosis vulgaris, which is dry skin on the lower legs and arms, and in the winter months it tends to be much worse, but can normally be treated with a rich moisturiser.
“Over winter I indulge a bit more, and foods are richer – I just accept that is what I enjoy eating when it’s cold outside. I tend to go for nourishing soups and stews, and have lots of hot drinks; in the evening I will always have a herbal drink or hot lemon and ginger.
“I mostly exercise inside and enjoy mat pilates but my husband and kids are obsessed with spending the entire day outdoors. Once I’m out there it feels wonderful, but I need that nudge to embrace the cold.”
Stay hydrated
Dr Tharusha Gunawardena, cardiologist, London
“Thirst drops off in the winter, so I am really conscious about hydrating, drinking peppermint tea, green tea, or honey and lemon.
“I go to the gym three times a week and try not to skip workouts, as tempting as that may be. My problem is willpower, so I have a routine and then it’s harder to skip. Even though it can be a struggle getting there, you never regret a workout.
“To try to stop myself from overindulging, I snack on things that will give me a dopamine release, such as dried fruit and low-calorie jelly, which scratch the itch for having something sweet without being too calorific. In terms of eating more, I think it’s OK to cut yourself some slack occasionally. It’s not all year round, and it is good to have these times to decompress and be sociable. You can’t eat like a monk all year.”
