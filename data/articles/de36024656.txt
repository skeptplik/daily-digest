Pentagon takes fresh aim at Canada's military readiness, defence spending priorities
Washington accuses Ottawa of putting economic interests ahead of security
The Pentagon has fired a further salvo at Canada, saying the country isn't meeting its international obligations to either NORAD or NATO and is prioritizing "parochial economic and commercial interests" in its defence build up.
The complaints are contained in a U.S. Department of War fact sheet covering U.S.-Canada defence relations, which was posted online Monday.
Axios, a U.S.-based news website, was first to report the contents on Sunday.
The fact sheet takes aim at the readiness of different branches of the Canadian military, saying the air force only has 41.9 per cent of its fleet ready for operations in the current budget year and that directly affects the country's ability to contribute to the air defence of North America.
It also cites low army and navy readiness figures.
"Canadian political leadership continues to pursue unfocused global commitments, resulting in personnel and platform overextension, deferred modernizations for key equipment and low readiness rates," said the fact sheet.
"Ottawa’s prioritization of parochial economic and commercial interests over military optimization introduces further uncertainty for NORAD and will further burden our Joint Force and the American taxpayer."
The fact sheet does not spell out precisely how Canada is not meeting its NATO commitments, but does point to the sorry state of the navy's submarine fleet, where only one of four boats is operational. It also mentions the ongoing review by the government of Prime Minister Mark Carney about whether to proceed with the full purchase of U.S.-made F-35 fighters.
The release of the document comes just two days after a report surfaced in the Washington Post that U.S. Secretary of War Pete Hegseth had issued a classified directive restricting military co-operation between the United States and Canada.
A leading U.S. senator, Mike Rounds, who sits on the armed services committee, told the Halifax International Security Forum that the restrictions were largely symbolic in the sense that they don't affect day-to-day military operations, intelligence sharing and defence planning.
"It does limit general flag officer meetings to those that advance [U.S. Department of War] objectives," he said.
U.S. defence officials — uniform and civilian — must seek permission before attending social events or think-tank conferences.
The memo follows the suspension last fall of meetings between the countries through the Permanent Joint Board on Defence (PJBD), a decades-old organization that met infrequently to co-ordinate North American security.
No one in Defence Minister David McGuinty's office was immediately available for comment.
The fact sheet said Canada "has yet to articulate a resourced path" to reach the NATO target of defence-related spending of five per cent of gross domestic product by 2035 "and is deferring the largest increases needed to actualize NATO targets until after the 2029 federal election."
The latest federal budget, released Nov. 5, contains spending plans going up to 2030-31. But absent was any clear sense on what the precise rearmament plans are for the Canadian military — beyond equipment that is already on order.
The fact sheet contained the Pentagon's advice: "Canada can maximize its limited but growing defence funds by prioritizing missions important for its homeland defence and meeting NORAD and NATO obligations by committing to the best military platforms."
Retired Canadian major-general Scott Clancy, speaking on CBC's Power & Politics late Monday, said there's an undeniable political and strategic tension between the United States and Canada that — for the moment — hasn't affected day-to-day co-operation.
"I think we're seeing a continuous deterioration of that relationship," Clancy said. "So, at the operational level, my friends are telling me the relationship is as good, if not better than before, but there are some cracks" that are making it increasingly difficult to maintain the business-as-usual approach.
