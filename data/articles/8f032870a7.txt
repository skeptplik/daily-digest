2 U.S. veterans, who both survived Pearl Harbor attack, die on same day
Gebhard Galle was 105. Freeman Johnson, the oldest known survivor, was 106
Two former U.S service members who were stationed at Pearl Harbor in Hawaii when Japan attacked in 1941 died on the same day last week, it has been learned.
Gebhard (Geb) Galle, died on Saturday at his home in Moses Lake, Wash., according to his daughter, Lynda Palmer. He was 105.
Galle died the same day as the oldest known Pearl Harbor survivor, Freeman Johnson, of Centerville, Mass., who was 106.
Galle and Johnson were among the estimated 87,000 military personnel on Oahu on Dec. 7, 1941, when Japan attacked — killing just over 2,400 Americans and drawing the U.S. into the Second World War.
The list of surviving veterans from the attack had stood at about a dozen names since the December 2025 death of Ira (Ike) Schab, who was 105.
The American Legion commended Galle and Johnson as "two members of a generation whose courage and sacrifice will forever be part of our nation's history," in a post on X.
Galle was serving aboard the battleship USS Nevada, while Freeman was with the cruiser USS St. Louis at the time of the attack, the veterans' organization noted.

Galle was born in Germany. He came to North Dakota with his family as a child and joined the U.S. navy in 1939 at age 18.
He was a machinist's mate aboard the Nevada, and was waiting below deck to be taken ashore for church services on Dec. 7, 1941. He looked out of a porthole and saw planes approaching.
A torpedo struck the ship, allowing water to enter, but officers were able to get it moving, the navy said. The ship was attacked again, causing more leaks in the hull and fires. The Nevada ran aground.
Galle could not see what was happening.
"Of course, we felt a couple jolts, and that's when we got hit with the torpedo, and we had a few bomb hits," he told the Columbia Basin Herald, a newspaper based in Moses Lake, in 2020.
"After we were beached and everything, and we got up to topside, then we saw all the mess that took place," including the capsized USS Oklahoma and the partially submerged USS Arizona, he said.
"A pretty sad lookout," Galle said.
Another near-death experience
Galle was reassigned to the USS Northampton. That ship came under fire on Nov. 30, 1942, by Japanese torpedoes during the Battle of Tassafaronga.
Galle told the Basin Herald he was ordered to leave a hatch closed behind four sailors who would've been trapped when the ship was sinking. The officer left, but Galle defied the order, opening the hatch. He closed and secured it after the sailors escaped.
"He heard those guys pounding on the roof of the hatch," Palmer said. "He went to open it up, and the senior officer walked by and said, 'No, leave it closed, because we don't want water to get in there because the ship might go down.'"
The Northampton sank, and Galle and other sailors spent more than a day adrift in life rafts before they were rescued.
His "courage under fire" in that incident, as well as his having fought in 19 of 20 Pacific naval engagements, was noted in a 2025 letter from then-acting Chief of Naval Operations Adm. James Kilby, marking Galle's 104th birthday.
"Your humility, quick thinking and selfless actions remain a shining example of Navy valour," Kilby wrote.
In December 2016, Galle attended a 75th anniversary service at Pearl Harbor to honour and commemorate the USS Nevada and those who served on it. A total of 76 sailors and marines lost their lives as a result of that attack, the navy said.
After the war, Galle got married and opened a refrigeration and air-conditioning business in Grafton, N.D. He then served as a civil engineer with the air force and then later worked for the Army Corps of Engineers. He retired in the 1980s.
Johnson's death was confirmed by one of his daughters, Diane Johnson.

Johnson was working on a boiler of the USS St. Louis when the Japanese surprise attack occurred. In a May 2026 interview with The Associated Press, Johnson said he didn't hear or see the attack. He never heard his shipmates firing anti-aircraft guns at the attacking planes.
"While all the rigamarole was going on topside, I was inside a steam drum. Couldn't see anything, absolutely nothing," said Johnson.

While Johnson didn't see the Pearl Harbor attack, he was witness to other historic moments during the war.
He helped commission the USS Iowa and recalled the battleship's preparations in November 1943 ahead of transporting President Franklin D. Roosevelt to the Tehran Conference with British Prime Minister Winston Churchill and Soviet leader Josef Stalin.
He also witnessed the war's end while aboard the Iowa. He was on its mast on Sept. 2, 1945, watching the surrender ceremonies about a mile away on the USS Missouri in Tokyo Bay.
"I could see the boats coming up with the marines escorting the Japanese onto the ship and sitting around a table," he said. "It was all over."
For all the attention to Pearl Harbor, the gruff Johnson, who was known for his cackling laugh and mischievous smile, didn't see it as one of the defining moments in his life.
Those would have been getting married after the war to his late wife and having three daughters. He worked for years in a machinist shop, then in a convenience store and, finally, delivering meals to seniors. He finally retired from all work at age 90.
