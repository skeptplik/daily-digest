A deep rift among nations on how to tackle climate change, and whether the global economy can be weaned off fossil fuels, was exposed after the spectacular collapse of talks at the COP31 climate summit in Antalya, Turkey.
After a tortuous fortnight of negotiations, COP31 ended in disarray late on 22 November after countries including the UK and the EU refused to agree to a political accord that failed to mention fossil fuels.
The Antalya pact would have seen countries officially recognise that the world is on course to exceed 1.5°C degrees of warming and promise to limit the scale of the overshoot as far as possible. It also would have committed nations to increasing the share of electricity in total final energy consumption to 35 per cent globally by 2035, a key target for climate campaigners who see electrification as the route away from fossil-fuel dependency.
But the draft agreement proved unacceptable to many countries, including the UK, the EU and some small island nations, after all mentions of fossil fuels were omitted at the behest of fossil fuel-dependent nations such as Saudi Arabia and Russia.
Failing to mention fossil fuels explicitly in the text would have marked a backwards step for the climate process, the EU argued. At COP28 in Dubai three years ago, nations agreed to “transition away from fossil fuels”, but since then, attempts to strengthen the language or clarify how this transition will take place have been blocked by petrostates.
Alden Meyer at the climate think tank E3G says the talks were subjected to a “brutish” strategy from fossil fuel-exporting states to “systematically block” any reference to the COP28 deal. “They clearly have buyer’s remorse about that agreement. They never were happy about it.”
The EU also took issue with a paragraph inserted at the behest of Saudi Arabia – which leads the 22-member Arab group of countries – calling for the rapid growth of carbon capture and storage technologies to the gigatonne scale by 2035. Carbon capture and storage technologies are expensive and unproven at scale, and many sceptics are concerned that focusing on them will justify the continued use of fossil fuels and distract from the deep emissions cuts necessary for controlling climate change.
Ireland’s climate minister Darragh O’ Brien, speaking on behalf of the EU, claimed the negotiations had been “hijacked by fossil fuel producers” and said the EU would “not accept a COP that greenwashes fossil fuels”.
Parties also vented their frustration at the hosts, Turkey and Australia, with the joint presidency leading to a sluggish and disjointed negotiating process in the final days of the summit. There was also disquiet from lower-income nations over the lack of new climate finance commitments from richer countries.
Fossil fuel-dependent countries have long sought to delay, obstruct and divert climate talks from making a dent in the worldwide consumption of oil, gas and coal. It was oil-exporting countries that insisted all UN climate decisions should be made by consensus when the COP process was established in 1992, a decision that has hamstrung subsequent meetings by effectively awarding veto power to every nation. Other tactics include delaying talks by raising objections and points of order during meetings and flooding the official agenda with additional discussion areas.
What was new at COP31 was seeing a group like the EU ditching a deal rather than finding a weak compromise in the name of progress, says Joanna Depledge at the University of Cambridge, a seasoned COP observer. “The usual political dynamics were completely upended,” she says of COP31’s endgame. “Usually the EU always backs down at the end. It doesn’t want to be seen as a blocker, but this time it played hardball.”
Many in the climate world are cheering the EU for standing its ground. Bill Hare, the CEO of the influential Climate Analytics think tank, said the deal was “clearly unacceptable”. “The lack of agreement on the main document here shouldn’t be seen as a failure of the COP process, but as a successful defence of the UNFCCC’s integrity against a coordinated attack by a few countries implacably opposed to action on climate change, in particular to any mention of the need to transition away from fossil fuels,” he said in a statement.
But the “no deal” outcome of COP31 is far from good news for those concerned about the world’s direction of travel on climate issues. The global consensus on action is fragmenting, even as climate impacts escalate and the world economy accelerates its embrace of renewable energy and electric technologies. Where does all this leave the COP process?
The debacle underscores the growing scepticism of so-called cover decisions at COPs. With nations at an impasse over even mentioning fossil fuels in any final text, it leaves many wondering what the point of a cover text is at all if it can’t ramp up the ambition signal sent to the rest of the world. Next year’s summit will be held in Addis Ababa, Ethiopia, and the hosts are highly unlikely to want to risk pushing for a cover decision.
With nations unlikely to agree on bold steps unanimously, the next-best thing is for large groups of countries to forge ahead with real-world change regardless. That means COPs will become more focused on the so-called action agenda, where groups of countries voluntarily band together to make pledges and set out roadmaps for adopting cleaner technologies. Yet whether that can be as powerful as all nations standing together under a singular message of ambition is doubtful.
But despite the disarray of the last fortnight, there are still reasons for hope. Meyer points out that progress was made in other areas of the COP, with 70 countries agreeing to accelerate the electrification of their economies, and countries including Brazil, Denmark and Spain presenting their roadmaps for removing fossil fuels from their economies. “In the real world, there’s a lot of progress,” he says.
The collapse of the negotiations might force better planning and strategy from nations going forward, he suggests. “I think, in a strange kind of way, the breakdown in the negotiations may serve as a wake-up call to the vast majority of countries that want to see progress on climate change,” he says.
There is also a clear signal from the wider economy that change is already under way. Renewables continue to grow faster than analyst predictions at every turn, while electric vehicles are hitting adoption tipping points in growing numbers of countries. The Iran conflict has underscored to many governments the security benefits of home-grown power over fossil fuel imports, with solar and nuclear energy the biggest beneficiaries, the International Energy Agency reported last week. Decarbonisation isn’t happening anywhere near fast enough, but the direction of travel is unmistakable.
UN climate summits have been teetering for many years, with the appearance of consensus maintained thanks to delicately constructed phrases and back-room horse trading. COP31 has brought the disputes and distrust out into the open in a spectacular fashion. Perhaps, out of the flames of this summit, a more honest and realistic approach to climate diplomacy can emerge.
