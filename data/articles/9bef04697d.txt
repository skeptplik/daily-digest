Colombian security forces stormed a supermarket in the Caribbean city of Cartagena early Wednesday, ending a day-long hostage crisis.
President Abelardo de la Espriella said all hostages had been rescued and one of the hostage-takers had been killed.
"In an extraordinary joint operation between the National Police and the Armed Forces, all the hostages were rescued," de la Espriella said.
Around 30 people had initially been taken captive at a branch of the Exito supermarket chain in Cartagena's La Castellana neighborhood.
Ten of them, including some elderly women, had already been released on Tuesday after hours of negotiations.
No further details were immediately available about whether any of the hostages had been injured.
How did the Cartagena hostage crisis unfold?
According to authorities, the hostage crisis followed a failed attempt to rob an armored vehicle transporting cash from a shopping mall.
Police, troops and special forces were deployed to the supermarket as authorities sought to secure the release of the hostages.
Images circulating on social media appeared to show people kneeling inside the supermarket with their hands bound. The images could not be independently verified.
Supermarket employees were among those believed to have been taken hostage.
"My son is there," Jacob Perez, the father of one of the workers, told news agency AFP during the standoff.
"He didn't leave work and he's not answering his phone. I think they took it from him," he said.
The supermarket chain said it was following the guidance of the authorities.
"For Grupo Exito, protecting the people's lives and safety is our priority," the company said in a statement.
The incident also temporarily disrupted air traffic in Cartagena, a Caribbean port known for its colonial walled old town, beach resorts and cruise ship tourism.
Arrivals at the city's Rafael Nunez airport were suspended for around 45 minutes in connection with the security operation.
What did Colombia's president say about the hostage crisis?
De la Espriella traveled to Cartagena on Tuesday, where he met commanders from a specialist anti-kidnapping and anti-extortion unit.
The president, who appeared in military fatigues, had previously expressed confidence that the captives would be freed.
"This is going to turn out well for the hostages," he said before arriving in Cartagena. "I'm not so sure it's going to turn out so well for the criminals."
De la Espriella ruled out the involvement of guerrilla or paramilitary groups in the incident.
Local media reported that the hostage-takers had made financial demands and requested safe passage to Barranquilla, another city on Colombia's Caribbean coast. Those reports could not be independently verified.
What is the security situation in Colombia?
Security has deteriorated across much of Colombia despite the tough rhetoric of its right-wing president, who took office in August.
On Monday, a car bombing in the central Colombian city of Villavicencio wounded 25 people and caused extensive damage.
The government blamed the attack on a faction of the former Revolutionary Armed Forces of Colombia (FARC) rebels that rejects a 2016 peace agreement.
De la Espriella had visited Villavicencio following the bombing before traveling to Cartagena.
According to a Defense Ministry document shared with AFP, armed groups carried out 358 drone attacks between January and November — an average of more than one attack per day.
