McKinsey estimates that about 3.7 million jobs are directly at risk in Europe, as almost half of the region's industrial companies were under financial stress or in distress in 2025. Early 2026 data suggest the decline is speeding up, but McKinsey says the problems can be fixed.
Around 3.7 million jobs at European industrial companies are directly at risk from financial stress, according to a new report by management consultancy McKinsey & Company.
So is more than €500 billion in value added, a measure of the companies' contribution to economic output.
In 2025, about 45% of the industrial firms in the study were classed as stressed or distressed, up from 39% in 2023 and 42% in 2024. Preliminary figures for 2026 suggest that share is rising even faster.
The report said that industrial companies in Europe are a backbone of the region’s economy, accounting for about 36% of value added, 29% of jobs, and more than 45% of research and development.
McKinsey nonetheless describes the companies' problems as "structurally fixable".
The consultancy's Industrial Financial Health Index covers almost 1,200 listed and privately held manufacturers in Europe, each with more than €100 million in revenue.
Its five financial warning signs include a sharp drop in earnings and high debt relative to earnings. The 2025 scores are based on full-year figures from financial data provider S&P Capital IQ.
The worst-hit countries
Portugal fared worst among the countries ranked in the report. It had the highest share of industrial firms under stress or in distress in 2025, ahead of Luxembourg, the Netherlands and Spain.
In Germany, the EU's largest economy, the share rose by 12 percentage points last year.
For instance, German carmaker Volkswagen is going through what it has called its deepest restructuring in 89 years.
Higher labour and energy costs have eroded European industry's past advantages, particularly against China, the consultancy says.
McKinsey's automotive R&D analysis puts labour productivity in China at 1.5 times Europe's level. Industrial electricity prices in Europe averaged more than 1.5 times those in China and the US.
These gaps put more than half of the EU industrial value chain's costs at a disadvantage against China, the report found.
Europe is also losing ground on gross productive investment, which McKinsey defines as spending on assets such as infrastructure, non-residential buildings, machinery, software and R&D.
China's investment in these assets has surged over three decades to about $6 trillion (€5.5tn) in 2025, according to the report, while Europe's spending has hardly moved and stood at around $3 trillion (€2.7tn).
Former European Central Bank President Mario Draghi's 2024 report on EU competitiveness called for an extra €750 billion to €800 billion of investment a year to keep pace with China and the US.
Six ways to turn industrial firms around 'the European way'
McKinsey, which advises companies on restructuring, studied five recent turnarounds in the sector. In one, a chemicals conglomerate generated more than €1 billion of free cash flow, the cash left after operating costs and investment.
The report's six success factors range from running the overhaul through a dedicated task force to selling or closing weak business units and using AI to lighten administrative work.
On labour, McKinsey says European rules allow companies to cut salary or benefit costs and extend working hours for the same pay. They can also reduce headcount through hiring freezes, natural staff turnover and, as a last resort, separation agreements.
At carmaker Mercedes-Benz, which has put new investment at its German plants on hold, one proposal already under discussion would extend working time without extra pay.
Three of the five turnaround companies cut labour costs by more than 15% within 12 to 18 months, without major protests or strikes. The report partly credits the involvement of supervisory boards on which workers hold seats, and cooperative talks with labour representatives.
The consultancy warns that Europe's industrial comeback "needs to start soon", even though some obstacles, including high energy costs, hinge on geopolitics and other external factors.
