'Licence to kill': French parliament adopts police shootings law slammed by activists
French senators gave final approval on Friday to controversial legislation granting police officers a presumption of legitimate self-defence when using firearms, fulfilling a long-standing demand of the far right even as weeks of student-led protests have thrust the issue of police brutality back into the spotlight. Left-wing lawmakers have vowed to challenge the bill before France's Constitutional Council.
France's parliament on Friday granted further protection to law enforcement officers using their weapons, after alleged police brutality was thrust back into the spotlight in student-led protests in recent weeks.
Backers of the new bill – including the government and the party of far-right presidential candidate Marine Le Pen – say the law is needed to avoid officers being automatically suspected of using disproportionate force.
Activists, however, argue it would amount to a "licence to kill", as it would hamper the opening of investigations and make holding security forces accountable even harder in a country where alleged police brutality cases rarely reach a criminal court.
The right-leaning Senate approved the text 205 votes to 110, after the lower house did so in July.
The text stipulates that "when they use their weapons", police officers "are presumed to have acted" within the framework of the law.
Interior Minister Laurent Nuñez on Thursday said it would "put an end to the automatic practice of taking into custody a police officer or gendarme who has used their weapon".
He said it would "correct this reflex of systematically calling into question the daily, highly demanding work of our security forces".
But France's Human Rights League has argued it will "block the opening of an investigation in the event of a police shooting following a failure to comply" with a traffic stop "or in the context of crowd control" during protests.
The left has pledged to appeal to the Constitutional Council, the country's highest constitutional authority, to try to halt the bill from coming into law.
Young men "perceived as Arab, Black or from North Africa" are four times more likely to be stopped than the rest of the population, according to a report last year by France's rights chief.
