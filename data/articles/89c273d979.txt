Fashion has a reality problem. Celebrities can be seen wearing clothes they’ve never touched, luxury houses may appear to release products they’ve never designed, and, in some cases, even clothes that actually exist don’t quite look like themselves online.
Earlier this month, the embattled Nvidia co-founder and now reinstated CEO Jensen Huang became an unlikely menswear sensation when the Instagram account @hidden.ny posted images of him wearing an archival, silver-studded Saint Laurent leather jacket from Hedi Slimane’s tenure. The post came just a day after Nvidia opened an investigation into Huang’s alleged affair with a suspected Chinese spy. “Nvidia CEO Jensen Huang in Hedi Slimane SLP Studded Leather Jacket after admitting to affair with alleged Chinese spy,” read the caption.
The internet rushed to crown Huang a Hedi boy, but he’d never worn the jacket. As CNN first revealed, the images were fake; Vanity Fair subsequently verified they’d been generated or altered using AI. The originals, in fact, showed Huang wearing Dior at an airport in Seoul in June. The post was subsequently taken down, but the fantasy had already traveled.
Days later, designers including Reese Cooper and Gerrit Jacob called out luxury retailer Ssense over product imagery that misrepresented their clothes, from distorted construction details to inaccurate branding. Unlike Huang’s fictional fashion moment, these were real garments available to buy.
Cooper, founder of Reese Cooper and RC Outdoor Supply, discovered the inaccuracies while browsing the website himself. He had not been consulted about the imagery, which depicted his brand’s signature trademarked hook at twice its actual size.
“There’s a lot of sewing and design detail inaccuracies but the important issue is misrepresenting my trademarks — our hook is a major part of our branding, and of course all of the logos,” Cooper says. “The initial reaction is disappointment and frustration. It takes an overwhelming amount of time and resources to protect your brand legally with these trademarks, not to mention the work going into designing the clothes themselves.”
The errors were subtler than AI signifiers of the past, like extra fingers or missing limbs. But when shopping online, details matter. Fashion commentator and journalist Odunayo Ojo identified missing design details and Gerrit Jacob trousers with an invented waistband, additional seams and altered lettering.
“AI pulls everything toward an average, but design lives in the details that depart from that average,” Ojo says. “Generative models are built to make something that looks plausible, not something that’s precise. Plausible is fine for a moodboard. On a product page, plausible is misrepresentation.”
In a statement to Vogue Business, a spokesperson for Ssense acknowledged the errors: “Accurate representation of our brand partners and their products is fundamental to Ssense. We recognize that some recent e-commerce imagery did not meet that standard, resulting in inaccuracies in product details. We take full accountability and have taken steps to resolve this issue with urgency.”
It comes after local media reported over the summer that Ssense had laid off its full-time photography and retouching teams as it invested in AI Virtual Try-On (VTON) in August to support content production and has since strengthened quality controls. The spokesperson for Ssense confirmed to Vogue Business that “in preparation for the transition to Virtual Try-On (VTON), Ssense made organizational changes in June that affected certain roles within its visual content department.” Ssense did not explicitly confirm that the AI technology caused the inaccuracies last week.
Instagram content
Together, the incidents exposed two sides of fashion’s growing synthetic image problem: AI can make an imaginary garment look real, and a real garment look imaginary, with both able to put brands in a difficult spot.
The Huang images were far from an isolated incident. In April, AI-generated images showed celebrities including Megan Fox and Robert Pattinson wearing fictional Balenciaga designs made from cardboard shipping boxes, while another fake imagined the Olsen twins in a Louis Vuitton campaign.
Nor was Ssense the only case of AI imagery creating confusion around real products. In May, after Swatch and Audemars Piguet teased a collaboration, AI-generated images of a colorful Royal Oak wristwatch circulated online. The actual release was a collection of Bioceramic pocket watches, disappointing fans who had come to expect a product that never existed.
While the average person has struggled to distinguish many AI-generated images from real photographs for a couple of years, experts say things seem worse now because it’s become so much easier to produce them. “It’s now at the point where it’s really easy to create highly realistic content with minimal knowledge of how to optimize for the tool you’re using, and there’s plenty of tools to choose from,” says Henry Ajder, a synthetic media expert and founder of Latent Space Advisory, pointing to their integration into popular chatbots and the growing availability of open-source alternatives.
Fashion is particularly exposed. “With fashion imagery, because there’s so much highly stylized, high-quality content associated with the industry, there’s been a huge amount of training data for these tools to utilize,” Ajder says, arguing that we’ve reached a tipping point. In 2026, unofficial campaigns can now look practically indistinguishable from a house’s own advertising, even as brands experiment with AI themselves.
The cost of getting it wrong
For Cooper, the Ssense episode has created a commercial dilemma. “Simply put, it’s brand damage,” he says. “RC Outdoor Supply is a new company and all we have is our trademarks and reputation of design aesthetic and quality. To have these things put in question by the customer, it really erodes trust for the brand as a whole.”
He points out that retailers judge brands on sell-through, and inaccurate listings can deter purchases or generate returns, while removing them means losing sales altogether. “Ssense has removed the images, which I’m thankful for. However, it also removed the ability for anyone to purchase those items, which will result in low sales when looking at this season’s data,” Cooper says.
Experts say the problem is particularly acute in luxury, where small design decisions and fit often underpin a garment’s value. “In luxury, the details are the price,” Ojo says. “If the image erases them, you’ve erased the reason the garment costs what it costs — the problem isn’t AI as a tool, it’s using it for the one job where accuracy is the whole point.”
Wholesale contracts, meanwhile, haven’t caught up. “When a retailer’s AI imagery misrepresents a garment, the designer absorbs the returns and the reputational harm, and most of the wholesale agreements I review do not address AI-generated product imagery,” says Shermin Lakha, founder and managing attorney at Lvlup Legal.
For luxury brands, the legal questions become more complicated when AI-generated images of their products circulate online without anything being sold, as in the Huang case. Lakha argues that luxury brands have fewer remedies against these non-commercial viral fakes than many might assume. “Existing law is effective against commercial actors and leaves a gap for the viral fake that nobody monetizes,” she says. Susan Scafidi, founder of the Fashion Law Institute at Fordham Law School, agrees legal options are limited. “There is no single, universal cause of action that a brand can invoke to target unauthorized imagery that includes its products, whether that imagery is created by AI or not,” she says. Trademark and false advertising claims may apply where recognizable products or logos are involved; other claims may arise from reputational harm.
The calculation changes if an account uses a brand’s identity to sell services, promote counterfeit goods or generate revenue. The Balenciaga cardboard box images, for example, were associated with an account promoting a course on creating viral AI visuals. But even when brands have grounds to challenge unauthorized imagery, the distinction between infringement and creative expression is rarely straightforward. “Luxury brands have for decades chafed against the imprecise nature of parody and other forms of ‘fair use’ under US law,” says Scafidi.
Can luxury reclaim control?
Daniel Shapiro, a brand protection executive at Red Points, which helps brands detect counterfeits and online impersonation, says traditional monitoring tools that look for logos are increasingly inadequate. Newer systems analyze entire images for unreleased colorways, inaccurate hardware or recognizable patterns, even when logos have been removed.
But policing the internet is only part of the answer. Scafidi urges brands to distinguish harmful images from passing memes to decide whether to take action. When necessary, brands can work with platforms on removals and respond through their own channels. Lakha also recommends tighter contracts and internal verification before brands share viral images.
On brand and retailer sites, Ojo wants human review against physical garments, designer approval and disclosure of AI alterations. “If what’s on the page isn’t a photograph of the real garment, they [a designer] should say so,” he says. “A small [AI] label costs nothing. Finding out later is what causes scandals and also costs Ssense the customer,” he says.
The deeper challenge may be cultural. Luxury has traditionally relied on controlling its image, from campaign casting to the placement of a logo. AI makes that harder, while increasing the value of visual credibility. Lizzie Wheeler, founder of archival bridal salon Studio Dorothy and the resale expert behind the Instagram account @shit.u.should.buy, sees consumers becoming more discerning and brands returning to human craft as a ‘realness’ signal.
“Aside from the legal issues, luxury is moving back toward more organic, human work (like the hand-drawn Prada holiday campaign) and showing AI shortcuts will dissuade a luxury client,” she says. “Ssense does rely heavily on the luxury client and I imagine this type of error will impact them seriously,” she adds.
The irony is that fashion’s most elaborate fakes have often been powered by some of its most recognizable signatures: a Balenciaga provocation, an archival Saint Laurent jacket, an instantly identifiable luxury watch. As these signatures become easier to imitate, the ability to prove what’s real is becoming a more valuable part of luxury itself.
“The consumer is becoming increasingly educated,” Wheeler says. “So I’d advise all retailers against pulling wool over the customer’s eyes.”
