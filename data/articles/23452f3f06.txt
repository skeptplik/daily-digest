Former French prime minister Dominique de Villepin announces presidential bid
France’s former conservative prime minister Dominique de Villepin, who served as former president Jacques Chirac’s foreign minister during the country’s steadfast opposition to the US-led invasion of Iraq in 2003, formally launched his presidential campaign Friday. But while De Villepin’s opposition to Israel’s Gaza campaign and assured analysis of international affairs has caught the eyes of younger voters, it remains to be seen if he can persuade them to cast a ballot for him.
France’s former prime minister Dominique de Villepin added his name to the already crowded list of candidates running for president in 2027, setting himself against what he described as the country’s “extremes” as well as the competing heirs to President Emmanuel Macron’s centre-right political project.
De Villepin announced his campaign at Paris’s Maison de l’Amerique Latine, founded by General Charles de Gaulle after World War II in a bid to strengthen ties between the French and Latin American republics.
The candidate positioned himself to an alternative to “three forces relentlessly trying to cut France apart with a scalpel”, his team told AFP.
According to the former conservative prime minister, those forces are the far-right National Rally – currently leading the polls – as well as the left-wing runner-up France Unbowed and the “Macronist centre”. He accused the three groups of being responsible for France’s “decay”.
De Villepin called on voters not to resign themselves to “a death foretold” in the 2027 presidential election, instead putting himself forward as an incarnation of “renewal”.

De Villepin’s candidacy has been more than two years in the making, and the former premier has not been shy about his intention to run in recent months.
He created his own political party, “Humanist France”, published a book, built up his social media presence and travelled across the country on different speaking engagements.
Whether or not he will actually be able to stand as candidate is still uncertain. Candidates must gather at least 500 signatures from France’s mayors in order to run for office. His team maintains that De Villepin has already secured pledges from 300.
It was his failure to gather these much-needed signatures that blocked the former prime minister from running for president in 2012. Despite having stalked the halls of power since the 1980s as one of right-wing president Jacques Chirac’s chief strategists, De Villepin has never actually been elected to public office.
But De Villepin, who served as Chirac’s foreign minister during France’s steadfast opposition to US-led invasion of Iraq in 2003, has built something of a following on the left as well as the right for his assured analyses of international affairs.
De Villepin’s opposition to Israel’s brutal campaign in the Gaza Strip has brought him to the attention of an electorate that would once have scorned the right-wing former prime minister – younger, and more left-leaning.
Whether or not they can actually be convinced to vote for him is another question entirely. Current polling puts De Villepin at between one to three percent.
