Flying in the face of Albert Einstein’s theory of gravity, quantum physics could turn the famous force repulsive instead of attractive – and we could soon test the idea in the lab.
A hallmark of gravity as we know it is that it pulls objects with mass together: gravity explains why the moon orbits Earth, and why an apple falls to the ground after detaching from the tree. While this is well established, there is still no agreement on how gravity works at scales dominated by quantum effects, and what quantum properties it may have.
Now, two research teams have independently found that quantum effects can make gravity act repulsively, pushing objects apart instead of pulling them together.
“Gravity is always attractive – this has been the cornerstone of gravity from Newton to Einstein,” says Pablo Saldanha at the Federal University of Minas Gerais in Brazil, who worked on one of the studies with Vlatko Vedral and Chiara Marletto, both at the University of Oxford. “Anti-gravity or repulsion: no one has seen that before,” says Sougato Bose at University College London, who co-authored the other study with Lev Vaidman at Tel Aviv University in Israel.
A key concept for both studies was that of a quantum superposition state. Consider a car driving down a city street. When it arrives at an intersection where one road forks into two, it can take either the left or the right branch. But a quantum car would have another option: to enter a superposition of taking the left road and also the right one, assuming a state where it is impossible to tell where the car is exactly without disturbing it.
Another quantum object, called a “probe”, moving along a path parallel to both the left and right road branches, would be gravitationally attracted to the car. In both new studies, the researchers mathematically modelled an analogous setup and used equations to show that this gravitational interaction would make the car and the probe inextricably connected through the link of quantum entanglement.
The two teams mathematically examined a scenario equivalent to the two roads – left and right – merging some time after they had split apart. At this point, a measurement taken by the probe can reveal how each of the paths shaped the superposed car’s quantum state.
Quantum mechanics is probabilistic. This means that if this experiment is repeated many times, the final state of the car won’t always be the same, but will follow some distribution of states instead, reflecting its capacity to follow a combination of the left and right branches. The researchers in both experiments found that if all but some specific states were then discarded after the measurement – a technique called post-selection, partly pioneered by Vaidman – then the probe should theoretically experience gravitational repulsion and be pushed away from the car in the experiment. “That repulsion, that going away [from the superposed object], is created at the time of the measurement,” says Bose.
Even Einstein’s theory of general relativity, which has for over a century proven to be an incredibly accurate description of gravity, doesn’t predict this kind of gravitational repulsion. “There is a measurement that will somehow conditionally give you repulsive gravitational force,” says Vedral. Confirmation that such a repulsive force occurs would demonstrate the quantum nature of gravity.
Because this measurement effect is inherently quantum, just like the possibility of a superposition state is, the scenario the two teams explored doesn’t have any non-quantum analogues. But it could still be tested in experiments.
Objects that can stay in a superposition state for the duration of an experiment would have to be very small, says Bose, and yet also still massive enough for gravity to make a difference to their behaviour. Bacterium-sized objects should do the trick. He and Vaidman calculated that the probe object at this scale would experience acceleration roughly smaller than a quadrillionth of gravitational acceleration on Earth. Vedral says that he and his colleagues are already working with experimentalists who specialise in unusually large quantum objects.
Tomasz Paterek at the University of Gdańsk in Poland says that the new idea is “brilliant”. Past studies explored making the force between electrons attractive, even though it is usually repulsive, through post-selection. But this is the first time that the same process has been shown to potentially reverse gravity’s character too, he says. In Paterek’s view, lab experiments that use gravity to quantum entangle objects could be achievable in the near future – but he cautions they will be really difficult to perform.
