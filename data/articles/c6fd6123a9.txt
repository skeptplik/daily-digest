An FDA milestone for limb girdle muscular dystrophy
Yesterday the FDA approved the first drug to treat limb girdle muscular dystrophy, a broad collection of progressive muscle conditions that has long received scant attention from physicians and drug developers. The drug, developed by BridgeBio, is specifically approved for patients with a subtype of the disease that the company estimates affects 2,000 to 3,000 patients in the U.S. and 4,000 to 5,000 in Europe.
“I’m pretty excited about it,” Nicholas Johnson, an LGMD specialist at Virginia Commonwealth University, told STAT’s Jason Mast. “It does appear that they had actually surprisingly robust effects on clinical outcome measures… and it also looks like it’s incredibly safe, so that’s a win-win.” Read more on the approval.
Why Barbara Kingsolver stopped signing books
The Pulitzer Prize-winning novelist Barbara Kingsolver was learning to play hand drums when her fingers started curling in on themselves. She was diagnosed with a genetic condition called Dupuytren contracture, in which the tissue of a person’s palm thickens and tightens, pulling on the fingers.
While the condition is genetic, doctors believe that the gene needs to be turned on in some way — either through age or perhaps a trauma. The drums may have been Kingsolver’s trigger, according to her hand surgeon at the University of Virginia, Bobby Chhabra.
The condition quickly began to impact her work. As a sort of celebrity author, Kingsolver’s book signings are no small feat. “The way I have to do it is sign 60,000 books — that’s an actual figure,” Kingsolver said at a media briefing with UVA on Monday. “It involves hours every day of clenching a pen. And every time I did that, I’d have to go in for another surgery.”
Kingsolver has stopped signing books en masse, but she bristles when people recommend speech-to-text technology for her writing. “This is the way I write, and I need hands,” she said. “As long as I’ve got them, and as long as I can keep them, I’m going to keep writing books.”
4.9 million
In June 2026, that’s how many menopause hormone therapy medications were dispensed to cisgender women in the U.S. between the ages of 40 and 74. It’s a major increase — 141% — from the roughly 2.1 million meds dispensed monthly between July 2023 and February 2024. That’s according to an analysis of national data published last week in JAMA.
The study found that dispensing rose before and after the FDA’s removal of the black box warning in November 2025. Menopause and its precursor, perimenopause, received a flood of attention in those years, reflected in a still-booming business.
AI offers hope in the Last Frontier
Stroke is the fourth-leading cause of death in the U.S., the majority caused by occlusions that block blood flow to the brain. Survival rates are significantly worse for rural patients, and in Alaska, the challenges sound almost insurmountable.
There are five neurointerventionalists in the entire state who can extract clots. Sometimes Alaskans arrive at their village clinics by snow machine or dogsled. Every minute is precious in the aftermath of a stroke, but it can take several hours to arrange a plane, airlift a patient to the airport in Anchorage, and deliver them by ambulance to one of those specialists.
“The distances that we have to cross are enormous. The weather that we have to face is awful. Resource availability is so low,” said Kelly Isaacs, the stroke program manager at Providence Alaska Medical Center in Anchorage.
That’s why, in 2023, a small network of hospitals agreed to use the same AI software to triage stroke patients and get them to the right care faster. STAT’s Katie Palmer reported on how it’s going and what the future of stroke care looks like in Alaska.
The pros and cons of routine PSA screening
A systematic review published yesterday in Annals of Internal Medicine provides important insight into the cost-benefit profile of routine PSA screening. According to the findings from nearly 800,000 men in 13 randomized trials with follow-up for about two decades, routine screening every two to four years between the ages of 55 and 69 led to fewer cases of metastatic disease and prostate cancer deaths. However, increased screening was also linked with an increased risk of false positives (about 150 per 1,000) as well as overdiagnosis (about 20 cases per 1,000).
The review is especially timely. Earlier this month, the American Cancer Society updated its prostate cancer screening guidelines, recommending that men at average risk be tested every two years for prostate-specific antigen (PSA) levels in the blood. Routine PSA screening had gone out of favor since 2010 specifically because of the problems identified in the review, though new evidence linking it to reduced disease-specific mortality led to reconsidering the approach. — Annalisa Merelli
‘I worry how easily we can grow used to being alone’
Second-year medical student Brian Zhang is what he calls a “class-goer,” meaning he, and most of his classmates, show up physically to class each day. But in a new First Opinion essay, he notes that preclinical lecture attendance among medical schools has been declining nationally since the pandemic.
“Hard workers are remarkable at hiding stress. Intelligent people are good at forgetting to be kind to themselves,” Zhang writes. “Class gives us a place, an excuse, not only to struggle through challenging coursework in real time together, but be part of one another’s lives.” Read more.
