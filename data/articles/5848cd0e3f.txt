Natural England objected to the building of a large AI datacentre near Bristol weeks before the government threatened to abolish the independent environmental adviser.
The nature adviser made a significant intervention to urge Bristol city council not to grant planning permission for a 40MW datacentre at Avonmouth, because of the threat to protected wildlife and habitats at one of the UK’s most important sites for migratory birds, which is within 600 metres of the site.
The Environment Agency – which is also threatened with abolition by the de facto deputy prime minister, Louise Haigh – objected in addition to the flooding risk at the site, which is on a high-risk flood plain, expressing concerns people would not be able to take safe refuge in the event of a deluge.
Haigh this week blamed quangos, including Natural England, for blocking development and indicated both bodies could be scrapped with their responsibilities handed to ministers or devolved. Ministers are pushing for the construction of AI datacentres across the country as key to growing the UK economy.
Hundreds of AI datacentres are planned, with more than 300 waiting in the queue to connect to the electricity grid, representing 73GW of demand – almost double the peak winter energy demand for the entire UK. But they face considerable environmental and sustainability issues over their high energy use, pollution, encroachment on protected habitats and emissions from fossil fuel power stations many want to install while waiting for grid connections.
To side-step local democracy, the government is allowing datacentre developers to use the nationally significant infrastructure projects (NSIP) regime, with the secretary of state acting as the decision maker.
But Natural England has expressed concerns to ministers about the rollout of datacentres, urging caution and a precautionary approach.
In a response to the government’s draft datacentre national policy statement, Natural England said AI datacentres were a relatively new development type whose full environmental impacts were still being understood.
It highlighted the potential impact on rare and vulnerable species from overabstraction from waterways to meet datacentres’ demands.
Government sources denied the objection to the datacentre at Avonmouth or Natural England’s stance on taking a precautionary approach to their widespread construction had influenced Haigh’s announcement of their possible abolition.
But Craig Bennett, the chief executive of the Wildlife Trusts, said the government’s threatened abolition of Natural England was a worrying sign of an attack on environmental regulation. “Regulatory arm’s-length bodies like Natural England are set up to implement the law of parliament,” he said. “If we want decisions made about nature on the basis of science rather than the very last private corporate or vested interest that a minister has spoken to, then we have independent arm’s-length bodies like Natural England.”
Earlier this month Natural England advised Bristol city council that the datacentre could cause “disturbance and displacement” to rare and vulnerable bird species within the Severn estuary special protection area, just 600 metres away. Another protected site, Hallen Marsh, was only 270 metres away but had not been accounted for by the developers when assessing noise pollution, Natural England said.
As well as the flood risk, the Environment Agency said the developers had not provided any information on the level of water use, and the risk of pollution from land contamination to controlled waters. More than 4,000 people have objected to the datacentre’s construction.
Bristol council said the proposal was “likely to give rise to significant environmental effects” and it wanted the developers to carry out a full environmental impact assessment.
Mary Stevens, an AI campaigner at Friends of the Earth, said: “Natural England was doing its job brilliantly here as an independent adviser. I would have no confidence if this was a government minister doing the work because they would not be acting independently to protect nature.”
Rivington Energy, which is developing the datacentre, said: “We acknowledge the feedback received from the Environment Agency and Natural England and welcome the opportunity to continue working constructively with all consultees throughout the planning process.
“While substantial environmental and technical assessment work has already been undertaken, additional studies are being prepared in response to comments received and will be submitted as part of the ongoing planning process.
“We … are now preparing a detailed environmental statement to support the next stage of project assessment.”
The Wildlife Trusts have launched a petition to save Natural England, saying the current body and its predecessors have been vital protectors for wildlife, land, water and sea across England since the 1940s.
A government spokesperson said: “Datacentres are essential to our economy, but their development must protect the environment and benefit local communities. Developers must meet planning requirements, with local authorities assessing environmental impacts, and then deciding on local applications.”
