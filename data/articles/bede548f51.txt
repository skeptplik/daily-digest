Meta employees have expressed concern at the company’s push to replace human moderators with artificial intelligence, claiming the technology is leading to mistakes with the potential for sweeping political consequences.
The social media company, which owns Facebook and Instagram, has been experimenting with AI-driven moderation for years, first introducing it in 2017 to filter out violent extremist content.
This March, Meta announced it was “transforming our approach” by rolling out “more advanced AI systems” to moderate content across its apps, framing the change as a way to improve enforcement.
Several Meta employees said that behind the scenes this push to AI was accompanied by mass layoffs across the company, severely affecting staff responsible for reviewing content on its platforms.
One described Meta’s content moderation as now operating with “skeleton teams”, some cut by at times half or two-thirds of their staff.
“It’s been really a mess,” they said, adding that on several recent occasions they had gone to discuss an issue with a specific content moderation team, only to find that entire team was now gone.
Meta has long sought inexpensive ways to moderate the vast volumes of content posted each day to its platforms. It has faced legal action from former outsourced content moderators in Kenya, who described struggling with PTSD and severe mental health issues after being paid as little as $1.50 (£1.13) an hour to view and evaluate extremely graphic content that users attempted to post.
Meta employees say AI systems that the company is now using struggle with nuance, especially in languages other than English, and frequently remove content that should not be taken down.
“You are trying to gut all human reviewers, which are kind of essential for making these nuanced decisions and policy decisions,” said one employee. “Of course there are going to be mistakes that are going to happen.”
The employee said that because algorithms were automated, and worked at greater speed than humans, these mistakes could happen at a greater scale. The employee said Meta was removing lower-paid contractors, such as those in Kenya, and the full-time employees who would handle more complex content moderation.
Once a post is taken down, there is far less recourse for the affected accounts than before. “Even if you know someone at Meta, it’s still very difficult to get through to someone who’s actually going to be able to help you, especially if it’s a highly nuanced case,” the employee said. “It’s so much more difficult now.”
The growth in AI moderation comes as researchers monitoring online censorship have noticed a new phenomenon: sudden restrictions on Instagram and Facebook accounts, often during pivotal political moments.
Some of these have been isolated occurrences, such as Meta’s suspension of the US No Kings account in the run-up to the US midterm elections. Others have amounted to “mass takedowns”, unfolding over the course of days and hitting dozens of social media accounts.
It is a growing phenomenon, the consequences of which could “shape the political future of entire nations”, said Martha Dimitratou, the founder of the group Repro Uncensored, which monitors digital censorship.
Repro Uncensored has tracked these takedowns in Brazil, the EU, Turkey and the US. The Guardian reported on a wave of account takedowns on Meta platforms late last year, affecting accounts posting queer content and those dedicated to reproductive health across Europe. Accounts backing the Brazilian president, Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva, were restricted or suspended from Instagram in the feverish days of Brazil’s election runoff, according to Repro Uncensored. Meta said most of the Brazilian takedowns were to comply with valid court orders or electoral regulations.
The numbers of affected accounts appear to be rising: Repro Uncensored documented 210 incidents of account takedowns on Meta in 2025, and 657 in the first six months of 2026.
Meta employees who spoke to the Guardian said AI moderation had a role in such incidents as algorithms worked faster, and with less nuance, than human reviewers. “The effect of it is you see these sweeping takedowns,” one said.
But they also said they could not rule out political considerations in how Meta’s algorithm was designed. Meta’s systems are opaque, even to its employees. They are a combination of LLMs – large language models, like chatbots – and older algorithms that filter content.
There are “thousands of levers and factors that weigh into how things are ranked”, said one employee, and these change constantly.
The employees suggested that at times Meta’s use of AI had allowed the company to obscure more controversial decisions it makes in how it moderates political content. They gave the example of how Meta made adjustments to its algorithm in late 2023 that drastically limited the number of posts that appeared from people in Palestine.
The company rejected allegations of bias and said it had previously carried out independent human rights due diligence in relation to the conflict, and since 2022 had been working to implement the resultant recommendations, including by “introducing a system that detects and prioritises content to moderators most likely to understand that particular Arabic dialect”.
One employee said AI offered “plausible deniability”. It afforded “exactly the kind of ambiguity that they hide behind”, they said.
In response to questions from the Guardian, a Meta spokesperson said: “Everyone on our platforms is subject to the same set of rules, and any claims of enforcement based on political affiliation or advocacy are baseless.
“While we have long used AI to help enforce our community standards, earlier this year we began experimenting with more advanced AI systems that can more quickly and accurately catch violations with fewer mistakes. The latest tests show that on average these systems make 13% fewer mistakes than humans when enforcing against violating content, while finding 10% more actual violations – and we give people opportunity to appeal decisions if they think we’ve got it wrong.”
