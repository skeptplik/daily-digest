We got two very different pictures of Nvidia this week, depending on whether you were focused on the company’s latest earnings report or the tech executives loudly calling for former CEO Jensen Huang’s return.
On the latest episode of TechCrunch’s Equity podcast, Kirsten Korosec, Tim De Chant, and I discussed the latest news from the AI chipmaker, which reported soaring revenue and insisted that it has no plans for Huang to return.
Kirsten described this as “the tale of two Nvidias this week,” with the company’s current executives assuring investors, “Hey, we’ve got this.” At the same time, “You’ve got Elon Musk and others out there really pushing to bring Jensen back, circulating a petition, Jensen himself saying that he’s prepared to come back.” (The company’s falling stock price certainly suggests that Wall Street isn’t feeling reassured.)
Keep reading for a preview of our conversation, edited for length and clarity.
Kirsten Korosec: Nvidia will no longer sell its H200 chips in China. This was coming directly from the interim CEO Colette Kress, and that coincides with the Trump administration also talking about closing off what we have discussed in the past, which is this idea of remote access — really closing and tightening all these restrictions [so] the chips can’t make it into China.
What was happening is that data centers were being set up right outside, in countries like Malaysia and elsewhere, and then companies within China were remote accessing these chips, and so that’s now also been shut down.
A lot is happening on the China front that is so much bigger than Nvidia, but Nvidia came out publicly and said, “Nope, we’re shutting this part down.” I’ll be very curious to see if this changes, maybe in the first half of 2027.
Tim De Chant: I’d love to be a fly on the wall in some of these Trump administration conversations, because they were much more stringent in terms of export controls at the end of the first Trump administration, and that continued through the Biden administration, where they really restricted Nvidia’s ability to export chips to China. I think that something changed in the last couple of years that really opened Nvidia’s ability to sell in the country.
Anthony Ha: One of the things that really surprised me as we were preparing for this episode was [learning that critics] of Nvidia’s stance towards China estimate that 20% of their business comes from China.
And again, that’s not something you’re going to see directly in their financials, because that’s not direct sales to Chinese companies. But they’re saying, “A lot of the chips that are getting sold to companies in Malaysia or other companies in East Asia or Southeast Asia are going to end up [used by companies in] China eventually.” And so that could be — if Nvidia is really serious about this, I think this is something that’s very much in flux — that could actually be a pretty significant blow to their bottom line.
Kirsten: Although Nvidia’s bottom line is doing just fine. In every possible way, their guidance was incredible. They beat expectations. They have a long pipeline.
It was really interesting because you have that happening, and then you have all this noise happening in social media, and also a number of other tech conferences were happening this past week — Dealbook Summit, there [were] a few others in which you had the highest profile people that you can think of in the tech world commenting on Jensen [Huang], and Jensen himself commenting.
So it’s like the tale of two Nvidias this week. You’ve got the earnings and the current executives really talking about, “Hey, we’ve got this.” In fact, that was the last line that Collette Kress said [on the earnings call]. And then at the exact same time, or in parallel, you’ve got Elon Musk and others out there really pushing to bring Jensen back, circulating a petition, Jensen himself saying that he’s prepared to come back. So it’s really quite the drama that we are definitely not done with, by the way.
Anthony: Although I think that what the folks who’d like to see Jensen Huang come back would say is that this most recent quarter is the last quarter under him. This is the [last of the] good times that we’re seeing under him. And so we don’t know how well Nvidia will do without him.
It seems like Nvidia’s public stance right now [on Huang’s possible return] is, “No, that’s not happening.” But Nvidia’s public stance a week and a half before that was, “We stand behind Jensen. We have every confidence in his leadership.”
So depending on the various pressures on them, you can imagine that changing. I don’t know what it would take for that to happen. I don’t necessarily think it’s that likely, but I also don’t think that just because they’re saying it’s not happening now doesn’t mean it will never happen.
Kirsten: Sure, never say never.
Tim: Right, never say never. I do think that to some extent, their steadfastness and how quickly they shot down the petitions asking for Jensen to return suggests there’s maybe a little bit more going on behind the scenes than we know on the outside. So that’s probably something to watch in the coming weeks.
Kirsten: I think that the board is trying to be in control here, and they don’t want to be forced into a decision because someone like Elon Musk is saying that it needs to happen. And so, even if there is a day in which Jensen returns — which I think is pretty unlikely, especially because the investigation into how much and who knew what about chips being sold, allegedly, secretly to China. We don’t know the full picture of that yet. But even if they did have the full picture, they want to present control, and they definitely don’t want to appear like they have deference to some other tech billionaire out there.
Tim: I’m sure they’re looking at what happened at OpenAI and not wanting to repeat that.
Anthony: I imagine the dynamic’s a little bit different, since Nvidia is a public company. So, I’m not an expert on this, but I would guess it would be much harder for you Jensen Huang to come back and just toss out a bunch of board members.
