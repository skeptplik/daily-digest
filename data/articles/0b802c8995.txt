Allies are buying Canadian underwater surveillance drones. Canada is 'not there yet'
NATO allies racing to protect critical underwater cables from sabotage
Canada has a three-ocean, 243,000-kilometre coastline and about 20 underwater drones to help defend it.
Defence officials talked last year about the need for a short-term fix to assert Canada's sovereignty, according to an internal document obtained by CBC News.
But the Royal Canadian Navy's commander, Vice-Admiral Dan Charlebois, confirms there isn't an interim solution ready.
"We're still working very closely with industry to get there. We're just not there yet," said Charlebois in an interview with CBC News.
The navy wanted to see if Canadian underwater drones could help address surveillance gaps along the "sparsely" monitored coast until its first uncrewed underwater surveillance system is ready, the paper obtained through an access-to-information request said.
The navy's current small fleet of American underwater drones is equipped with tech built by Canadian company Kraken Robotics. But the navy uses those drones for mine hunting and testing, not surveillance.
Kraken, based in Newfoundland and Labrador, and Cellula Robotics in B.C. are viewed as world leaders in underwater drone technology and are already selling their systems to allies around the globe for surveillance. But not yet to Canada.
Other countries are racing to monitor and protect critical underwater cables from potential acts of sabotage by adversaries.
A massive network of these communication cables runs along ocean floors, carrying more than 95 per cent of the world's internet traffic and trillions in financial transactions. Damage could cripple societies.
Hundreds of submarine cables run along ocean floors around the world. These fiber-optic cables have allegedly been targeted by adversaries. (Kevin Kirk/CBC)
University of Calgary defence expert Rob Huebert said Canada has "very significant vulnerabilities" because it can't fully detect when adversaries are entering Canadian waters.
Canada has long relied, Huebert said, on co-operation with the U.S. Navy as a safety net and allowed its fleets, submarines and satellites to age and deteriorate.
"The backstop that I think Canadian governments have always counted on was of course, if we were slow, if we were late on any of these surveillance capabilities off our coastline, well the Americans would take care of it," Huebert said.
He called that a "policy of hope."
The navy is undergoing its biggest peacetime fleet renewal since the Second World War. But some replacements aren't coming until the 2030s and 2040s.
Ottawa's in the initial procurement phase for a $250-million to $499-million underwater surveillance system that uses drones and sensors to detect, monitor and gather intelligence about threats. The system is expected to be used in Canadian waters, including the Arctic, and during deployments abroad.
"We expect to take delivery of some large autonomous vehicles in the early 2030s," Charlebois said. "That is just around the corner when you're talking about delivering a very advanced large-scale system."
The Royal Canadian Navy's commander, Vice-Admiral Dan Charlebois, says the navy is testing out drone technology. (Mathieu Theriault/CBC)
The government wants the system fully delivered by 2043. But it isn't clear yet if foreign companies will be able to bid on the project.
When asked about the obstacles to finding an interim solution, Charlebois said the procurement system "can be challenging," but he's seen an appetite from Ottawa to cut down on red tape.
"The products that the navy needs exist here in Canada," he said. "We just have to enable ourselves to actually procure them and procure them at pace."
Prime Minister Mark Carney promised to speed up procurement through the Defence Investment Agency. He's also vowed to massively scale up drone production.
Canada has 2,000 drones or so in its inventory, Carney said earlier this fall. But he said the country will need millions in the years to come.
Industry Minister Mélanie Joly said the government is creating a roster of preapproved companies for sole-source contracting. The navy says this will open up "new opportunities" to work with the industry on underwater drones.
Allies using tech to monitor underwater cables
Off the coast of Halifax, Dave Shea, the executive vice-president of strategy and growth at Kraken Robotics, said one of the biggest challenges his company has faced in Canada is the pace of procurement. But he is excited the government wants to speed it up.
His company outfitted the navy's small fleet of drones with synthetic aperture sonar systems.
Those sonar systems, Shea said, scan the sea floor for objects of interest and take high-resolution images. Kraken's technology can also listen for any submarines or boats operating in the area, he said.
The Kraken Robotics underwater drone Katfish is designed to be towed by a boat. It carries a synthetic aperture sonar system that can take high-resolution images of the seabed. The Royal Danish Navy, the Polish Navy and Royal Australian Navy use this technology. (David Laughlin/CBC)
In recent years, Shea said allies have pivoted to use their technology to monitor if underwater cables or pipelines have been tampered with.
Canada could too, he said, but would need a much bigger fleet and longer-range drones because the coastline is so vast.
"There's a lot of technologies that are being built and delivered by companies like Kraken … that could be deployed directly here in Canada to help understand what's actually happening, who's in our waterways, who's in our sovereign waters and what are the risks to be able to protect Canadian capabilities," Shea said.
Orders headed to Europe, U.S., Asia
Cellula Robotics CEO Neil Manning says his company has sold its long-range drone to five NATO allies, but not Canada.
Systems are on order and will be heading to Europe, the U.S. and Asia, he said.
Cellula Robotics says its Guardian drones can carry out long-endurance missions. (Submitted by Cellula Robotics)
Cellula uses hydrogen fuel cell technology, Manning said, that's been adapted from NASA's space launches to work in the water.
The company's long-range drones can travel up to 5,000 kilometres or stay out at sea for up to 45 days, he said.
"I'm highly confident that we will see domestic spend as well," Manning said. "But at the moment we're still in that cycle of building trust with with our local governments."
'We need to get further ahead'
Charlebois is aware critical infrastructure is being challenged elsewhere in the world and said he's trying to get ahead of the problem.
"This is why we're working more closely with Canadian industry than I've seen in my 34 years in the navy because we know we need to get further ahead in this space than we are," he said.
The navy's fleet diving unit put an underwater drone in the water to practice looking for mines in 2021 off the coast of Halifax. (Master Cpl. Anthony Laviolette/Canadian Armed Forces)
Canada contributes to underwater surveillance capabilities with the U.S., Charlebois said, that deliver a "really high level" of awareness, but he wants to do more and is prioritizing sensors.
Asked why Canada is lagging behind protecting underwater infrastructure, Defence Minister David McGuinty said the government is working on it.
"I say that we're rebuilding all of our capacity underwater, on land, in the air and connected to satellites," McGuinty said. "We're building a combined layered approach to being able to understand and identify what might be coming at Canada."
The navy said it has a layered network of capabilities to protect this infrastructure, but noted the government, owners and allies are also responsible.
