Though David Fincher’s fans had been eagerly anticipating The Further Mis-Adventures of Cliff Booth, early screenings of the Once Upon a Time in Hollywood sequel starring Brad Pitt have raised questions about whether generative AI was used in the film. People have claimed that the movie is chock-full of uncanny, inconsistent visuals that make it feel more like slop than the sort of polished film you’d expect from the director of sleek productions like Fight Club and The Social Network.
David Fincher is being dragged for suspected use of AI in his latest film
Early screenings of David Fincher’s latest film are raising questions about whether the director used generative AI.
Following a number of Cliff Booth critics screenings that took place last week ahead of the film’s premiere on November 25th, moviegoers rushed to social media to lament what they saw. One of the most vocal reactions came from YouTuber / director Patrick Tomasso, who said on X that he walked out of his screening feeling “completely gutted” and that the film featured “some of the most blatant and egregious AI use” he has ever seen from a major studio. Tomasso also insisted you can see AI-generated footage in Cliff Booth’s trailers that have been posted online, and he was confident that Fincher’s latest film is filled with “slop as you know and loathe it. The shit you can pump out of Higgsfield.”
Others echoed Tomasso’s sentiments, writing that “the amount of AI in the CLIFF BOOTH movie is astounding to an absurd degree” and vowing to “never watch another Fincher, Pitt or Tarintino [sic] movie again.” Yet some critics enjoyed the film and suggested that the bad AI people thought they were seeing might have actually just been bad CGI. Netflix has neither confirmed nor denied whether AI was used in any capacity to make the film, but Netflix’s heavy involvement in the film’s production and the company’s recent investments in generative AI technology left many suspicious.
It did not help that David Fincher, in a recent interview with the Los Angeles Times, responded to questions about his thoughts on AI with “I’m not a Luddite, so I don’t fear the unknown.” Fincher also reasoned that the anti-AI crowd is advocating for artists to stop iterating on their creative processes: “Is everyone satisfied that we have figured this art form out enough that what we really need to do is go back to celluloid? How long have people painted? 40,000 years? We’re talking about a thing that’s a hundred years old and everyone’s going, ‘Stop. Pencils down. No more iterating. It’s not necessary to have at your fingertips the ability to change the color of that person’s eyes.’ Well, it exists. Should we not use it?” The spiel made it sound like he might be jumping on the AI bandwagon.
Set about a decade after Once Upon a Time in Hollywood, Cliff Booth picks up in 1977 at a time when Booth (Pitt) has left his life as a stuntman behind to become a fixer who quietly deals with scandalous problems for Hollywood elites. Cliff still works a number of side gigs as a projectionist and bartender. But the movie is primarily focused on his budding romance with critic-turned-talent agent Karen Crosby (Elizabeth Debicki) and his working relationship with Charlie Peters (Scott Caan), a TV actor who needs Booth’s services to keep his wife (Carla Gugino) from being murdered.
At least one trailer for the film features some baffling shots of what looks like people sitting backward in a horse-drawn carriage that make it seem like there might be some generative AI in the mix. It’s hard to tell from the trailers whether that’s actually the case, but in a moment when many creators have been trying to pass off AI generations as human-made art, it’s not surprising that people are suspicious.
Netflix has been incorporating AI into its movies and series for some time now, and it seemed like the company was doubling down earlier this year when it acquired Ben Affleck’s startup, InterPositive, for around $600 million. Rather than turning text into visual outputs like many commercially available AI tools, InterPositive’s AI models are designed to generate assets based on footage captured during a film’s production. According to Netflix, InterPositive’s AI was developed with the intention of making it easier to tweak details like lighting or to remove unwanted background elements. The company also made a big deal of emphasizing that InterPositive’s models were highly customizable and well suited to replicating a human director’s signature style.
Netflix buying InterPositive and absorbing the company’s development team is part of why there has been so much speculation about AI being used in Cliff Booth. If Cliff Booth actually uses AI and the visuals really are as bad as some have said, it would be a major knock against both Netflix and Fincher — a director known for his meticulous (obsessive, some might argue) attention to detail.
