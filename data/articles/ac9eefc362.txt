A prime minister candidate needs 176 votes for an absolute majority in the first vote. What are the chances of a coalition with the conservative Partido Popular?
Spaniards are getting ready to cast their ballots on Sunday following months of social and political unrest.
The campaign for this snap general election, called by prime minister Pedro Sánchez in July, sees centre-right Partido Popular (People's Party) ahead in the polls, with Eurosceptic party Vox in third.
The conservatives are unlikely to secure enough seats to govern alone, and that could make Vox's support decisive. In 2023, the PP won 137 seats and Vox 33, only 6 seats short of an absolute majority.
The party is pushing for stricter migration rules, national preference in access to certain benefits, and a rollback of some environmental and social policies.
What conditions could Vox lay out to join a coalition with PP?
“Vox is going to obtain lots of electoral support" tells Euronews Pedro Riera Sagrera, Professor of political science at University Carlos III, Madrid.
"And as a consequence, I think that they are going to be strong enough to basically force the formation of a coalition government between the two of them."
Vox's influence will depend not just on how many seats it wins, but on how other parties vote or abstain.
If the PP needs Vox to take power, the far-right leader Santiago Abascal could use that leverage to demand concessions.
Joining the government, with cabinet positions, or staying outside it while supporting PP leader Alberto Núñez Feijóo in parliament, seems the likeliest scenario.
A deal could include policy commitments, particularly on immigration, one of Vox's central campaign issues.
Could a deal also include regional alliances?
Feijóo has made it clear that he prefers to govern alone, with parliamentary support from Vox only when needed.
In a November interview with national media, he described his goal as securing support agreements with the far-right party.
But he has not ruled out a coalition which might also include regional agreements as an alternative to cabinet positions.
Vox has already secured vice-presidencies and ministerial portfolios in several Spanish regions, though some political experts cast doubts about the stability these agreements might provide.
If the two parties fall short of a majority, other political groups could vote against Feijóo or negotiate support for an alternative candidate.
Abstentions could also prove decisive in the second investiture vote. And if no candidate wins Congress's confidence within two months of the first vote, another election must be called.
Housing crisis: Is it the most important issue of this election?
Vox has vowed to tackle Spain's unsustainable housing shortage and to give Spanish nationals priority access to social housing.
According to a poll by the Centre for Sociological Research (CIS), housing emerged as the most important issue in the country, ahead of migration and the economy.
Many European countries are struggling with housing, but, in Spain, the situation has become critical.
The Bank of Spain says that over the past 45 years, the financial effort needed to buy a house has grown by 89%, compared to only 10% in the euro area.
In other words, while house prices and incomes have increased more or less in tandem across much of Europe, in Spain the property tag has grown way bigger than salaries.
What's driving Spain's housing crisis and how to fix it?
Mostly, a massive housing shortage that’s been building up since the 2008 financial crisis.
The country has accumulated a housing supply gap of around 750,000 homes, putting enormous pressure on the market and inflating prices.
And that’s certainly not because Spain’s population is booming.
Lack of housing workers, delayed licences and planning, but also building restrictions enforced by local authorities are the main culprits, according to José García-Montalvo, Economics Professor at Barcelona's UPF.
Cutting red tape isn't the only solution. According to Ramon Bastida, Director of the Sustainable Housing Department at UPF, there are also practical ways to speed up the process.
For example, off-site construction, which means pre-producing parts of buildings in factories and assembling them on site.
But the housing crisis is also affecting renters. Spain's fees grew by 44% in the past five years, on average, with dramatic increases in places like Malaga (+86%), or Madrid (nearly 60%), according to property platform Idealista.
No wonder most young Spaniards don't leave their parental home until they turn 30, according to Eurostat, the last to fly the nest in the EU, only behind young Greeks (31), Croatians (31.5) and Slovaks (31).
