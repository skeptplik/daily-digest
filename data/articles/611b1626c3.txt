When 85-year-old William Murphy became seriously ill at his home in September, his family expected help to arrive quickly.
Instead, they waited more than 12 hours for an ambulance, despite a new initiative - the Release to Rescue scheme - designed to free up crews to respond to calls more quickly.
"Dad was deteriorating... he couldn't walk," his son Ronnie recalled. By the time William reached hospital, he needed oxygen as his levels were severely low.
William's experience was not an isolated case in Northern Ireland, where patients facing lengthy waits for an ambulance are not uncommon.
The Release to Rescue initiative was introduced this year to ensure a maximum two-hour wait for patients stuck in ambulances outside busy emergency departments - therefore allowing paramedics to get back on the road to help other people, like William.
But more than 2,000 ambulance handovers exceeded the two-hour limit between May and August, the first four full months of the scheme.
The Department of Health said the scheme had delivered "improvements", with more than 6,000 waits of two hours or more in the same period last year.
The Murphy family, from County Down, were told over the phone that the ambulance service was "under pressure" and that a crew would come as soon as possible, according to Ronnie.
"I was concerned - the length of time it was taking and Mum was ringing but they were just saying still the same message," he told BBC News NI.
"Whenever he got into hospital he had to go onto oxygen and his oxygen levels were severely low.
"Had there been another emergency, had my dad taken more seriously ill, where would we have been?"
The Northern Ireland Ambulance Service (NIAS) has apologised to William for the "delay experienced following his 999 call".
"The demand for our services exceeds our capacity to respond in as timely a manner as we would like," it said.
The Release to Rescue protocol had "delivered some benefits", it said - but it accepted that "more focus is required to reduce the turnaround times even further".
Speaking to BBC Radio Ulster's Evening Extra programme, Health Minister Robbie Butler apologised to William.
"I very much regret and apologise to Mr Murphy for any failure on my part, or our part," Butler said.
"It's deeply regrettable and I know that having spent time in the control centre for Northern Ireland ambulance that the staff are absolutely dedicated and committed to ensuring that we have our resources in the right place at the right time."
'Increased demand on hospitals'
Ronnie was unhappy that his father spent almost two days in the Ulster Hospital's emergency department before he was admitted to a ward - but added that he had no complaints about the care he received.
"The floor, the waiting area in the new A&E - every seat was filled," he said.
"There were people sitting on the floors; there were people all sitting everywhere; there were people crying, families in distress."
The South Eastern Health Trust said it was "sorry to hear of the experience" Ronnie and his family described.
The health minister said there have been "growing pressures over a number of years", but added that "Release to Rescue in the greater part has been very successful".
He said the programme "still faces challenges", adding that as we face into winter pressures those challenges "ramp up".
"I think it's really important that the work that has been embarked upon, and there are multiple layers to that work, continues," Butler said.
"As minister I will ensure that there is a focus on those pressures at the moment because those are the imminent pressures today and the minutes and hours of delay obviously is not something we want to see happening."
Dr Michael Perry, from the Royal College of Emergency Medicine, said a protocol limiting ambulance handovers to two hours should not be required.
"If we had a flowing system where patients moved in a timely fashion [through the hospital system], where there was space in emergency departments, ambulances would offload as soon as they arrive," said Perry.
"We're now coming into the colder winter months, where we do get increased demand on our emergency departments because of normal seasonal winter illnesses.
"And we're expecting that pressure to get even higher again on us.
"To meet the requirement to offload ambulances in that timely fashion, it's going to be very challenging for us."
Perry told Good Morning Ulster that "real political focus" to come up with a long term political strategy was needed.
"It's going to be very, very difficult and it's not going to be a quick fix," he said.
"[We] can't go through this not agreeing a budget one year cycle, [it] needs a long-term strategy plan to fix this, it's the only way we can do it."
Norman Cunningham, a Unite union representative for the NIAS, said resolving patient flow problems in hospitals would be difficult.
"There will never be a quick fix with health because demand, particularly for the ambulance service, goes up 3% to 5% a year," said Cunningham.
"The problem for us is not so much the waiting outside hospital, which is disastrous for an ambulance service, but it's the people waiting at home who are seriously ill, who are relying on an ambulance getting to them, but it can't get to them."
'Significant success so far'
The Department of Health has asked all six health trusts, including the ambulance service, to make savings of up to 12% over three years.
According to a NIAS savings plan published in August, Â£5.2m of cuts to its 2026-27 budget are "dependent on the successful continuation" of Release to Rescue.
The document warns that if other trusts "withdraw or reduce the operational measures supporting Release to Rescue" as part of their own savings plans, "there will be a direct impact on NIAS capacity, response performance and expenditure".
The Department of Health said that "severe pressures" on emergency departments created "additional challenges to implement the Release to Rescue protocol".
But it said that the protocol had "shown significant success so far", with a reduction in ambulance response and handover times across the health service.
The South Eastern Health Trust said it "intermittently paused" the protocol at the Ulster Hospital on 1 September after an "exceptionally busy" bank holiday Monday, with 23 ambulances breaching the two-hour limit.
That was the only time so far that a health trust has paused the policy.
The trust said it had made "significant progress" in improving ambulance handover times, with 90% "turned around in under two hours" in August.
The Southern Health Trust said there had been no decision to pause the protocol at its emergency departments - but it "may on occasion be breached due to day-to-day service pressures."
The Northern Health Trust said it had "never stepped down the protocol", while the Western Health Trust and the Belfast Health Trust both said they had not paused it since its introduction.
