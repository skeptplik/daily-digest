It sounds idyllic. Who wouldn’t want to occupy a newly built residence in the Florida Keys, complete with its own coral garden right outside the window? The only complication is that it is 17 metres below the surface of the Atlantic Ocean.
That is not putting off Jim Brittsan, though. “This is really exciting,” he says. Brittsan is the founder and executive director of coral restoration non-profit Sustainable Ocean and Reefs (SOAR). He is also the diver who arranged the temporary coral nursery beside the residence, having brought the corals from a site where, 10 metres closer to the surface, they faced increasing environmental stress. Now, in reduced temperatures, they are starting to flourish, hosting their own marine life – visible day and night, in all its busyness, from the underwater residence known as Vanguard just 15 metres away.
Vanguard is a long-stay underwater station for marine biologists. It was deployed in June 2026 at Tennessee Reef on the seafloor of the Florida Keys National Marine Sanctuary, around 8 kilometres south of Long Key. Its coral nursery looks like a strange marine shrubbery: the coral fragments are arranged on anchored ropes that stand upright in the water. Once Vanguard is operational, a more permanent nursery will be established and the corals will eventually be planted on the seabed as part of ongoing restoration efforts, with their long-term survival assessed through monitoring.
Resident scientists will be able to monitor marine life around the clock
DEEP, the international design and engineering organisation that built Vanguard, aims to make humans aquatic by establishing more subsea habitats like it. These will allow people to live underwater for extended periods to engage in scientific research and conservation. This opens up unprecedented opportunities to do research because there is no need for divers to bounce up and down to a research vessel on the surface. Those round trips eat into valuable work hours, because divers can spend only limited time at depth and must ascend slowly to avoid decompression sickness. “If we’re diving from a boat, we can do about 3 hours’ work a day at depth,” Brittsan says. “It’s much more efficient if we can just stay down there.”
There is plenty of work to do in the Florida Keys. Elkhorn (Acropora palmata) and staghorn (Acropora cervicornis) corals, which are vital reef-building species, are now considered functionally extinct in Florida. But it is not just in Florida waters that corals are struggling. Climate change, which brings elevated ocean temperatures, is a significant threat to corals around the world. In warmer waters, corals eject the algae that live in their tissues. This upends their mutually beneficial symbiosis in what is known as a bleaching event – without the algae and other symbionts, the corals are left a ghostly white. If temperatures do not fall and the algae do not return, the corals eventually die.
In September 2026, amid concerns about marine heat stress, SOAR received authorisation to relocate 11 different coral species to the new nursery next to Vanguard. The corals were transported using different methods depending on the species: acroporid corals were transferred submerged in bins of seawater, while other corals were moved encased in seawater-soaked bubble wrap and placed in temperature-controlled coolers. The corals were then placed in the temporary nursery in deeper water near Vanguard.
Vanguard should be fully commissioned early in 2027. After that, those transplanted corals, and their attending creatures, can be continuously monitored by aquanauts who will be living in the habitat for a week at a time.
Coral space
Brittsan and his team have also observed juvenile long-spined sea urchins (Diadema antillarum) that naturally recruited to Vanguard. “A lot of Diadema have started settling all over the habitat,” he says. This species of sea urchin is considered a keystone because its grazing reduces algal cover on reefs, providing space for corals and other reef organisms to settle and grow. That is why DEEP is taking a holistic approach, also planning to build sponge nurseries outside the habitat, for instance. “You need corals, sponges, sea urchins and everything else,” says Thomas Potts, science program director at DEEP.
Diadema urchins are particularly active at night, which means short daytime dives would miss a lot of what needs monitoring. “That’s where Vanguard really comes into play,” Potts says. Resident scientists will be able to monitor marine life around the clock.
The lessons from Vanguard will inform marine research in the Florida Keys for years to come. Vanguard is also a proving ground for DEEP’s future habitats. “It’s a great place to train scientists that might be working in different habitats on the continental shelf,” says Potts. The longer-term vision is to build a fleet of undersea residences at other locations. “This could be replicated throughout the Florida Keys and even in other parts of the world,” Brittsan says. “It’ll be a game-changer.”

If you want to get to know a place, go and live there
Living among the species you are studying can provide deep insights, according to marine scientist Dr Steve Gittings. “We all know that if you really want to get to know a place, you should go and live there,” he says.
Gittings spent 27 years as chief scientist at the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration Office of National Marine Sanctuaries. In that time, he spent many weeks living on Aquarius Reef Base, an ocean-floor habitat used for vital marine surveys. One of the biggest advantages of living underwater, he says, is that it creates a deeper appreciation of the wide variety of phenomena that contribute to a healthy reef. “There’s millions of things going on that are super-critical.”
But it is not just a useful path to deeper insights: it is a transformative experience. An extended stay in the ocean changes people in the same way that astronauts are changed after living in space. “There’s an emotional connection to living underwater that keeps you dedicated and committed,” Gittings says. That is why he is so excited about the inspiration that Vanguard will give to the aquanauts who live there: they will gain an irrepressible, lasting desire to help nature heal itself by removing stressors where possible, he reckons. “Only nature can do the full restoration,” Gittings says. “Our role is to create the conditions that allow nature to do its thing.”
