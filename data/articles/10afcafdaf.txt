In a world where garment makers are forced to wear AI-training headgear and T-shirts can be ordered from Kmart on Uber Eats, it may seem as if the days of procuring something made by hand from an artisan are numbered.
While jobs in Australian textile, clothing and footwear manufacturing have been in freefall since their 1989 peak – from 115,000 to approximately 30,000 in 2026 – the work of a dressmaker or shoemaker who stitches every piece by hand is even harder to find.
But in certain studios and fitting rooms around the country, tape measures are wielded, fabric is pinched and pinned and the warmth of service extends well beyond holding out an Eftpos machine.
For hand-makers, the work is about more than just another product or a paycheck. The beauty of their vocation is in many small acts: mastering technique, appreciating texture, building a community of clients, and drawing on the mysterious well of creative intuition that allows humans to make wonderful things.
Andrew McDonald, A McDonald Shoemaker
Andrew McDonald has been making shoes for 30 years. But no matter how many times he’s measured a client’s foot, produced a wooden last, selected materials, and cut and stitched the leather, he still worries about the end product.
“We’re always trying to solve problems for the client along the way,” he says. “I’m always thinking, is this good enough?”
The veteran shoemaker studied at Sydney Tafe before leaving to apprentice under Paul Harnden near Aberdeen in Scotland and with the legacy boot manufacturer John Lobb in London. Now, he works out of the Strand Arcade in Sydney’s CBD, employs an apprentice and runs a work experience program.
He produces a range of ready-to-wear leather shoes, alongside a bespoke service that involves a 30-minute consultation and a 16-week manufacturing period that includes three to four fittings. The ready-to-wear shoes cost between $1,000 and $1,200, while custom-made pairs start at $3,600 and range up to $5,000 (returning customers receive a discount of $1,000 to $1,200 to reflect the repeated parts of the process).
Given the cost and the lead time, he’s aware that what he does “is not for everybody”. McDonald is catering to a specific type of person, who is looking to buy into more than just a glossy brand.
“The thing about handmade is, it’s very personalised and at the centre … is the relationship you have with the maker,” he says. “Over the years, we get to see that person a lot, and we get to know who they are.”
Repairability and product longevity are at the heart of this. Although he has many clients who have worn his shoes for years, recently a customer asked to have a pair from the 1990s repaired. McDonald felt it was time to let them go, but the customer had a sentimental attachment and insisted. “I mean, he’s a nut,” says McDonald. “But it goes to show, my business is essentially a relationship business.”
Jeorge Bradford, Iti
Jeorge Bradford makes every single garment for his label, Iti, by himself. Working out of a converted three-car garage on his mother-in-law’s property in Moss Vale in the southern highlands of Australia, he produces a small range of made-to-order shirts and pants defined by panels of latticed fabric.
Bradford, who graduated from the University of Technology Sydney with a bachelor of fashion design and textiles in 2023, developed the weaving technique for a university assignment. “I just really fell in love with it,” he says. He kept wearing the shirts he’d made after graduation, but hadn’t considered launching a brand until someone asked if they could buy one. He now has two stockists and a cult following.
While it varies for each piece, he estimates it takes 48 hours to make the simplest shirt – with prices starting at $250. Generally, the time between an order being placed and delivery is two to three weeks. While the process is so time intensive, or until he hires some staff, it will be difficult for Iti to scale, but for Bradford, fast growth is not really the point. “Whether it’s uber-successful or not, I’m still going to be doing it because I’ve always wanted to make clothes,” he says. “It’s the only thing I am interested in.”
Amy Lawrance
“In general life, I’m quite an anxious person but I find that when I am making, I do feel calm and a lot more at ease,” says Amy Lawrance. The winner of the National Designer award in 2025 is widely regarded as one of the industry’s most exciting new talents. She works exclusively with raw silk to create the floating gowns, skirts and tops that define her eponymous label.
While Lawrance was studying at RMIT University, she came across an old-fashioned technique in a Vogue sewing manual called the faggot stitch – a ladder or lace-like way to join two pieces of hemmed fabric. She worked to evolve it from a decorative stitch into something that could work as a significant part of a garment’s construction. Her resulting pieces sit off the body, and move in a unique, otherworldly way.
In her business, every order is bespoke. They often begin with conversations via Instagram direct message. After a bit of back and forth about what the client wants, Lawrance takes their measurements at her studio in Melbourne, drafts a new pattern and makes the garment by hand. Prices start at $2,800 and range up to $7,000. She spends between 10 and 20 hours on each garment, but the turnaround time is generally dictated by the client’s needs.
Although Lawrance is exploring expanding the business to wholesale, and what it may look like to externalise some of the production process to make this feasible, it is the “slow process of making a garment”, the meditation and muscle memory of the craft, that makes her happiest.
Daniel Jones, Zink & Sons
Daniel Jones, the director of Zink & Sons, is a third-generation suit-maker in a 131-year-old business. In the 1950s, when his grandfather took it over from the Zink family, they had been operating from the same premises on Sydney’s Oxford Street since 1895 – a lease that still stands now.
“We still maintain the traditional bespoke way of tailoring,” says Jones, who studied coat-making and fashion design at the University of Technology Sydney. “Most of the cutting and the tailoring side of things I learned alongside the tailors in our workroom.”
Every suit begins with a consultation on the suit’s purpose and material selection, before a pattern is made for each client. “So not just the measurements, but the posture, the body shape, all that’s taken into account,” Jones says. The suit is then crafted to fit. In the full bespoke service, everything from the buttonholes to the seams is sewn by hand. There is also a cheaper, semi-bespoke option that’s finished by machine.
A bespoke suit costs between $5,000 and $7,000 – depending on the cloth selection, while a semi-bespoke suit starts at $3,300. The turnaround times range from two to four months.
In the Zink & Sons workroom, there are just four full-time tailors who are supported by a few subcontractors offsite. Mass-produced suits are sewn flat and might pass through hundreds of hands from start to finish – a process that could not be more different from Zink & Sons’ approach.
Although Jones expresses concern about the future of the industry in Australia, particularly with regard to a lack of training, he takes solace in the fact that their clientele is getting younger. Jones thinks this is a good omen. Anything handcrafted by a skilled artisan will be seen as incredibly rare and valuable, he says, “because it’s been made by a human being using their time and knowledge and skill”.
Sean Tran, Shhorn
At Shhorn in Redfern, Sydney, every design is an experiment driven by instinct. “The technique is very important to me,” says Sean Tran, who founded the label in 2015 after graduating from architecture school. The result is an avant garde range of unisex coats, pants, shirts and accessories made from high-quality Japanese fabrics.
Tran’s cross-disciplinary background has given him a unique approach to garment construction that honours the intuitive process of hand-making. Rather than working from an image, he designs and solves problems on the fly, patterns are drawn freehand and silhouettes can change during the process of fitting and making. “The shapes and the construction are being discovered on the body in real time,” he says. “It’s sort of as banal as just feeling something and being like, ‘that feels too chunky’ or ‘that feels too flat’ or ‘it feels like it might tear too quickly’.”
The brand has two stockists and produces about 100 garments a year, most of them on a made-to-order basis. Clients visit the atelier, where Tran has two assistants, and everything is customisable, including fabric, length and volume. The turnaround time is eight to 12 weeks; prices start at $350 and range up to $2,500.
“It surprises me all the time how much interest I have, not only from the retail side, but also interest in the making and doing,” Tran says. “We have kids from the other side of the world come to work here, and they’re just super keen to learn the craft. I was pessimistic for a long time. But the internet created this opportunity for young people to discover things, and this world speaks to them.”
