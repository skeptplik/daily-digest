If you need a refresher on why we talk about sunscreen so much at Vogue, board-certified dermatologist Marnie Nussbaum points out that while our skin-care routines may include plenty of salmon sperm and copper peptides, the most effective step is also the least exciting: sunscreen. Used diligently and consistently, it can help prevent much of the damage we might otherwise try to address later, including collagen and elastin loss, skin laxity, hyperpigmentation, fine lines, and, most importantly, skin cancer.
As for the market itself, board-certified dermatologist Lindsey Zubritsky, MD, FAAD, attributes much of the category’s growth to increased consumer education. “In the past few years, there has been so much incredible education about the importance of wearing sunscreen every day,” she says. “With more people looking to incorporate SPF into their daily routines, the market has drastically expanded.” Today, that means everything from invisible formulas and tinted sunscreens to SPF lip balms and on-the-go sticks.
Another perk of being alive in 2026? Having easier access to formulas that were once largely limited to their home countries. Whether you’re discovering Korean, Japanese, and Australian sunscreen innovations on TikTok or asking a friend to bring back a French pharmacy favorite, it’s never been easier to explore what the rest of the world has to offer. The US has historically been slower to approve newer sunscreen filters than other markets, although that’s beginning to change. In June 2026, the FDA approved bemotrizinol, a UV filter already widely used abroad and the first new sunscreen ingredient permitted in the US in more than 25 years. Still, international markets offer formulators a broader range of UV filters, helping explain the variety of textures, finishes, and formulations available overseas.
Sunscreen has come a long way from formulas that focused primarily on UVB protection. Broad-spectrum options added UVA protection, and newer formulas have made daily SPF easier to wear. Board-certified dermatologist Dara Spearman, MD, FAAD, adds that newer formulas are not only more wearable but also easier to incorporate into everyday routines. “They’re more lightweight and suitable for everyday use across skin types,” she explains. Many newer tinted formulas are designed to blend more seamlessly into a wider range of skin tones. Several also contain iron oxides, which help protect against visible light, making them especially beneficial for those with melasma or hyperpigmentation.
But knowing how important sunscreen is and actually wearing it every day are two different things. According to board-certified dermatologist Sandra Oska, MD, daily use remains inconsistent. “There has been a genuine shift in how consumers think about sunscreen, but the majority of people still aren’t wearing it every day,” she says. Men, in particular, tend to underuse SPF, while women and Gen Z are generally more consistent. Even among regular users, common mistakes like underapplying can limit protection.
Ultimately, usability is what drives consistency. As Oska notes: “What are consumers actually going to use first thing in the morning, and what’s going to make it into their bag for reapplication?” Formulas that layer well with skin care tend to win out for daily use, while sticks, powders, and SPF lip balms have become essentials for touch-ups throughout the day.

Everything You Need to Know
How Does Sunscreen Work?
According to Dr. Nussbaum, sunscreen helps protect against both UVA and UVB rays, which cause different types of damage. UVB rays primarily cause sunburn and can damage DNA, increasing the risk of skin cancer. UVA rays penetrate deeper into the skin, contributing to collagen and elastin breakdown, fine lines, and premature aging. They can even penetrate windows, making daily protection important when you’re indoors, too.
When it comes to sunscreen formulas, there are two main types of UV filters: mineral and chemical. Mineral sunscreens use zinc oxide, titanium dioxide, or both to protect the skin, primarily by absorbing UV radiation, with some reflection and scattering. Chemical sunscreens also absorb UV rays and convert that energy into less harmful forms, primarily heat. Both can provide effective sun protection, although their textures and how they feel on the skin can vary. Mineral formulas are often recommended for sensitive skin, while chemical formulas tend to be lighter and less likely to leave a white cast.
What to Look For in a Face Sunscreen
SPF Level: “Picking a sunscreen does not have to be complicated!” Los Angeles dermatologist Chris Tomassian says. “Look for a sunscreen that is SPF 30 or higher and broad-spectrum, meaning it blocks both UVA and UVB rays. You want to use at least two finger lengths of sunscreen for the full face, and when you are out in the sun, reapply every two hours to maintain adequate sun protection.” Apply sunscreen at least 15 minutes before going outside.
Type: Mineral sunscreens use zinc oxide, titanium dioxide, or both to protect against UV radiation, primarily through absorption, with some reflection and scattering. Chemical sunscreens also absorb UV rays, converting their energy into less harmful forms. “Although both can be incredibly effective, chemical sunscreens can be somewhat more irritating to sensitive skin,” explains Dr. Nazarian. “Physical sunscreens are a bit more stable, but are also a little more occlusive and can trap heat, so it may feel a little heavier than the chemical sunscreens.” Zinc oxide and titanium dioxide are mineral sunscreen ingredients to look for.
Texture: How the sunscreen sits on the skin can make all the difference in how it wears. Those looking for something under makeup might appreciate a lightweight formula or something with a primer-like texture. Conversely, someone with dry skin might consider a richer, emollient formula similar to a face cream. That being said, keep your routine (and skin type) in mind when selecting your SPF this year.
White Cast: A white cast is a whitish, almost purplish hue left behind after applying some sunscreens. This often happens with mineral formulas that utilize zinc oxide to provide a physical barrier between the skin and sun rays. Fortunately, most sunscreens these days—even mineral options—do not leave a white cast.
