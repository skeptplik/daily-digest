UN-affiliated athlete swims in dangerous Strait of Hormuz to highlight Iran war's impact on wildlife
British endurance swimmer Lewis Pugh spent three days last week swimming parts of the dangerous waterway
British endurance swimmer Lewis Pugh has swum in the North Pole and Antarctica to draw attention to environmental causes. But battling freezing waters and extreme conditions hasn't prepared him for his latest challenge: swimming the Strait of Hormuz while tankers are attacked and blown up nearby.
Pugh, who is a United Nations Patron of the Oceans, spent three days last week swimming parts of the dangerous waterway to highlight how the Iran war threatens the area's habitat and wildlife.
Wearing only swimming trunks, goggles and a cap bearing the UN logo, the athlete started from the Musandam peninsula, on the southern tip of the strait, and ended by rounding the Cape of Hormuz, completing about 15 kilometres in total. Pugh is believed to be the first person to undertake a long-distance swim there since the war started.
The swimmer said that minutes after he entered the water on Monday, he heard two loud explosions that rocked the sea.
A crew member aboard a boat accompanying endurance swimmer Lewis Pugh looks toward a burning liquefied petroleum gas tanker in the Strait of Hormuz on Tuesday. (Lewis Pugh Foundation/The Associated Press)
"I looked up and I could see that an oil tanker about four kilometres away had been hit," he told The Associated Press after he returned to London. "The explosion was so loud, two booms, and then fighter jets coming in. And at that moment I realized we have to get out of here right now."
Pugh, 56, managed to finish his feat after more stops and starts, including passing another vessel that sent fireballs into the sky after it was attacked.
"Most people, when they think about the Strait of Hormuz, they think of a shipping lane," he said. "But it is also one of the most beautiful places I have ever been … it's one of the treasure troves of the natural world."
"With all these ships being sunk, this area is under severe threat," he added. "It’s going to take years, decades for this area to recover."
Oil pollution from damaged tankers threatens dolphins and mangroves
Iran has in recent weeks increased attacks on vessels that it claims travelled along unauthorized routes in the strait as it seeks to impose its control on the crucial waterway, where a fifth of all oil traded passed through before the U.S. and Israel launched the war on Feb. 28.
Pugh adjusts his goggles before diving into the Strait of Hormuz last week. Over the course of the week he completed about 15 kilometres in total. (Lewis Pugh Foundation/The Associated Press)
Several tankers have also struck sea mines laid by Tehran, in some cases exploding and bursting into flames, among them one that was carrying over two million barrels of oil last month.
Pugh warned of how oil leaking from the dozens of tankers damaged since February could create lasting problems for the region's animals, as well as its coastlines, coral reefs and mangrove forests.
He described seeing large numbers of dolphins following his boat as he sailed in, as well as turtles and ospreys. He also marvelled at the hundreds of turtles he saw during a swim along the Omani coast before the war.
"When oil gets in there, when chemical pollution gets in there, it becomes very, very difficult to clean up," he said. "It lasts for generations."
Swim challenge took place as vessels were attacked
To stay safe, Pugh swam the strait in sections over three days, with a total of about six and a half hours of swimming. The athlete recalled seeing a gas carrier being struck as he returned to the harbour on the second day.
Smoke rises from a burning liquefied petroleum gas tanker in the Strait of Hormuz — an area described by Pugh as 'one of the most beautiful places' he has ever seen. (Lewis Pugh Foundation via The Associated Press)
"I'll never ever forget going past that gas carrier, the smell of the chemicals and then the flames, they were literally like fireballs going up into the sky. Around the ship, the water was on fire," Pugh said.
"The impact which a casualty like this has on nature — it was very, very acute."
Using sport to lobby for green causes
Pugh has spent much of the past 40 years swimming in places from Antarctica to the Red Sea and the English Channel to urge more action to protect marine environments from climate change, pollution and loss of biodiversity.
He was the first athlete to swim across the North Pole and complete a long-distance swim in every one of the world’s oceans. In 2023, he swam all 507 kilometres of the Hudson River in New York state to highlight the importance of clean rivers.
"My message is very simple," he said. "We've got to stop the war. We’ve got to open the strait, and we’ve got to clean up the pollution."
