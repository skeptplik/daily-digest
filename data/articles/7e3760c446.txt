Last year was a mixed bag for Whovians. Disney dumped Doctor Who, leaving the franchise in such dire straits that the BBC had no choice but to look for someone new to steer the long-running sci-fi show into a new era. That said, the franchise also got a major win with Russell T. Davies and director Dylan Holmes-Williams’s climate-focused spin-off, The War Between Land and Sea, which debuted in the U.K. that December but still hasn't officially been released in the U.S.
Critics praised the Doctor Who spin-off for its more mature tone and grown-up storytelling, with Radio Times in particular suggesting that it could potentially “turn the tide” for the Whoniverse. The approval of The War Between Land and Sea’s more adult themes reminded me of another adult spin-off that, at the time of airing, wasn’t as well received by critics when it aired but deserves a modern reappraisal.
Torchwood was the answer to Doctor Who’s greatest mystery
Throughout David Tennant’s first season as the time-traveling, universe-hopping alien in 2006, one word kept showing up again and again: Torchwood. It appeared briefly in 2005, during Christopher Eccleston’s tenure, as a quiz-show answer in “Bad Wolf.” It popped up again in “The Christmas Invasion,” when Harriet Jones (Penelope Wilton) called on Torchwood to shoot down the retreating Sycorax spaceship. Eventually, the full origins of the mysterious organization would be revealed later in Tennant’s season during the episode “Tooth and Claw.”
By the end of Tennant’s first season, the full mystery was solved: Torchwood was a secret British agency founded by Queen Victoria in 1879 to investigate alien threats and obtain their technology (Torchwood is an anagram of Doctor Who). But the agency's full impact on the Who franchise would only become clear on Oct. 22, 2006, when showrunners Russell T. Davies and Chris Chibnall — who would later go on to become the showrunner of Doctor Who during Jodie Whittaker’s 13th Doctor era — debuted the series on BBC Three with two episodes: “Everything Changes” and “Day One.”
Torchwood came hot on the heels of the end of Doctor Who’s second season. Set in Cardiff, Wales, the series follows a small, covert group that works independently of the restof the ultra-powerful organization. Led by Captain Jack Harkness (played to perfection by John Barrowman), this group is far more open to accepting aliens than its London counterpart. Still, when push comes to shove, they’ll do whatever it takes to defend humanity from an extraterrestrial threat. Over four seasons, this included the team dealing with an alien that lives off orgasms, a memory-altering alien that shakes up the team’s lives and memories like its own personal fanfiction, and one of my favorite additions to the Whoniverse: humanoid scavengers known as Weevils.
Doctor Who but for adults
The number one thing you need to know about Torchwood’s original reception is this: Look up ‘gritty’ and ‘edgy’ in the dictionary, and you’ll find this Doctor Who spin-off staring back at you.
Unlike the more family-friendly Doctor Who, Torchwood had sex, profanity, drug use, and more. For some critics, the overabundance of risqué content — who can forget the infamous and scantily-clad Cyberwoman? — proved too grating for critics to find anything positive about the series.
Even as a lover of Torchwood, I can’t help but somewhat agree with that line of thinking. Much of Season 1 puts characters in situations where sex, murder, and little else seemed to be the only options. If Doctor Who was the sweet, innocent teen who does their homework on time and kisses their parents before bed, Torchwood was the chain-smoking, rebellious hooligan whose favorite word was “fuck.”
That said, it would be a mistake to dismiss Torchwood entirely. Not only did the series find its footing by season 2, adopting a less juvenile tone and a greater focus on the mental and physical repercussions of dealing with alien threats every day without a sonic screwdriver or TARDIS to back them up, but it also expanded on one of the greatest characters to emerge from Doctor Who’s 2005 revival: Jack.
The man, the myth, the legend
Viewers first met Jack in the Doctor Who revival’s debut season, in the two-part arc “The Empty Child” and “The Doctor Dances.” A scoundrel originally from the 51st century, Jack joined the Ninth Doctor and Rose (Billie Piper) on their adventures, but met a terrible end at the hands of a Dalek in “The Parting of the Ways.” Luckily, Rose, having absorbed the energy of the TARDIS’s time vortex, accidentally resurrected Jack, making him a fixed point in time and leaving him unable to die.
Viewers didn’t see Jack again until Torchwood. In the show's first season, he was portrayed as brutal, utterly changed by the Doctor’s abandonment and the burden of immortality. Though he still had his cheeky moments, Jack was far from the man he’d been with the Doctor. He was unforgiving, relentless, and determined to survive long enough to see the Doctor again.
The Jack audiences see in Torchwood is the Doctor's perfect foil in every way, and, as a Whovian who loves both, that's one of the key reasons the spin-off is so damn appealing. The Doctor changes people, and through spin-offs like Class and The Sarah Jane Adventures, we can see how much of that change is for the better.
In Torchwood, we see Jack at his lowest after traveling with the Doctor. He’s angry and traumatized, and he isn’t afraid to hurt the people he’s supposed to rely on, a tendency mirrored in the rest of his teammates at Torchwood.
As a result, for much of season 1, we see a group of people who bite and snarl at one another, acting selfishly and disloyally. From these shaky beginnings, the team gradually mellows and learns to trust and love one another despite the dangers they face every day. It’s this progress between seasons that makes Torchwood a satisfying watch and the perfect adult addition to Doctor Who.
