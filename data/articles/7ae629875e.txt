On the morning of May 31, 2025, Shirley Bekker made an impulsive decision. The then 29-year-old doctor living in London finally pulled the trigger on plane tickets to visit Foshan, in southern China, for a flight later that same day.
Two months prior, Bekker had little knowledge of the city of 9 million; she had no idea what hotel to stay at or where to go once she landed. But Bekker was convinced that six days in Foshan to furnish her apartment—and possibly having all of the furniture shipped back to London—would still be cheaper than getting everything at home. With over 100,000 followers on social media, she also later turned her adventure into content, detailing her finds and their cost.
Bekker was in the process of buying her dream one-bedroom apartment in East London and spent weeks obsessively watching home renovation videos on TikTok. “When I saw that things like a sofa cost thousands of pounds, I was like, ‘OK, I’m convinced now that I’ll probably do something drastic to not have to pay that much,’” she says. Her social media algorithm was already feeding her a radical solution. “Made in China, shit quality?” a Chinese furniture seller said in videos on Bekker’s TikTok feed. “Grass sofa? Our factory has mass-produced it; UFO table? Our factory has mass-produced it.” Soon, Bekker says, her “entire timeline became Foshan, Foshan, Foshan.” She learned all about China’s furniture capital, which each year exports nearly $10 billion worth of couches and beds and bathroom vanities, and everything else you could possibly need for your home.
Bekker is just one of a growing number of people around the world who order furniture directly from factories in Foshan. By either flying to the city—or through WhatsApp, TikTok, AliExpress, or Alibaba—buyers can commission made-to-order pieces. And most importantly, they can purchase dupes.
Duplicates were once primarily the domain of fashion and beauty, but consumers are now seeking out knockoffs of luxury designer furniture that promise the same quality as the original at a much lower cost—even after international shipping rates and tariffs. During the week of April 13, 2025, the number of videos urging Americans to buy directly from Chinese factories soared 250 percent. Tens of thousands of videos had been posted under hashtags like #ChineseFactory, and many of them were about furniture pieces. Some videos garnered millions of views and included contacts of Chinese sellers in the comments.
@gabwithmeg, a working mom in California, claims on TikTok that she saved at least $100,000 by furnishing her entire house directly from China. “It is all Restoration Hardware and Arhaus-style furniture, and I’m going to tell you exactly how I did it,” she promised her followers in January. @gabwithmeg’s post received over 65,000 likes, and the comments are filled with users clamoring to opt out of the traditional furniture market, if not bragging about having already done so.
Together, they represent a turning tide in furniture shopping for Westerners. If people can buy a couch that looks exactly like the famous Togo for $200 on AliExpress, why would they pay $4,800 for one from Design Within Reach? Sure, Ligne Roset is a legendary brand, famously crafting all its furniture near the French Alps, made by trained artisans using an almost entirely manual process. But not everyone cares about history, owning an original, or the ethics of intellectual property in design. In fact, some furniture buyers, including Bekker, aren’t even aware that some pieces they covet are copies. Bekker feels that designer furniture is more expensive because of the brand name, not necessarily the quality. “Sorry to say, for me, it’s a bit bullshit,” she says. “I’m really not one to buy into the idea that the name of the brand makes it worth the price.”
“We’re seeing the beginnings of a revolt against the brandification of everything,” says Christopher Sprigman, a law professor at New York University who has studied the knockoff economy. As a consumer, Sprigman expects brands to offer good and consistent quality, but there is a perception that “there’s no real quality control, there’s no real integrity, it’s basically just advertising and driving up costs, creating demand, creating desire without any real substantial benefit that the consumer can feel or taste or see,” Sprigman says.
The videos Bekker saw never explicitly said how much the furniture in Foshan would cost, but she was confident it would be far less than at the posh showrooms in London. Despite not speaking Chinese, she roamed around the furniture markets in Foshan’s warm and humid air. By the end of the trip, she had furnished her entire apartment for around £7,000 (about $9,200). Everything would go in a 20-foot container, embark from Guangzhou on a freight ship, and arrive at an English port more than two months later. Bekker’s videos attracted millions of views, and fans swarmed to the comments with the same refrain: “I have never been so influenced to go to China.”
Everyone who goes to Foshan for furniture makes sure to go to the Louvre.
In the central part of Foshan, the Louvre claims to be one of the largest furniture shopping malls in China, and likely the whole world. At over 4 million square feet, it’s bigger than its namesake in France, which is the largest museum in the world; 4 million people from over 150 countries and regions visit Foshan’s Louvre every year to shop for furniture. Past the facade—which looks like an outdated convention center with blue-tinted windows and rows of country flags above the entrance—is an opulent atrium with marble columns and gilded moldings.
The complex was designed to be part of a tourist experience package, with official certification from the Chinese tourism bureau. There’s a shuttle bus from the nearest international airport and a five-star Sofitel hotel for guests. In addition to tourists from the US and Europe, there are ever more shoppers, architects, and traders from the Middle East, Africa, Russia, Central Asia, and South Asia.
The Louvre contains showrooms for over 2,000 Chinese brands and 400 European ones, so there’s something for everyone. As many of these brands construct their furniture in Foshan, it’s the ideal place for a showroom. Italian minimalism is one of the most visible aesthetics here, but you’ll also find styles like Russian imperial, “new Chinese,” and wabi-sabi. In most places, photography is strictly prohibited to “protect original designs.”
But the Louvre is often just a shopper’s first destination. “Foshan is not actually one furniture market. It’s a district, it’s a city, it’s an ecosystem,” says Claudia Hofmeister, an Australia-based furniture sourcing agent who has helped clients from around the world buy furniture from Foshan; she has personally traveled there at least six times. While the Louvre is the pinnacle of luxury furniture shopping, Foshan also offers other mid-tier wholesale businesses that are 100 percent Chinese, plus lower-end furniture brands that Hofmeister says she won’t touch. “And there’s a whole lot of other models like big supercenters just dedicated to lighting, tiles, and building supplies,” she says.
For many travelers, the real shopping doesn’t happen there. The dupe factories, along with home construction and decor manufacturers, are a few miles away in Longjiang. Longjiang is known as the hub of furniture manufacturing, where you can find everything from fabrics to timber to metal parts. Its streets are lined with miles of furniture shops and nothing else. The town has its own exhibition center, but it stands in stark contrast to the opulent tourism spectacle at the Louvre.
Every March and August, Longjiang hosts its own furniture fair, which attracts small manufacturers, dealers, and domestic buyers from other parts of China. The furniture brands in the district care less about original design and are sometimes explicit about where they draw inspiration. On the facade of a showroom building next to the exhibition center, a billboard-sized banner advertises a “Lawrence sofa,” a popular 2017 design by the famous Italian brand Minotti that can sell for over $10,000. In their booths, some sellers display portraits of a white “art director,” an Italian flag, or plaster sculptures.
Even for Chinese people, the idea of traveling to Foshan to buy furniture instead of placing orders from home is relatively new. As China’s real estate economy slowed over the past decade, the traditional business model of licensed furniture distribution struggled. Factories now have to contend with tariffs and competition from countries where labor can be cheaper. Sourcing companies position themselves to factories as a more direct connection to consumers. Even though buyers pay to travel, they can save “30 percent to 50 percent” while still gaining access to new designs and high-quality products, says Lin Fangfang, the founder and chairman of Yueanju, one of the earliest and most influential B2C furniture sourcing agents in China.
Yueanju, which was founded in 2016, says it has worked closely with the local government to develop the “travel to Foshan” marketing strategy. On the radio nationwide, ads hype shopping for furniture in Foshan, with organized pickups from train stations and airports, as well as customized factory tours based on specific needs and budget. A typical three-day trip costs as little as $170 per person. Agents take care of everything and even provide a cafeteria and nap area with recliners in Yueanju’s office building in Longjiang. “Even though [Longjiang is] only one bridge away, they just won’t come here because they don’t know about us,” Lin says. “[Now], we can accommodate over 200 visitors with no problem on this floor.”
Services like what Yueanju offers quickly exploded in popularity, bringing a steady flow of consumers to what had previously been a business-to-business manufacturing hub. They are now working with clients from countries including the US, Russia, Saudi Arabia, and Australia. In March, during Ramadan, the company hosted a group from Saudi Arabia. “They are royals, so their homes are pretty big,” Lin says. The company customized the shopping experience based on their fasting needs, only going out after 3 pm every day to preserve energy.
The modern history of furniture in Foshan started with a dupe. As the lore goes, in the 1970s a man named Li Jungang working at a carpentry factory got his hands on a couch from nearby Hong Kong, then a British colony. While upholstered furniture took off in Europe in the 17th century, traditional chairs in China were still made of hardwood and had no built-in cushioning. By the 1970s in Hong Kong, upholstered, Western-style couches were all the rage. Li cut open the couch, studied interior construction, and is said to have built what is considered the first-ever upholstered sofa in China. Similar stories citing the origin of Foshan’s relatively short history in modern furniture manufacturing are now proudly recorded in local history books, displayed in furniture showrooms, and retold by every entrepreneur in the city.
The rest of the story tracks the rise of manufacturing in China. As the government started embracing the market economy in the late 1970s, China became the locus of offshore manufacturing for the rest of the world. Foshan’s voracious entrepreneurs and artisans saw an opportunity to remake the city—which until then was known more for kung fu and local cuisine—into a manufacturing powerhouse. The city became known for factories that specialized in building materials, home appliances, and furniture. Foshan’s home goods industry grew to include over 7,000 businesses that account for at least one-fifth of the furniture market in China. In 2025, Foshan exported $2.65 billion worth of furniture alone. “If you have a home, you have something made in Foshan,” says the promotional slogan and industry alliance launched by the local government in 2022.
As the city became a furniture juggernaut, it developed an obsession with Italy and, more specifically, Milan, the capital of 20th-century interior design. At Salone del Mobile, the internationally renowned annual furniture fair showcasing some of the most innovative designs in the world, Foshan factory manufacturers study the latest luxury designs from Italian brands. A smaller number of companies bring their own products to the fair, and local furniture leaders also visit to help promote government collaborations between Foshan and Milan. In 2011, a Chinese newspaper reported that during that year’s furniture fair, Fendi Casa refused to admit Chinese visitors to its showroom. (Salone del Mobile did not respond to a request for comment.)
Some Foshan furniture makers do copy designs from Salone, and they do it fast. The 2026 fair ended on April 26; by May 11, Foshan furniture factories were posting on Chinese social media about mass-producing the Anywhere sofa by Edra, one of the breakout products of the year. “The first domestic new Anywhere sofa is here,” says a Chinese woman in a brightly lit showroom, walking past a sofa that looks identical to the original, on Chinese social media platform Rednote. The account, under the name HY Casa, says it’s a source factory in Foshan that specializes in “high-end Italian minimal made-to-order 1:1 furniture dupes.”
To create a truly convincing replica, snapping a few photos in Milan is not enough. Wang Xunyong is the owner of EIBA, a furniture brand in Foshan that produces high-end dupes of Minotti sofas by ordering the original, shipping it back to Foshan, and taking it apart to study its internal structure, just like Li did in the ’70s.
Before the success of EIBA, Wang had a series of failed ventures—restaurants and several other furnishing-related businesses. Eventually, he arrived at a decision: “Instead of doing a hundred mediocre things, it’s better to take one thing to the extreme,” he says in a video posted on his social media account. In 2023, he founded his current company with a small workshop model, leveraging his past experience in the furniture business. This time, it worked.
Wang’s factory in Longjiang occupies the fourth floor of a pristine white industrial building, where a polished lobby opens into a modest showroom/workshop. Several rows of finished sofas sit near the entrance beneath ceiling spotlights. Wang has a makeshift office area that he shares with his sales staff. Every so often, a Bluetooth speaker linked to his phone announces in a flat robotic voice that someone has made a furniture order online. On the workshop floor, five or six women cut and sew sofa fabric, while several men shape foam, spray adhesive, and stretch upholstery over frames.
Wang has never been to Italy. His understanding of Italian design comes from sofas dismantled inside his factory. The first time Wang cut open an Italian-made sofa, he was struck by its complexity. It wasn’t just layers of materials stacked together, but rather a system with different densities, cutting methods, and structural logic working in harmony. He compares the sofa manufacturing process to making a cake: You can standardize the baking process by using a mold, but a complex curve would sometimes require human hands. For a long time, Wang says, many factories focused only on replicating the look of luxury couches. The proportions and silhouettes of the sofas were similar to the originals, but the internal structure was oversimplified. But that misses the essence of what makes a piece of furniture great.
This path isn’t cheap. Purchasing original pieces requires significant investment, often eight to 10 times the price of the dupes his factory produces. Once the model is thoroughly studied, efficiency improves. Today, Wang says, his team can complete the reproduction of a new design within 45 days. This methodology has allowed his factory to reach annual sales of 20 million yuan (about $3 million). Unlike Minotti, which makes almost every piece of its custom furniture in Meda, Italy, Chinese factories can count on much lower costs for labor and manufacturing.
To outsiders, Wang’s work is ethically dubious, but he is unapologetic. “What we’re trying to do is copy it first and surpass it later,” he says. “Many people look down on copying others, but we ignore those noises. First, we learn their details and craftsmanship. Once you have the financial strength and vision, you’ll be much better equipped to do your own thing.”
In his view, as long as a product does not carry the original brand’s logo, it cannot be considered “counterfeit and substandard.” On a more practical level, he has developed strategies to reduce legal risk. Based on his understanding of regulations, Wang says, a piece is legally distinct if there are “seven points of difference,” even if that’s as minor as changing a board’s thickness from 3 centimeters to 2.9.
Wang’s rule of seven is a common belief among Chinese manufacturers, but it’s more folklore than law, says Morgan Pietz, managing partner at the Los Angeles office of Merchant & Gould, who has represented both sides of furniture IP lawsuits. “At least under US law, there’s no rule that if you make seven changes, you are good to go,” he says.
But it is difficult to legally claim that someone has knocked off a furniture design, lawyers say. In the US, most plaintiffs in the furniture industry need to go down one of two routes: Either they register patents for each of their products, or they need to demonstrate that a layperson would be able to discern the product’s origin. Both are difficult hurdles to clear. Design patents can cost around $5,000, including attorney costs and a filing fee for one product, and they only last 15 years; to make a “trade dress” claim, a furniture company needs to show that its consumers recognize the design as being from a specific source. In most cases, furniture designs don’t rise to this level of scrutiny. And no matter which route one chooses, bringing the claim to court and enforcing it could cost millions of dollars.
Designing a piece of furniture is not the same as writing a book or composing a piece of music, and it isn’t protected to the same extent, says Sprigman, the NYU law professor. “People may be under some mistaken apprehension that the designs for chairs are owned like the design for a symphony is owned. And it actually doesn’t work that way, because chairs are useful articles, so they don’t get the same kind of copyright protection that symphonies get.”
Sometimes there can even be a correlation between the popularity of dupes and the sales of originals, Sprigman says. When studying the luxury fashion knockoff industry in China, he found that buyers of counterfeit handbags can often move on to purchasing the genuine product once they become wealthier. “For some people, you could view dupes as a gateway drug to the traditional thing,” he says.
To Wang, the production of dupes is not only a furniture industry phenomenon but also indicative of how capitalism in China works more broadly. “Many of our industries started from scratch. If you want results as fast as possible, you have to learn from abroad first. Whether it’s equipment, aircraft, artillery, or high-speed rail, haven’t we always followed this same path?”
Still, creating dupes is not a business that can scale infinitely. In Wang’s view, the market for copies has become oversaturated, and competitive price wars are eating into profit margins. Foshan brands are now pivoting to original designs, not only out of ethical concerns but also due to market pressure. One day, when he has enough financial capital and experience, he hopes to develop his own designs. And only then will he consider actually traveling to Italy. Not to copy their furniture, but to experience Italian culture and find inspiration.
