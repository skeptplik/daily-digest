Japan ranked 86th in the world in the 2026 edition of the largest global ranking of adult English proficiency, showing signs of a turnaround after years of decline.
The ranking, released Wednesday by Education First (EF), an international education organization, showed that Japan rose 10 places from the previous year, when it ranked 96th among 123 countries. The 2025 index marked an all-time low, extending a downward trend in Japan’s English proficiency, which fell continuously in recent years.
This year’s data was compiled from the test results of 2.3 million participants across 117 countries and regions.
In the 2026 index, Japan also moved from the lowest proficiency band “very low,” to the “low” band.
Despite the improvement in this year’s index, Japan continued to lag behind its peers in Asia and elsewhere in the world in English proficiency.
Compared to South Korea, for example, which reached 50th place in the index, Japan was significantly lower — ranking 36 places below its neighbor.
European countries dominated the top of this year’s rankings, with Sweden claiming the top spot followed by Austria and Denmark.
Japan also scored below the overall average in EF’s assessment, receiving 450 points — 35 points below the 485-point average.
In terms of specific areas of proficiency, Japanese learners performed better in reading than in speaking and writing.
EF also found regional differences in English proficiency, with learners in urban hubs such as Yokohama, Tokyo and Kyoto performing better than those in Hiroshima, Shizuoka and Kochi.
As in many other countries covered by the index, younger people in Japan showed particularly low levels of English language ability — with 18- to 20-year-olds recording the lowest scores of all age groups.
EF suggested that the decline in English proficiency among younger people, observed worldwide, was partly attributable to the COVID-19 pandemic.
The organization suggested that there is still a knock-on effect on this cohort who had to take online rather than in-person English classes between 2019 and 2022, which is being seen in Japan and across the world.
Masato Suzuki, business development manager for EF Japan, said “Raising the English proficiency of this (young) generation will create a bottom-up effect that elevates proficiency levels when they enter the workforce.”
In recommending ways to improve Japanese people’s English proficiency, Nana Gronning Ito, EF country manager, advised not relying solely on digital learning and instead communicating in real life.
“We believe the next step is very clear: it is to build on the foundation there already. We want people to experience the joy of expressing their feelings — even if they make mistakes along the way,” she said.
