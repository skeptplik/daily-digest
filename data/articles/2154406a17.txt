Grand Theft Auto VI has received near-universal acclaim and could be the highest-grossing entertainment launch in history. But it holds a more esoteric honor: It might be the most accurately rendered depiction of Florida’s bird life to appear in a video game.
Rockstar Games has filled the latest GTA installation with over 170 animal species that players can tally in their logbook. At the same time, there’s been an explosion of interest in birdwatching, especially among young people, since the last GTA came out 13 years ago. While many players have gotten busy stealing jets and going to strip clubs, birders and streamers are doing “ornithologist runs” to see as many bird species as they can.
I myself am not a gamer, but I’m an avid birder based in New York City who’s familiar with a few indie birdwatching games like Alba and Flock Around, and I have done a bit of birding in Florida—which GTA VI is modeled after, with Vice City being a stand-in for Miami. So I was hungry to get my hands on GTA VI and see the birds for myself. Were the details of GTA VI’s avian NPCs as lifelike as its human ones? (Full disclosure that a friend of mine worked on the game, though not the birds specifically.)
You receive a pair of binoculars with the first mission, and most of the map is open to explore once the intro is over, so you can begin your birding adventures immediately. I left the house and walked around town, trying to get a sense of the neighborhood and searching for places I knew I’d find wildlife, as I would in real life. It turns out you don’t have to look hard. Birds are everywhere in GTA VI—brown pelicans soared overhead, a flock of house sparrows alighted onto a telephone wire, and a blue jay stared down at me as I rounded a corner.
I zeroed in on a gull perched atop a building and noticed its partial black hood of feathers, an adult laughing gull. In a sign of how committed Rockstar is to accurately portraying the real world, I walked further and noticed a bird with the same shape, but it was grayer and light on the black feathers, an immature laughing gull.
This is GTA VI, and all means of improving your birding experience are at your disposal—legal or not. With that in mind, I headed toward the water and performed my first act of theft, jacking a kayak so I could inspect the mangroves and sandy islands in the bay. While motorboats were available, I opted for human-powered to avoid scaring the birds.
The sky was filled with iconic South Florida birds like white ibises, magnificent frigate birds, and osprey. I shouted excitedly on spotting a flock of Caspian terns loafing on a small islet’s shore, then eyed a spotted sandpiper. The latter didn’t have its real-world analogue’s “twerking” strut (a big missed opportunity for a GTA game), but its physical appearance was accurate. I beached my kayak and went ashore, where I eyed a pair of great blue herons and then scared a tricolored heron from a shrub as I sprinted away from a crocodile I nearly stepped on.
I was impressed by the game developer’s commitment to accurately rendering South Florida’s coastal mangrove habitats, which provide shelter for an abundance of fish, food for birds, and serve as a habitat for the threatened American crocodile. However, they only host so much avian diversity.
To see more species, I drove north through the various habitats in and around the fictional city of Leonida and stopped to scan the skies. I spotted American robins, northern mockingbirds, and “New World orioles,” the game’s analog to the Baltimore oriole, which I assumed is named as such because Baltimore is not part of the Grand Theft Auto universe. Downtown Vice City featured non-native peacocks and rock pigeons—the latter of which were introduced to North America in the 1600s. When I arrived at the pine-covered areas north of the city, I began seeing red-cockaded woodpeckers and scrub jays, an analog to the rare Florida scrub-jay that similarly inhabits sand pine areas north of Miami and is endemic to the state of Florida.
Birders who’ve been to Florida will find Leonida’s abundance and distribution of wildlife familiar. As in meatspace, pelicans and ibises are constantly flying overhead, and wading birds fill every body of water. Birds are properly segregated into their habitats, with songbirds and city birds in built-up areas, shorebirds and long-legged wading birds in wetlands. In both the game and real life, despite intensive human development, birds still attempt to survive where they can.
The birds’ behaviors are also accurate beyond standing and flying. As I was trying to study my first bald eagle, a pair of crows flew over to chase it, a behavior regularly seen in the wild to scare large raptors away. Monk parakeets preened while perching on a wire, and a juvenile northern mockingbird did its characteristic wing flicking. Meanwhile, GTA VI’s great blue herons always seem to be having sex, not something I’ve noticed in real life but a silly detail nonetheless.
Frankly, the whole experience of driving around while distractedly watching telephone wires and roadsides or pulling over to check every little water body is itself the most accurate representation of the Florida birding experience.
My other birding friends who have played the game concurred. “The first thing I thought was how closely this resembles birding in Florida,” says accomplished bird author Nick Lund, who has done extensive birdwatching in Red Dead Redemption II, another game where avian life is a big part of gameplay, and is currently doing a GTA VI birding speed run. He’s noticed other in-game birding Easter eggs—for example, he’s encountered spot-breasted orioles, a Central American species introduced to southeastern Florida that birdwatchers specifically target when visiting Miami.
While impressive in its fidelity to real life, GTA VI’s avian depictions aren’t 100 percent accurate. I found the flight styles and silhouettes of many birds to be a little off, while some renditions missed the mark—the abundant crows all feel too long and pale. While walking around Jason’s neighborhood, Lund and I both noticed the apparent song of the bananaquit, a Caribbean species that rarely appears in the US. The game is also filled with whooping cranes, one of the rarest birds in the US. They’re also mostly gone from Florida after an unsuccessful reintroduction effort, where sandhill cranes are much more common. (Maybe the makers of GTA VI are telling real-world conservationists to try harder next time.) Lund flagged that the roseate spoonbill seems to make the call of the willow ptarmigan—a detail he also noticed in RDR II. And somehow I’ve yet to spot a bird in a tree, but this may be a skill issue.
Ultimately, GTA VI isn’t a birdwatching game, and does lag behind the real world and birdwatching-specific games in terms of avian content. Sixty-two species might sound like a lot, but birders in Miami-Dade County have spotted more than 240 species in November 2026 alone, and one real-life birder in Miami spotted 61 species in a two-hour stint on the day of the game’s release.
There’s a certain magic and unpredictability to birdwatching that the game doesn’t nail—finding a migratory warbler in a hotel parking lot, catching an egret hunting anoles in a topiary, or spotting an out-of-place vagrant bird are all real-life thrills the game can’t fully capture. Of course, GTA VI also has an actual plot and storyline that I surmise might be slightly more important to its developers and the majority of the gaming public than solely building the world’s most accurate Florida birding simulator.
Still, GTA VI’s animal observation mechanics have continued to delight after 12 hours of play. Birding is certainly an auxiliary part of the game, rather than its core thrust—but if you’re a birder wondering whether to play the game just for its birds, I think you’ll still have plenty of fun.
