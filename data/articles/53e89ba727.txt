Preserved teeth from two caves in southern China indicate Homo erectus lived on mainland Asia as recently as 184,000 years ago. That is recent enough for the species to have met later groups like the Denisovans.
It seems these Chinese H. erectus were living in a tropical rainforest – an environment where the species has not previously been found.
“We’ve got this late surviving population, and we’ve got this rich story of how they were living,” says geochronologist Kira Westaway at Macquarie University in Sydney.
H. erectus was one of the first species in our genus, Homo. It was also the first species of hominin we know for sure migrated beyond Africa. The earliest H. erectus fossils are from Africa 2 million years ago, but by 1.8 million years ago they were living in what is now Georgia on the border between Europe and Asia. Eventually the species spread further east into what is now China. They even made it to the island of Java.
The most recent fossils identified as H. erectus are from Java and are between 117,000 and 108,000 years old. However, it’s been thought the species vanished much earlier from mainland Asia. Until the discoveries from China announced today, the most recent securely dated H. erectus were from around 400,000 years ago.
Around this time, other hominin groups emerged, like the Denisovans who lived in East Asia. “The assumption would have been that erectus is basically no longer there, because they have evolved into a Denisovan population,” says molecular palaeontologist Frido Welker at the University of Copenhagen in Denmark.
Welker, Westaway and their colleagues investigated two caves in southern China. Baxian and Naxian are both limestone caves, about 15 kilometres apart. They were initially excavated by Chinese teams led by Yingqi Zhang at the Institute of Vertebrate Paleontology and Paleoanthropology in Beijing and Wei Wang at Shandong University.
Combined, the two caves contained more than 18,000 mammal fossils. These included nine hominin teeth: six from Naxian and three from Baxian. Based on their sizes and shapes, and CT scans of their internal structures, eight of the teeth were identified as belonging to Homo erectus. The ninth tooth, from Baxian cave, could not be clearly identified.
For further evidence to confirm the identity of the hominins, the team turned to molecular biology: they attempted to recover proteins preserved inside the dentine of the ancient teeth. Previously, only a handful of H. erectus specimens have yielded proteins – primarily those described in a study published in May. This time, Welker, Westaway and their colleagues were able to obtain proteins from five of the teeth, including the mystery ninth tooth.
The team focused on a protein called ameloblastin, found in the dentine. The four teeth identified as H. erectus turned out to have a telltale signature in the protein, only previously seen in other Asian H. erectus fossils and not in fossils belonging to other ancient hominins. This strengthened the case that these teeth did indeed come from the mouths of H. erectus individuals.
However, the H. erectus teeth also contained an unusual signature elsewhere in the protein, not seen in other groups. The team suggests that the teeth may be evidence of a genetically distinct population of H. erectus, probably separated from those we already know of by geographic distance.
“I can absolutely see how they came to the conclusion that this is a different population,” says Sheela Athreya at Texas A&M University. “They were careful to compare it to the entire database they could get a hold of.”
The remaining tooth, which could not be identified morphologically, had ameloblastin that could not be matched to a hominin – though that could be because most hominin fossils haven’t yielded preserved proteins. “We really cannot say at this stage what it is,” says Welker.
The researchers performed an array of experiments to determine the ages of the H. erectus teeth. They directly dated the teeth themselves, the sediments in which they were found and mineral formations in the caves. “They all date different events in the cave, and as long as you understand the events, they do all correlate really well with each other,” says Westaway. The team concluded the teeth are between 324,000 and 184,000 years old.
At the time, the caves seem to have been located in a tropical rainforest. The animal bones found at the site came from an array of rainforest-dwelling species, including extinct species of rhinoceros and elephant. The H. erectus population present at the cave was part of a food chain based on trees rather than grasses, according to analyses of carbon isotopes in the teeth. Other isotopic evidence points to humid tropical conditions.
The team also looked at strontium isotopes, which reveal where the H. erectus were living during their first few years of life while their teeth were still developing. “Their strontium levels do not match the area where they were found,” says Westaway. This suggests they travelled considerable distances within the rainforest.
“It speaks to the ecological flexibility of Homo erectus,” says Karen Baab, a palaeoanthropologist at Midwestern University in Arizona. She points out that the species existed for about 1.9 million years and spread from Africa to Java. “They seemed quite adaptable and flexible in general, because of the incredible range of niches they were occupying.”
