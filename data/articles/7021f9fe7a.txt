Growing up in Scotland, our family was one of many who kept their photographs in an old shortbread tin. The lid usually showed a castle beside a loch, a stag, a Highland coo and plenty of tartan. It was always sunny.
Ma Wee Tin o’ Shortbreid is Scots for “my little tin of shortbread”. The title started as a warning to myself: don’t make the Highlands look like a tin of shortbread.
Then, at Eilean Donan Castle, about as biscuit-tin lid as it gets, I photographed a Chinese tourist under a wee umbrella, remaking the view for her own photograph.
The warning became the title.
They were shot to extinction in Britain, the last one in Shetland in 1918. Brought back from Norway in the 1970s. Now they are one of the island’s biggest draws. A bird once shot on sight is part of the sales pitch. The Scotland on the lid promises wilderness and wildlife. The real one comes with binoculars and a long wait.
One of the buses that leave Edinburgh every morning to do the Highlands in a day. Most of the day is spent on the bus. Glencoe seen through the window of a Heilan coo (Highland cow).
Some of that emptiness was made. Highland regiments fought the empire’s wars while the glens were cleared for sheep and sporting estates. The view people come for was somebody’s home.
Visitors arrive looking for a Scotland they’ve seen on screens. They come for wilderness and find a queue, a coach park and a wait for somebody to move out of the frame.
At the famous sites, I kept noticing how many visitors were Chinese and Indian, from countries Britain once ruled, traded with or fought. They were photographing the familiar symbols of old Scotland. This time, Scotland was the attraction, and they were the ones behind the camera.
Even the weather is for sale. Cool air and rain have become part of the escape. In what felt like London’s hottest summer ever, Londoners drove north to find the cold and wet, though a week of horizontal rain may test the sales pitch.
I’m making these photographs on repeated trips in my camper van: 60 days and 5,000 miles so far, a good part of it stuck behind a motorhome. I’ve been to Skye five times and driven the North Coast 500 (NC500) road in both directions. The project grew out of Weather Permitting, my photographs of Scotland’s ski centres.
Reaching the wilderness usually means driving to it, on the same narrow roads as residents, deliveries, coaches and motorhomes. An empty road makes a good advert. Everyone driving down it makes a different picture.
Tourism brings work and money. It also needs people who can afford to live close enough to keep the place running. Between No Motorhomes and No More Homes, it’s getting harder for anyone to stay.
The NC500 was launched in 2015 to sell 516 miles of empty road. It worked. Camper van life has its own pitch: freedom, independence, a new view every morning. In practice, it depends on ferry bookings, fresh water, waste points and somewhere you’re allowed to stop. Freedom, it turns out, needs a booking reference.
Travelling this way, I’m part of what I’m photographing, on the same roads, ferries and overnight stops as everyone else. The open road can still end at a full layby or a No Motorhomes sign. Passing places become car parks and viewpoints become campsites.
Over July and August one year, about 14,000 motorhomes and nearly 11,000 tents pitched up within five miles of Durness. A few hundred people live there … The NC500 has been a named tourist route for just over a decade. What will it look like in another 10 or 20 years? Part of making these photographs is recording this moment, before we find out.
The pictures are about the gap between Scotland as advertised and the place people find. I set out not to photograph the shortbread tin. I kept finding people trying to climb inside it.
