Analysis of a skeleton from Denmark, nicknamed “Rødhals Man”, hints that the individual lived through one of the most fundamental changes in human history: the transition from hunter-gathering to farming.
“What was it like to live through such a momentous transition, seeing the archipelago where your ancestors had fished being transformed by the arrival of a new group of people?” says Anders Fischer at the University of Gothenburg in Sweden.
Rødhals Man lived some 6000 years ago, and was discovered on the small island of Sejerø in Denmark in the 1950s, buried in a shell midden – an ancient accumulation of discarded sea shells and other domestic refuse. Previous investigations have indicated he was unusual. He lived some time between about 3800 and 3900 BC, near the start of the Neolithic period in the area, when diets changed to consist predominantly of terrestrial food produced by farmers. And yet Rødhals Man, with his genetic ancestry typical of the local Mesolithic Ertebølle culture of hunter-gatherers, didn’t seem to have eaten that diet: a study in 1981 indicated that he ate mainly seafood.
“There was something wrong and it is always when things don’t fit that you may be on the track of something important,” says Fischer.
To get a better idea of what was going on, Fischer and his colleagues have now looked at the levels of rare isotopes of elements including carbon, nitrogen and sulphur in several of the man’s bones, which renewed at different rates during his life, preserving different snapshots in time. The researchers also analysed the chemical signatures in one of his teeth, which preserve a record of diet from childhood. Collectively, the data gave the team an opportunity to look for clues about how Rødhals Man’s diet changed over the 30 to 40 years of his life.
“You can see that most of the time, the primary food source is what we call the eelgrass biotype. This is not eelgrass itself, but the animals that live in the thick forests of this sea grass in shallow water,” says team member John Meadows at Kiel University in Germany. This could have been animals like molluscs, flatfish or eels, he says.
But from the age of about 11 onwards, Rødhals Man’s diet became unlike that of any Mesolithic hunter-gatherer recorded so far, with increasing amounts of terrestrial, probably farmed, protein.
Rødhals Man’s burial also shares more similarities with burials of Early Neolithic farmers than those of Late Mesolithic hunter-gatherers, because his bones lacked an added coating of ochre staining and his grave featured a stone frame.
The evidence leads Meadows to suggest that Rødhals Man interacted with the Neolithic farmers and adopted some components of farmer culture while maintaining the way of life of his ancestors on the small island. Sejerø is just 12 square kilometres in area, but is only about 5 kilometres from Zealand, which is around 7000 square kilometres.
“He was on a tiny island with very little in the way of wild terrestrial resources to live on,” says Meadows. “He may have spent part of the year living in a farming community on Zealand and part living on the island and only eating fish.”
“It is quite amazing that all this information could be retrieved from one single skeleton,” says Mathias Currat at the University of Geneva. “I think this is an interesting story, which probably represents part of the main story in this area.”
It would be unusual to change diet in this way, says Marcello Mannino at Aarhus University in Denmark. “Such cultures were often very conservative in terms of their dietary habits, because diet was interlinked with the subsistence practices that shaped that culture together with religious and cosmological views of the world.”
He says it is likely that the Neolithic farmers probably first occupied areas where soil conditions were better, such as in central Zealand, and were slower to inhabit more marginal areas. “We need to find more human remains that span the transition to try to understand how it happened,” he says.
