Hayley Atwell is bringing the party to Broadway.
After a record-breaking run on the West End, which wrapped with the five highest-grossing weeks for a play in West End history, Jamie Lloyd’s take on “Much Ado About Nothing” has arrived at the Winter Garden starring Atwell and Tom Hiddleston as Beatrice and Benedick.
Teeming with modern, comical twists, the 400-year-old drama feels timeless and renewed with a cast that makes the performance feel like a never-ending party.
“I’m like, well, what is a big party for Broadway? A big party for Broadway is allowing audiences from the beginning, from as soon as they walk into the orchestra into the auditorium to go, ‘This is our party, and this is for us,’ and that we as actors on stage are hosting that party,” Atwell says, the morning of the show’s official opening. “And so everything has been about translating it to today’s audience in the way that it would have delivered back then 400 years ago.”
The show is Atwell’s Broadway debut, and it is hotly anticipated. For her performance in London, the 44-year-old received The Standard Theatre Award for Best Actress; Critics’ Circle Theatre Award for Best Shakespearean Performance, and a WhatsOnStage Award nomination for Best Actress in a play.
“We just walked into such an extraordinarily robust and energizing and galvanizing environment. To bring a show with the same people that we built it with a year and a half ago from a record-breaking West End run…having won the Critics Circle Award for Best Shakespearean performance for it and the Evening Stands Award…it was a sense of being able to have a debut on Broadway with the best chance of truly being able to connect with a Broadway audience,” she says. “It felt that Broadway had invited us. And so there was already a mutual, loving relationship, I think.”
Atwell and Hiddleston have known each other for more than 20 years; she credits their friendship with strengthening their chemistry, trust and ability to experiment in their performance.
“It’s like a tennis match between us. Every time I throw him a ball, he bats it back to me and he doesn’t miss anything that I do,” she says. “And that can really only come, I think, from both two people sharing a work ethic of really respecting the craft, respecting the language, respecting each other and having that trust, which means you can go to these places.”
As much as the play is a rhapsodic revival, Atwell contrasts the exuberant moments with sobering junctures that reveal the vulnerability her character is so skilled at masking, with her overly confident and unbothered posturing.
“Behind all the giddy, the silly, the fun, the joy, the humor, the comedy, the ridiculousness of it all was a woman who was deeply alone and hurt from past experience with Benedict,” Atwell says. “And she had developed this sort of impenetrable persona of bravado.”
“I just thought, ‘I don’t buy that.’ I think that’s all the defense,” she continues. “And so the challenge for me was making sure that she could be impressive and light and sophisticated, funny and silly and ridiculous. It meant that the higher I could go with those moments, the more it gave me to then bring her down to those moments of stillness and pain.”
It’s the moments between Beatrice and Benedick where her tenderness is revealed as she stops resisting her feelings.
“It’s about forgiveness and transcendence of that. And that’s where love can then come up. You know, that’s when love can manifest and like spread throughout the audience in a place of utter joy, because there is something bigger than the need to be right or wrong or to win a battle,” Atwell says. “There’s something much bigger than that, that’s going on by the end of the play, that seems to be, um, deeply felt by the audience and a deeply cathartic experience for the audience too.”
The show culminates in a moment of collective resolve and accountability between the characters before one final dance scene to celebrate.
“Joy is a radical act in this play, and giving the audiences an opportunity to sit beside the realities of their life and of our world as it is today in all of its horrors, and yet still defiantly experience the collective power of joy and love for that moment,” Atwell says.
“To get here at this stage in my life, but with this particular show, just feels like something that I will cherish for the rest of my career.”
