For almost a decade, the Kepler Space Telescope trailed Earth’s orbit, watching out for flickers in the light of distant stars. In 2011, two years after its launch, the NASA mission found what it was looking for: the signature of a roughly Earth-sized planet dimming out a tiny sliver of light as it transited across the face of its star.
The identification of Kepler-10b, a planet almost 1.5 times the size of Earth that orbits a star around 600 light years away, was the first time we had spotted a rocky exoplanet – a world beyond our solar system. Since then, our rapidly expanding exoplanet catalogue has revealed plenty of surprises, but one above all: most planets in our galaxy aren’t like Earth. In fact, they are like nothing in our solar system at all.
Out of the now 6451 confirmed exoplanets, by far the most common type are the “sub-Neptunes”, planets between Earth and Neptune in size that old theories of planet formation based on our solar system didn’t predict. And there are lots of them – over 3000 at the last count. “You know how evolution, like, really likes making crabs? Planet formation seems to really like making sub-Neptunes,” says astrophysicist Matthew Nixon at Arizona State University.
But there is no sub-Neptune in our solar system, and that leaves researchers with a puzzle: what exactly are these mysterious planets? Now, aided by NASA’s orbiting high-tech thermal camera, the James Webb Space Telescope (JWST), they are coming into sharper focus. New discoveries are answering some questions, but raising many more about what sub-Neptunes look like, how they form – and whether they might even host life.
Exoplanetary science is still young. Astronomers spotted the first planet beyond our solar system, a gas giant circling a pulsar, in 1992. In 1995 came the first exoplanet around a sun-like star, a giant, close-orbiting “hot Jupiter” called 51 Pegasi b. But the hunt didn’t hit its high season until Kepler launched in 2009. It flew with just one instrument: a photometer that could precisely measure stars’ brightness and catch the telltale flickers of passing planets. By the time the mission ran out of fuel in 2018, it had discovered over half of all exoplanets we know today.
Back in those early days, our ideas of how planets formed were guided by a sample of one: our solar system. It is widely agreed that our sun formed from the gravitational collapse of the solar nebula, a giant, spinning cloud of hydrogen and helium gas, plus a sprinkling of heavier elements. Once the young sun ignited, a disc of material continued circling it that would give rise to the planets.
Looking at our solar system, scientists broadly concluded that what planets look like came down to where they formed within this nebular Frisbee. Closer to the sun, it is too hot for anything but rock and metals to solidify and clump together, meaning less material to sweep up into planets. So, the planets of the inner solar system – Mercury, Venus, Earth and Mars – ended up small and rocky. Meanwhile, in longer orbits beyond the “snow line”, where compounds such as water, ammonia and methane freeze out and form ices, baby planetary cores could get bigger. These bigger cores had the gravitational heft to gobble up hydrogen and helium gas from the solar nebula. They formed the outer solar system’s two gas giants, Jupiter and Saturn, and its more distant “ice giants”, Uranus and Neptune.
Earth is the solar system’s largest rocky planet; Neptune, at around four times Earth’s diameter, is its smallest giant planet. The gap in size between the two seemed natural. Given how we thought planets form, worlds much bigger than Earth should be incredibly rare within the snow line. That, it turns out, isn’t true.
Exoplanet astronomers have found everything from alien gas titans that would dwarf Jupiter to planetary pebbles comparable to Mercury. But among confirmed exoplanets with a known radius, about half are sub-Neptunes, many of them hewing in warm orbits very close to their stars. “They’re really the ultimate mystery,” says exoplanet astronomer Eliza Kempton at the University of Chicago.
Kempton has been studying sub-Neptunes since before they were called sub-Neptunes. In 2010, even before Kepler found that first Earth-sized planet, she and her colleagues used the ground-based Very Large Telescope facility in Chile to observe the hazy atmosphere of a planet called GJ 1214 b some 48 light years away from Earth. With a radius 2.7 times that of Earth, but orbiting far closer to its star than Mercury does to the sun, that planet is now something of a poster child for the sub-Neptunes. But back then, it was a “super-Earth”.
“We just called everything a super-Earth,” says Kempton. “This was before we even knew that small planets bifurcate into, like, the ‘smaller’ class and the ‘bigger’ class.” That divide refers to a striking discovery made in 2017 that deepened the mystery of sub-Neptunes further still. When astronomers used Kepler data to chart the number of exoplanets of different sizes, it revealed two bumps. One was centred on planets with radii about 1.4 times Earth’s, and another on those of about 2.4 Earth radii. In between, there was a gap with relatively few planets. The sub-Neptunes appeared to be not just one population of planets, but two.
The discovery of this “radius valley” lent support to an idea that harsh radiation emitted from young stars, plus leftover heat from planet formation, blasts away the baby blankets of hydrogen and helium gas enveloping smaller planets. That leaves bare, rocky worlds that form the super-Earth peak. Bigger planets with more gravity, meanwhile, hold onto at least some hydrogen and helium. They end up as puffier, gas-cloaked worlds in the sub-Neptune peak.
In this picture, terrestrial planets like Earth might be the naked cores of erstwhile sub-Neptunes. “If you’re ultimately interested in more Earth-like worlds, sub-Neptunes represent a kind of arrested stage of development. These are the things that stop short of becoming something like Earth,” says planetary scientist Raymond Pierrehumbert at the University of Oxford. “I think of them almost like fossilised atmospheres.”
Unlike the ice giants of our solar system, sub-Neptunes close to their stars might form pretty dry – as “gas dwarfs” with no ice, just a rocky interior and a thick gas envelope made almost entirely of hydrogen and helium. But there are other possibilities. The discovery of hot Jupiters – gas giant planets in close orbits where they couldn’t possibly have formed – helped scientists realise that planets can migrate, something we now know probably happened in our solar system, too. Sub-Neptunes could be ice worlds that form beyond their planetary system’s snow line and wander inwards. In some simulations, this migration produces a radius valley: one that divides small, rocky planets from big, watery ones.
Since JWST’s launch in 2021, astronomers have been able to start differentiating between such scenarios. Its great infrared eye sees in wavelengths of light at which molecules such as water, ammonia, carbon dioxide and methane leave distinctive fingerprints on starlight passing through exoplanets’ upper atmospheres.
In at least one case, that of the hot sub-Neptune TOI-421 b, which orbits its sun-like star far closer than Mercury does our sun, JWST observations largely match what you would expect for a gas dwarf. But observations are revealing that sub-Neptunes are a diverse group (see “Five ways to make a sub-Neptune”, below). If some of them are worlds with abundant water, it would be a big deal for the search for life. “Where you can find water, you can find habitable conditions,” says astrophysicist Caroline Piaulet-Ghorayeb also at the University of Chicago. Finding sub-Neptune water worlds “would kind of change our thinking about habitability”, she says. Earth-sized planets are very hard to observe in detail with JWST, so bigger water worlds could be our best immediate hope for finding signs of life on an exoplanet.
The mere presence of water doesn’t make a habitable planet. Sub-Neptunes could be balls of exotic, high-pressure ice and “supercritical” fluids that are neither liquid nor gas – mini ice giants, not ocean worlds. And because hydrogen is a strong greenhouse gas, sub-Neptunes with hydrogen atmospheres can end up as boiling “steam worlds” with atmospheres rich in water vapour. JWST observations suggest that a watery sub-Neptune called GJ 9827 d is such a planet.
However, in the lead-up to JWST’s launch, astrophysicist Nikku Madhusudhan at the University of Cambridge and his colleagues showed that water worlds with thin hydrogen atmospheres could have liquid water on their surfaces (too thick an atmosphere, and the water boils). Madhusudhan later dubbed these planets “Hycean” worlds, a portmanteau of “hydrogen” and “ocean”. JWST observations have hinted that a sub-Neptune called K2-18 b orbiting a star some 125 light years from Earth might belong to this group. It sits in its star’s habitable zone, where it is theoretically cool enough for surface liquid water.
This plot thickened in 2023, when Madhusudhan’s team spotted what it interpreted as tentative evidence for dimethyl sulphide (DMS) in K2-18 b’s atmosphere. On Earth, DMS is made only by life – primarily plankton in the ocean. The team doubled down after follow-up JWST observations, but the result remains fiercely disputed. Nixon objects that the data isn’t enough to uniquely identify a molecule as complex as DMS. “A lot of other molecules, if you were to test them, you would also get a comparable result,” he says. Madhusudhan stands by the detection. He argues that researchers have historically accepted detections of more mundane gases like CO2 based on comparable evidence.
If there is DMS on K2-18 b, it is possible that an unknown chemical process might produce it, rather than alien microbes. Madhusudhan says he would “absolutely” be happy either way. “The discovery of a new chemical process in a planetary atmosphere? I mean, that’s a huge deal,” he says. “To me, that is as good as finding life.”
But even the identification of K2-18 b as a Hycean world is disputed. What scientists see in sub-Neptunes’ atmospheres depends in large part on what happens out of view, deep in the planets’ hot, high-pressure interiors. The mix of molecules JWST has spotted in K2-18 b’s atmosphere matches a planet with water oceans – but might also fit a gaseous mini-Neptune without a real surface, or a gas dwarf with a magma ocean simmering below its hydrogen envelope.
Earth once had a magma ocean, too. Rocky worlds go through an initial molten phase with a global magma ocean before they cool down and solidify. Some sub-Neptunes might trap enough primordial heat below blankets of gas and icy material that their magma oceans never freeze – with significant consequences. “Magma oceans are a chemical factory,” says Pierrehumbert. “Chemicals can get in there and react and do all sorts of stuff.” One reason to think K2-18 b is a watery world is the lack of ammonia in its atmosphere: ammonia dissolves in water. But it also dissolves in liquid rock. So ultimately, researchers need to learn more about K2-18 b’s atmosphere to pin down its composition.
Magma oceans do more than conceal compounds such as ammonia – they “fundamentally change the composition of the atmosphere”, says Kempton. Reactions between hydrogen and magma could even produce abundant water on planets that were born dry, without any water, she adds. This process might have produced some of Earth’s water back when it had a magma ocean.
Magma isn’t the only thing that might be smouldering deep within sub-Neptunes. A study led by Kempton’s University of Chicago colleague Jeehyun Yang recently found that sub-Neptunes could be a bit like combustion engines, burning carbon-rich compounds to produce soot. A layer of these planets might be, rather literally, on fire, says Yang. Clouds of black soot belched up from below would block out some molecular signals, perhaps explaining a pattern of muted molecular fingerprints in JWST data from several sub-Neptunes.
Some worlds might make soot; others might actually be made of it. Just as there is a snow line beyond which water is solid, there is a “soot line” around young stars where organic carbon molecules can start to form grains of black, tarry material. Sub-Neptunes are reviving an old idea that this could create “soot worlds”, says planetary scientist Zifan Lin at Washington University in St. Louis, Missouri. Lin and his colleague Sara Seager at the University of Toronto recently showed that soot worlds with cores of rock and metal, overlain by layers of diamond and graphite and with hydrogen-rich atmospheres, could match JWST observations of three sub-Neptunes, including K2-18 b.
So, gas dwarfs, water worlds, soot planets – what’s the answer? The boundaries might be fuzzier than they sound. Recent work by Piaulet-Ghorayeb and her colleagues has shown that some sub-Neptunes that appear dry could hide water deep in their interiors, where it is invisible to telescopes. And there could be sooty water worlds, sooty gas dwarfs and everything in between.
One lesson sub-Neptunes have already taught scientists is that planetary evolution, not just formation, determines what worlds are made of. Losing an atmosphere or reacting one with a magma ocean, say, shapes a planet just as profoundly as forming beyond or within the snow line. Nixon says he is working on finding ways to distinguish between water worlds that wandered inwards and sub-Neptunes that formed dry and later made their own water. To this end, he is observing a hot sub-Neptune called HD 86226 that is virtually guaranteed to have a magma ocean, eliminating some guesswork.
Ideally, scientists would eventually like to understand whether sub-Neptunes are all basically variations on the same planetary theme, or are many sub-groups with different histories. Observations so far have pinpointed only a few molecules in a handful of sub-Neptunes’ atmospheres. Surveys of more sub-Neptunes are under way, and these could help pick out different groups if they exist.
The more we learn about sub-Neptunes, the more they surprise us, making them one of the most exciting active frontiers in exoplanetary science. We still don’t know why our solar system lacks one of these abundant worlds, but they are the closest thing to a world like Earth that we can observe easily at present. Through them, we learn not only about the broader universe, but also about the forces that sculpt worlds like our own. “The sub-Neptunes give us that next stepping stone,” says Kempton. “We’re kind of marching our way towards the study of Earth-like worlds in the future.”
