California voters pass billionaire's measure to bankroll his own medical lab
California voters passed a ballot measure backed by a billionaire to fund $8.4 billion in immunology research. Half the funding will go to the research group funded by the same billionaire.
A MARTÍNEZ, HOST:
California voters rejected a proposal to fund healthcare by taxing billionaires, but they approved a ballot measure from a Southern California billionaire asking taxpayers to bankroll his new medical lab. From member station KQED, April Dembosky has more on Proposition 38.
APRIL DEMBOSKY, BYLINE: This fall, Marianne O'Malley was at one of her regular boxing classes for people with Parkinson's. Her classmates asked her if they should vote for Prop 38.
MARIANNE O'MALLEY: We're all desperate for a cure or at least treatment.
DEMBOSKY: Exercise is the top therapy proven to slow progression of the disease. Her classmates were excited by Prop 38 and its promise to find new immunotherapy treatments for Parkinson's, Alzheimer's and cancer.
O'MALLEY: The disappointing thing is to tell them, no, this measure doesn't do what you think it's going to do.
DEMBOSKY: O'Malley worked for 30 years at the state Legislative Analyst's Office in Sacramento. When she dug into the fine print of Prop 38, she says she was horrified. The measure authorizes the state to borrow $8.4 billion to fund immunology research. But half the money is slated for one institute, backed by the same billionaire who put the measure on the ballot.
O'MALLEY: This measure hijacks the initiative process to steer public funds to a billionaire's philanthropic project.
DEMBOSKY: But other patients see merit in Prop 38. Laurie Adami was diagnosed with blood cancer 20 years ago. She suffered through multiple treatments, including grueling rounds of chemo.
LAURIE ADAMI: So chemotherapy is like the napalm. So they poison your whole body in the hopes of killing off your cancer, but there's a lot of collateral damage.
DEMBOSKY: In 2018, she enrolled in a clinical trial for an immunotherapy called CAR-T. Doctors extracted her immune cells and re-engineered them to attack her cancer. Put another way, these immune cells are like Pac-Mans. And scientists altered them so they'd be hungry only for Adami's cancer cells, then infused them back into her body to start eating.
ADAMI: So this idea of waking up the immune system in a much more elegant way to target the cancers.
DEMBOSKY: Adami had one CAR-T treatment and has been cancer-free ever since.
ADAMI: Why wouldn't I want more patients to be able to access the type of treatment that finally saved my life?
DEMBOSKY: But immunotherapy doesn't work for everyone. Only 15% to 20% of patients have durable results. Clinical trials in general are largely speculative. Only 14% of experimental drugs ever reach FDA approval. That's why another cancer survivor, Dan Carson, is suspicious of the claims that Prop 38 will, quote, "pay for itself" by returning 10% of licensing fees from new discoveries back to California.
DAN CARSON: I am here to tell you that won't happen. Not even close.
DEMBOSKY: Carson says the odds of finding enough successful new treatments are too low. He's also a retired legislative analyst. He says supporters of an earlier bond measure to fund stem cell research made the same claim that it would be cost-neutral, but the state only received a tiny fraction of the billions it was promised in returns.
CARSON: So here we go again with Prop 38.
DEMBOSKY: The billionaire behind the measure, Gary Michelson, is not doing interviews. But his campaign says the global immunotherapy market is worth more than $200 billion, and it's growing fast. They're confident the research will generate plenty of revenue to repay the bond with interest.
For NPR News, I'm April Dembosky in San Francisco.
