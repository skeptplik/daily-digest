When Jeff Clinton, an American visiting Rome with his wife for the first time, found a US one-cent coin on a street close to Termini train station, he knew precisely what he was going to do with it. “I’ll throw it into the Trevi fountain,” said Clinton, holding the copper coin in the palm of his hand as the couple admired the landmark.
He was well aware of the tradition among tourists of tossing a coin over their shoulder and into the waters of the baroque masterpiece, in a ritual fabled to ensure a return visit to the city or make other dreams come true.
But the only thing holding him back from partaking in the gesture was the distance: the steps towards the fountain’s sunken basin are separated by a low barrier and accessible during the day only by Rome’s residents or visitors who pay €2 (£1.64).
The fee, in place since February, has been partly blamed for taking some of the allure out of the coin-throwing tradition. The Rome unit of the church-run charity Caritas, which sweeps up the Trevi fountain’s coins, usually brings in more than €1.4m a year. But it noted a shortfall of about €200,000 between January and September compared with the same period in 2025.
“The closure of the direct access to the Trevi fountain might be a factor,” Giustino Trincia, the director of Caritas Roma, told Il Messaggero. The charity uses the donations to fund food banks and other welfare projects.
Clinton did contemplate asking someone in the basin area to throw the coin into the fountain on his behalf, but in the end slipped it back into his pocket.
The shift towards contactless payments and mobile phone wallets is also believed to have eroded Caritas’s collections.
Eric and Patty, a young couple from New York, said they might have thrown a coin, but did not have any change. Neither did they want to pay the €2 fee to get close to the basin. “It’s such a breathtaking monument, we’re happy just to take it in from here,” said Patty as they posed for selfies.
Alan and Ying, visiting Rome from Australia, did pay the fee. While they had heard that throwing coins “is what people do”, they decided not to indulge because “nobody else was really doing it”.
“I always carry cash with me because you never know when you might need it,” said Ying. “But we didn’t see anyone else doing it, so we didn’t either.”
A spokesperson for Caritas Rome told the Guardian the charity was not making any further public statements about the dwindling coin collection. The spokesperson said the collection so far this year did not take into account foreign currency coins, so the hit might not be so bad.
Still, the matter is understood to have caused friction with Rome’s council, which generated €435,194 from the access fee within the first month of its inception.
The initiative is aimed at curbing overcrowding at the monument while raising funds for the upkeep of the fountain and other Rome monuments. No further figures have been published, but with 4.4 million tourists flocking to the Trevi in 2025, the fee had been forecast to generate a tidy income for the council each year.
A team of stewards staff the ticketing system and monitor the flow of the crowd in the basin area. Nicolas Romero does shifts of seven or eight hours. He said people quite often asked him for change in order to throw a coin. “But many don’t even seem to know about the tradition,” he said. “This might also explain the decline.”
The gesture is believed to have its origins in an ancient pagan ritual offering to the water gods, although the custom of throwing coins only really took off globally after the 1954 romantic comedy film, Three Coins in the Fountain.
