Five EU countries have announced a controversial plan to set up a centre in Rwanda for migrants with no legal right to stay in Europe. While supporters say it will help governments to deter illegal immigration, critics are concerned about potential human rights violations and a lack of oversight.
Austria, Denmark, Germany, Greece and the Netherlands have announced plans to set up a return hub in Rwanda, which they say will allow the EU to secure control of its borders.
The group of five want to start deporting rejected asylum seekers — and those who have exhausted all appeal options against their removal — to Rwanda in 2027. Today's Euronews Verify episode takes a closer look at the measure.
The details of the return hubs are still being ironed out, but the first is expected to accommodate around 100 people with no legal right to stay in the EU at a time, in a pilot run of the model.
Those moved to Rwanda will be free to live and work there or return to their home countries.
Announcing the plan on 16 November, Denmark's Prime Minister Mette Frederiksen said that the move will serve as a strong deterrent for anyone looking to enter the EU illegally.
"The signal that we are sending, that you can no longer enter the European Union illegally and think that you can just stay around ... will prevent a lot and ensure that thousands of people will think it through before starting their journey into Europe," she said.
Rwanda has also hailed the plan as a way to "protect the right to asylum while treating migrants with dignity at every stage".
What don't we know and what are the concerns?
There's still a lot we don't know, however, and critics are concerned about the lack of detail given in the announcement of the Rwanda plan.
"We don't know how much it will cost; we don't know what Rwanda has been offered in exchange; we don't know what guarantees exist for people; we don't know how the courts will intervene; we don't know what protections will be in place; and we don't know who's legally responsible if something goes wrong," Olivia Sundberg, EU advocate on migration and asylum at Amnesty International, told Euronews Verify.
While both the EU countries in question and Rwanda itself assure that the hubs will comply with EU, international and human rights law, critics and experts are less convinced.
They've also raised concerns about the hubs' ability to even deter illegal immigrants, as such measures, they say, only force people to take more dangerous trips to Europe rather than giving up the plan entirely.
Can nuclear play a part in Europe's energy transition?
Also on today's episode of Euronews Verify is a look at whether Europe is heading for a nuclear revival.
As the COP climate summit in Turkey wraps up, nuclear energy has been a particularly big elephant in the room, complete with nuclear-related events on the sidelines.
From calls to triple capacity by 2050 to countries reconsidering their phase-outs, there is a growing push to bring nuclear back to the table as a low-carbon source.
Soaring energy bills in Europe are also presenting the industry with an opportunity to turn political momentum into climate legitimacy.
"If you want to have cheap energy, it's going to be very, very difficult to do it without nuclear power," Damien Erst, electromechanics professor at the University of Liège, told Euronews Verify.
"Especially in a context where gas is becoming extremely expensive, and in a context where foreign powers also use our dependency on energy imports to threaten and weaken the EU."
Several countries are already weighing up their options, with Italy giving the strongest indication that it's time for change: the government just passed new legislation this year to pave the way for a return to nuclear energy after decades without it.
Spain and Belgium, meanwhile, have postponed reactor shutdowns; the Netherlands is planning new units; and Poland and Estonia are laying the groundwork for their first nuclear plants.
