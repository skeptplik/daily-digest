While average global temperatures continue to set records through one of the strongest El Niños on record, temperatures across a large swathe of Europe could plunge this week, as Arctic air sweeps in.
On Friday, the global average temperature was 2.12C higher than the preindustrial average, the biggest ever anomaly and equivalent to 1.22C above the 1991-2020 climate average.
But in parts of Europe, high pressure building over Scandinavia and low pressure shifting south-east into western Russia will open a corridor of cold air from the north. Temperatures in the Baltics and in countries such as Germany, Poland and Ukraine are likely to fall widely below zero from Monday, with ice days – in which temperatures stay below 0C all day – expected from midweek. By the weekend, temperatures could be 5-15C below average, reaching double-digit negatives on higher ground.
The low pressure over eastern Europe and western Russia will bring extensive snowfall. Southern Poland, Belarus, eastern Ukraine and south-eastern parts of the Baltics will be the first to see widespread snow, which could reach up to 10cm in southern Poland.
Later in the week, as a smaller area of low pressure pushes into western and central Europe, significant snow is expected to spread across southern Germany, Austria and Czechia, where depths of 10-20cm could build. The Alps could get a significant dumping of snow, a welcome boost for the region’s ski resorts which will open in the coming weeks.
Into the weekend and the start of December, high pressure is set to remain dominant across northern Europe, maintaining below-average temperatures in central and eastern Europe and possibly extending into the Balkans. There are also hints that the sudden stratospheric warming earlier this month could finally begin influencing the lower atmosphere, with high pressure extending farther west and perhaps bringing cold easterly winds into western Europe and the UK, although confidence on this is low.
The cold in Europe is in stark contrast to the global picture. This October was the warmest on record, with an average global temperature of 15.35C, 0.9C above the 1991-2020 average and 1.75C above the estimated preindustrial level. It was the third month in a row above 1.5C, following August and September, and while parts of Europe, including the UK, have seen below-average temperatures so far this November, global temperatures this month have continued to run above average.
