Introduction
Nothing really exists in isolation. Almost every part of our lives exists in a network. Consciousness arises from what neurons do together, life from what genes and proteins do together, society from what we do together. When researchers began mapping real-world networks in the 1990s – from the World Wide Web to Hollywood actors linked by films – they found that wildly different systems shared architectural features that their components alone could not explain. This suggested common organizing principles for how networks form and develop.
In this episode, two of the pioneers of network science – Albert-László Barabási and co-host Steven Strogatz – discuss how the field took shape, the ideas that underpin it, and why this is more than graph theory under a different name. The conversation then turns to health and nutrition. Disease-related genes cluster in “network neighborhoods”, Barabási explains, which may help identify better drug targets and predict which patients will respond to which drugs. That work led him to his Foodome project, which catalogs the molecules found in the food we eat and asks what they do inside our cells. By his count, around 135,000 compounds lie outside conventional nutrition, which concerns itself mainly with energy, building blocks, and vitamins. These compounds, which he calls the “dark matter of nutrition,” and their effects on the body are only beginning to be understood.
