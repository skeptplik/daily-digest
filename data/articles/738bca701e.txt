The Rohingya question has returned to the centre of international attention following the landmark judgment delivered by the International Court of Justice (ICJ) on November 19, 2026. Yet, for more than a million Rohingya living outside Myanmar, legal recognition is only one part of a much larger struggle. The fundamental question remains: how does a community that has been systematically marginalised, displaced and denied its identity reclaim its place in the country it considers home? The crisis also exposes the international community’s inability to translate diplomatic declarations, humanitarian assistance and legal proceedings into a sustainable political settlement.
When I began working on Myanmar in the United Nations Department of Political Affairs in 2011, the Rohingya question was largely confined to perfunctory diplomatic talking points. Although concerns about discrimination, communal tensions and citizenship were acknowledged, there appeared to be limited understanding in many diplomatic discussions of the geographical and postcolonial nuances underlying the crisis.
This began to change with the outbreak of communal violence in May 2012, which dealt an early blow to Myanmar’s fragile democratisation process. The developments gradually compelled interlocutors to recognise that the country’s transition was not simply about moving away from military rule or introducing electoral reforms. It also involved confronting deeply entrenched postcolonial challenges, competing historical narratives, unresolved questions of identity and citizenship, and the complex relationship between the centre and its ethnic peripheries.
The Rohingya question could not be understood simply as another episode of communal tension between religious communities. It was deeply embedded in the history of colonial-era migration, the changing political geography of the Arakan region, competing narratives of belonging and the post-independence construction of citizenship in Myanmar. The historical relationship between the territories that now constitute Bangladesh and Myanmar, population movements during British colonial rule and the subsequent politicisation of identity were essential to understanding the dispute. The tendency to reduce the issue to a humanitarian concern or communal violence obscured these deeper historical and political dimensions.
