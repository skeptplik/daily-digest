Anglo-Australian miner Rio Tinto has agreed to pay a quarter-of-a-billion dollars to begin the clean-up of the toxic Panguna mine site it abandoned more than three decades ago.
Panguna, once one of the world’s most profitable gold and copper mines, was shuttered in 1989 after violent protests over environmental damage and the inequitable division of the mine’s profits sparked a decade-long civil war on the Papua New Guinean island of Bougainville.
But the sprawling mine pit, once a mountain, remains a scar in the middle of the island, with vast pillars of rubble at risk of collapse, and polluted rivers running bright blue with toxins.
Rio Tinto was forced from the island and has never returned, but in recent years has been part of a detailed consultation process with landowners and government. It has now agreed to voluntarily contribute US$250m (A$370m) towards the remediation of the mine site.
Theonila Roka Matbob, a former cabinet minister in Bougainville’s autonomous government and Panguna community leader, said the fund was a vital contribution towards repairing the “devastation left by the Panguna mine”.
“We have a lot of work still ahead of us, but the establishment of this fund gives us the chance to find solutions to the huge problems we have been living with since the days of the mine’s operation.
“No amount of money will restore the damage caused to the environment in Panguna, but this sets a foundation for us to invest in addressing the environmental issues and invest in our children’s futures as well.”
Roka Matbob said the most fundamental remediation was to improve “accessibility” for the people living around Panguna – “accessibility to clean drinking water that is safe and secure … and infrastructure, in bridges that makes people’s access to trade, to school, to health[care], much easier. This is one critical area that immediately needs attention.”
The president of Bougainville, Ishmael Toroama, seeking to become the world’s newest head of state at the forefront of a long-running independence movement on the island, said the money would make a “meaningful difference” to villages and communities that had suffered from the impacts of the mine’s pollution.
“This work will deliver benefits not only for people today, but for future generations. Continued engagement with our communities will be essential to ensuring the work responds to local priorities and delivers lasting outcomes.”
The Rio commitment has emerged from the Panguna Mine Legacy Impact Assessment consultation process, which was initiated in response to a human rights complaint brought by the Australian-based Human Rights Law Centre, with Roka Matbob as the lead complainant.
Keren Adams, the HRLC’s legal director, said establishing the fund had taken years of work.
“It is a testament to the courage and persistence of community leaders, and the willingness of all parties to engage in difficult but constructive dialogue,” she said.
“The significant funding contributed by Rio Tinto creates a real opportunity to now address an environmental disaster that has remained unresolved for decades.”
Mark Davies, Rio Tinto’s chief safety and technical officer, said the establishment of the fund was an “important milestone … intended to support the transition from the assessment to practical remedy implementation”.
“Community engagement is an important next step to identify priorities and we remain committed to working with communities to shape the activities supported by the fund.”
The quarter-of-a-billion dollars will not be sufficient to entirely remediate the mine site, but it is being seen as a major step towards rehabilitation.
The funding commitment for Panguna comes at an acutely sensitive time for the restive Bougainville island.
The mine has become inextricably linked with the Bougainville’s political future: restarting operations at Panguna – believed to still hold at least $60bn in unrealised reserves – is seen by many, including the president, as the fundamental financial basis for the island’s independent future. Indian miner Lloyds has been granted a 25-year large-scale mining lease, but any future mining is years from starting.
Rio Tinto and other international investors earned nearly $2bn from Panguna over 17 years of operation from 1972. But less than 1% of the mine’s profits went to Bougainvilleans, whose homes and lands were destroyed by it.
Disaffected landowners ran a sabotage campaign that forced Panguna’s closure in 1989. The PNG government responded by sending in police, then soldiers, to restore law and order. Bougainville was blockaded and the island descended into a brutal civil war – known as “the crisis” – that ran for a decade. Between 15,000 and 20,000 people were killed, roughly 10% of the island’s population.
A recent environmental impact assessment found the abandoned mine remains an acute threat to people’s lives, including from landslides, levee collapses and floods. Untouched, the river delta will stay polluted for a century.
The future of Bougainville’s independence campaign is now before the PNG parliament, which will decide whether or not to recognise a UN-backed independence referendum held on the island in 2019, in which 97.7% of voters were in favour of Bougainville seceding and becoming an independent country.
The PNG parliament, which heard lengthy and impassioned debate over Bougainville last month, appears almost certain to vote against recognising the referendum result.
But Toroama, who has staked his presidency on achieving political independence, has said, regardless of the parliament’s decision, Bougainville will enter a period of “self-government” from September next year, before seceding, breaking away from PNG as a fully fledged independent nation, by 2030.
