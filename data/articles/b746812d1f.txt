Military analyst Phillips O'Brien on Ukraine and Europe being left out of Trump's talks
As President Trump met with Russian and Chinese counterparts, Ukraine and its European allies were left out of the talks. NPR's Scott Simon talks with Phillips O'Brien of St. Andrew's University.
SCOTT SIMON, HOST:
Two summits this week on two sides of the world. President Trump was in China with leader Xi Jinping and Russian President Vladimir Putin, as European leaders gathered in Berlin with President Volodymyr Zelenskyy to show their support for Ukraine. We're joined now by Phillips O'Brien, professor of strategic studies at University of St. Andrews in Scotland. Professor, thanks for being back with us.
PHILLIPS O'BRIEN: I'm (ph) glad to be here.
SIMON: What do you think we learned from the two summits this week?
O'BRIEN: Well, we've learned that the United States is now officially much closer to Russia than it is to Ukraine or its European partners and that the United States' goal is to now bully Ukraine into giving up territory and people to Russia so that Putin gets a very good deal. The other thing we've learned is that Ukraine's support now is going to come from Europe and Europe alone, that the United States - oh, and Canada. But that, you know, it is up to Europe now to work with Ukraine to fight Russia off. And that's the reality. It's been the reality for a while, but that's what we actually had confirmed this week. And that was a useful thing.
SIMON: Is Europe up to it?
O'BRIEN: It is, certainly. I mean, Europe is - you would say, if you stepped back, Europe's more than capable of helping Ukraine. It has the money. It has the technology. It has the people. It has the brainpower. It has the engineering. What it doesn't have is the ability to think strategically on its own. It's become, in some ways, infantilized by the United States over the past 80 years. The United States did so much of its strategic thinking. And Europeans are panicked, really, when faced with the reality of having to do it without the USA. They are slowly adjusting, but the real issue's not material. It's mental.
SIMON: Ukraine carried out a major attack this week on Russia. They hit a bridge linking Russia with the occupied Crimea Peninsula. And Russia retaliated with deadly missile strikes in Volodymyr Zelenskyy's hometown yesterday. What does this tell us about the military capabilities of both countries?
O'BRIEN: Well, what we know - Russia's plan is to try and break Ukrainian civilian will this winter by committing war crimes on a daily basis. And that's been the - what they've been doing for a while. So they're going to try to kill civilians. They're trying to freeze civilians. They're trying to cut power. They are going to try and make Ukraine as unlivable as possible. And they have been helped a great deal by the United States because the United States has deprived Ukraine of air defense. And right now, by the way, the United States and - through Starlink and Starshield is actually hampering Ukraine's ability to defend itself by shooting down drones. So the Russian strategy's quite straightforward in - when we see it in action.
What the Ukrainians are trying to do is, in some ways, something far more ambitious and actually, by the way, legal. And that is they're trying to cut the flow of supplies to occupied western Ukraine. It's very hard for the Russians to get supplies into Crimea and then occupied parts of Kherson and Zaporizhzhia. And so what the Ukrainians seem to be trying to do is to isolate those regions and make it very difficult for the Russians to supply them.
SIMON: The U.S. envoy Jared Kushner said he believes that Russia - his phrase was comfortable - is comfortable with...
O'BRIEN: (Laughter).
SIMON: ...NATO-like - well, you anticipate the question...
O'BRIEN: (Laughter).
SIMON: ...NATO-like security guarantees for Ukraine...
O'BRIEN: Yeah.
SIMON: ...As a way to end the war. Does this strike you as a plausible proposal for negotiations?
O'BRIEN: Well, there's two things to say. NATO-like is nothing. Yeah, there's either NATO or not NATO. And, you know, NATO-like can be whatever they want it to be. But the key thing is Kushner's desperate to get a deal because they understand what's coming down the pike with the new Congress. And by the way, this was - they're saying it, oddly, to the Ukrainians and Europeans, who are then reporting it to the press, that what's going on is they - that, you know, Kushner and Witkoff and the Americans are saying, we need a deal by January 3 because we're going to have less power when the Democrats take over Congress. But this is simply posing. It's arguments, you know, that Putin is not going to accept anything like a real security guarantee for Ukraine. But it's the Trump administration's desperation to get something for Putin before the Democrats take over Congress.
SIMON: It's been almost five years since Russia launched the full-scale invasion of Ukraine. It's a long time for Ukraine to hold out, isn't it? What's your sense of where the war might be heading?
O'BRIEN: Well, it certainly is a long time, and they've done a remarkable job. And from the Ukrainian perspective, when you talk to them, their view is, we have no option. I mean, if we're - if we so - don't hold out, we'll be dead. I mean, it's quite clear that the Russians don't care about their lives, don't care about their cultures or societies. So resisting isn't really an option to them. They have to resist, or they will be wiped out.
The thing that is very frustrating, and I got this from when I was last in Ukraine and talking to them, is they feel, in many ways, the war is heading in their favor. They're more adaptable. They're doing better on the front line. They were doing a very successful strike campaign against Russian oil refineries and logistics. But then the United States has, you know, come out and switched sides. And so the United States is providing a huge lifeline to Putin, which is really helping the Russians by leaving Ukrainian cities defenseless. And that's the reality of that - what they face.
SIMON: Phillips O'Brien, professor at University of St. Andrews in Scotland. Thank you so much for being with us.
O'BRIEN: Thank you.
