In short:
Matildas vice-captain Steph Catley will play her 150th game tomorrow night.
She'll become the seventh player to reach the milestone.
What's next?
Australia will face Colombia in friendlies in Canberra and Geelong.
It was almost imperceptible.
The slightest shake of her head as Steph Catley listened to Matildas coach Joe Montemurro describe her as one of the world's best footballers.
It was as if she disagreed with the label. But there is no disputing the veteran defender's standing in the game.
She is among the world's elite and one of Australia's greatest ever, as a player, leader, and humble champion.
Against Colombia in Canberra tomorrow night, she will become just the seventh Matilda to reach 150 caps; three other members of that club are in this squad.
"This team feels like my family. We've grown up together, and I'm honoured every single time I touch the pitch with these girls, and I wear that jersey," Catley said.
"So 150 times is massive, and I'm very, very proud of that."
Catley has been at the heart of this generation that has driven women's football in Australia at full throttle, from the shadows into the spotlight.
"I think the work that the previous Matildas have put into this team and women's football has been incredible," she said.
"But this group has done so much for the game, for this team, for women's sport in general.
"There's still a long way to go, but what we've achieved so far has been incredible."
Catley is a fierce presence on the pitch, whether playing for the Matildas or Arsenal, and, at 32, is still very much in her prime.
But when she took the time to reflect on her career, the tears welled up in her eyes.
She said the emotion made it hard to talk about, but she regained her composure and did it anyway.
"I'll never take it for granted that I'm able to represent this team and play for my country," she said.
"And even representing Arsenal, it's everything that I dreamed of as a kid. It's everything I've worked my entire life for, and 150 caps is massive.
"And you do get to a point where every day gets a little bit harder on your body and mentally. But I think being around the girls and the fact that we are like a family … I want to be the best version of myself for them.
"That's what drives me, I think, and my family as well. They're all the way over here in Australia, and I've played overseas pretty much my entire adult life.
"So those sacrifices are big, and I think you want to make it worth it in the long run."
Montemurro has been a constant in the Catley journey, from coaching her in the A-League Women, to Arsenal, to the national side.
His affection for her as a player and person is clear.
"I've had the honour and the pleasure of working with what I think is one of the world's best footballers, but more importantly, as a person," he said.
"We should be proud as a country to have someone of the calibre of Steph Catley in our midst and growing up and hopefully still with us for a while.
"But what she's given on the field and off the field, I think, has been absolutely special and very unique in the world of football."
Catley, like so many members of this Matildas' golden generation, has achieved so much but is still missing a major international trophy.
Her tearful interview after losing the Women's Asian Cup final in March was filled with the feeling of a missed opportunity.
Time has helped reignite the fire to try again.
"We really could have won that tournament, so I think that was tough for us to take," she said.
"It just builds that hunger for the next chance that we get, another step to build towards what we want to achieve."
