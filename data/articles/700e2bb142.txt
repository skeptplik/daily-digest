Part of what makes Grand Theft Auto 6 feel like a major cultural moment is that there’s never been a game quite like it. There are plenty of open-world games that share a similar structure, but there’s very little in the way of big-budget sandbox crime simulators that balance rich storytelling with emergent chaos. If you want to play a game like GTA in 2026, your only real option is GTA.
That wasn’t the case in the series’ PlayStation 2 heyday. The success of games like GTA 3 gave rise to a whole host of copycats. Some of them were fairly good, giving us franchises like Saints Row and Mafia. Others, like Crime Life: Gang Wars, were downright terrible. My favorite of the bunch, which turned 20 this month, very much falls into the latter category: 2006’s The Sopranos: Road to Respect, THQ’s ill-fated attempt to give the HBO phenomenon the GTA treatment.
Did it review well? No. Did it sell well? No. Has it held up surprisingly well? Also no. But is it a hidden gem of the GTA copycat era that deserves more respect for pushing a level of storytelling that quietly paved the way for GTA 6’s nuanced characters and Hollywood style? Look, it’s going to be a no any way you slice this pie, but that won’t stop me from telling you how much I am ride or die for Italian Yakuza.
Released in 2006, right as The Sopranos was careening towards its final season, Road to Respect was about as much of a billion-dollar idea that you could ask for at the time. GTA mania was riding high after the series dropped three bangers in rapid succession, and it felt like the entire world was following Tony Soprano’s every move with bated breath. The idea of combining both franchises, roping in the series’ iconic actors to reprise their roles, felt like the kind of idea that could not possibly fail.
Or, at least it does now. While video games based on successful IP tend to be handled with great care today, that’s a relatively recent phenomenon. Road to Respect launched instead during an era where licensed games were usually a mark of low quality. The best you could hope for was something like Enter the Matrix, a rough action game that delivered just enough to earn it a cult status with franchise diehards.
Road to Respect couldn’t even clear that bar. Developer 7 Studios turned in an all-around clunker: a unwieldy beat-’em-up that struggled to do right by its very enthusiastic actors. I’ve returned to it multiple times over the past few years, and I never get very far just because brawling feels fundamentally terrible. As funny as it is to smack people around with combos with names like the Asshole Tax, you’re more likely to spend fights getting punched in all directions from multiple mobsters. A bizarrely implemented restraint system made it so that shooting a gun would get you almost instantly whacked by Tony on the back of a boat. There’s a mission where you need to help AJ run a rave. How many sins can one game commit?
Much has been said over the past 20 years about why Road to Respect was a failure. Even putting aside its awful gameplay, it had the misfortune of launching on the PS2 about a week before the PS3’s launch. It was dated before it was even out the door. As my Italian father says anytime he doesn’t know how to end a conversation: What are ya gonna do?
I’m not here to convince you that Road to Respect is actually great; not every game needs a revisionist reassessment. But what I will say is that I’ve thought about it a lot over the years, and especially now in the wake of GTA 6. In some ways, maybe Road to Respect was ahead of its time. Take its storytelling, for instance, which tried to mirror the feel of a prestige TV show long before that became fashionable in gaming.
The plot revolves around Joey LaRocca, the son of Salvatore "Big Pussy" Bonpensiero, and his attempt to become a made man in Tony Soprano’s crime family. He’s a punk kid who’s in need of direction if he’s going to step out from his father’s shadow. (Not unlike Jackie Jr. in the TV series.) It’s a story about growing up and finding discipline to earn a kind of respect that is not a given in life. It feels like a very early draft of a modern Naughty Dog game, right down to Big Pussy’s ghost dramatically appearing throughout the story to voice his son’s insecurities.
Okay, it’s also a game about beating the shit out of dudes and stuffing their heads in toilets. I’m not going to pretend like Road to Respect is some nuanced morality tale, but hey, is it any sillier than a lot of modern games that try to balance thematic depth with ultraviolent gameplay? This year’s Marvel’s Wolverine tried to paint Logan as a tortured soul who desperately wants to get his rage under control, all while having players gleefully disembowel enemies. And while GTA 6 has a lot to say about the American prison system and how it throws reformed criminals to the wolves, it’s still full of completely over-the-top shootouts (and also cryptids). Road to Respect made an earnest stab at an elevated action game, even if it missed its mark by a mile.
More than anything, though, I’ve just come to appreciate the absurdity of the entire project over the years. In 2026, Road to Respect now feels like a lost Yakuza game. The mob politics, soap opera drama, dark comedy, beat-’em-up combat, poker minigame — it really checks a lot of the same boxes, but with Paulie Walnuts in place of Goro Majima. Maybe one day it will get an eye-opening remaster that reveals a solid cinematic action game that was judged too harshly because of the expectations of the IP attached to it. Probably not, but even a flop deserves to be a context point in gaming history. GTA 6 might not exist without it.
Okay, it absolutely would, but let’s allow ourselves to be a little romantic about a bad game this Thanksgiving.
