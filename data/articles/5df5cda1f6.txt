White House pulls Heidi Overton's nomination to lead FDA
The White House has dropped its nomination of Heidi Overton to lead the Food and Drug Administration, a White House official told the Guardian.
Donald Trump nominated Overton – a physician and prominent abortion opponent – in August. But Overton faced a challenging path to Senate confirmation, particularly after Republican senator Bill Cassidy said today that he remained undecided about her nomination ahead of a Senate health, labor and pensions committee vote scheduled for tomorrow.

A federal appeals court has blocked the defense department from forcing 27 transgender troops out of the military, while the Trump administration asks the Supreme Corut to hear the case.

The White House is considering nominating senator Roger Marshall to lead the Food and Drug Administration, after it pulled its backing of Heidi Overton earlier today, Reuters reports citing two people familiar with the matter.
Marshall is finishing out his term representing Kansas in the Senate after losing his reelection bid in an upset that saw Kansas elect its first Democratic senator in 94 years. Lawmakers have agreed to a plan to require civilian aircraft carry systems that help pilots locate nearby aircraft, spurred by the deaths of 67 people in an American Airlines and Black Hawk helicopter crash in January 2025.
Senators Ted Cruz, a Republican from Texas, and Maria Cantwell, a Democrat from Washington, announced the agreement today at an event organized by a group representing the families of those killed in the 2025 crash. Although the House and Senate have passed competing aviation safety reform bills since the American Airlines crash, today’s announcement represents efforts to draft a compromise bill.
“When military aircraft fly in the same airspace, they must meet the same standards as everyone else,” said Cantwell.
Cruz added that he believes Congress can pass the bill before the end of the year. Lawmakers are still working to agree to air safety provisions for the military.

Reproductive rights group celebrates Overton's failed confirmation and issues warning to Republicans
A prominent reproductive rights group celebrated the White House’s decision to pull its nomination of Heidi Overton to lead the Food and Drug Administration.
“Heidi Overton posed a serious threat to the future of medication abortion access. Voters rejected that anti-abortion agenda nationwide, and our members kept the pressure on to tank her confirmation. If Republicans try to rush through another nominee who threatens our healthcare, we’re ready to keep fighting,” said Mini Timmaraju, the president and CEO of Reproductive Freedom for All (formerly NARAL Pro-Choice America).
As head of the FDA, Overton would have of overseen the agency that regulates medication abortions. In a 2023 policy brief for the conservative America First Policy Institute, Overton said Congress should strictly regulate the abortion pill mifeprestone “to protect women and children”, criticized federal policies that allow for the mailing of abortion pills and wrote that abortion “is corrosive to children, women, and society broadly”.

A number of lawmakers have raised concerns about a spat of outages over the past week, offered their perspective on what may be happening and called for increased regulations on artificial intelligence and tech companies.
“Over the last week, we’ve heard A LOT about ‘system outages’ from government agencies such as the FAA and NSA, as well as major companies like Microsoft and JPMorgan Chase. We don’t know the full story yet, but the chaos makes one thing obvious: Letting a handful of tech giants control this industry is just too dangerous,” said congresswoman Pramila Jayapal, a Democrat from Washington state.
“At this point, there is no indication that these incidents are the result of a cyberattack, foreign interference, or some coordinated effort. What appears to be happening instead is a consequence of how quickly artificial intelligence is changing the cybersecurity landscape,” Mark Warner, a Democratic senator from Virginia, said in a social media post. “While we still need to get to the bottom of these incidents, the evidence so far points toward legitimate efforts to secure our systems, not malicious activity.”
“The American people can see everything happening with these service outages, and they feel like they’re not getting the whole story. You can’t blame anybody for that. I’m a member of Congress, and I feel that way!” said senator Rick Scott, a Republican from Florida. “We need congressional briefings. These companies and federal agencies need to be fully transparent about what’s going on.”
“We don’t yet know what has caused recent outages at major companies and government agencies. We do know that advanced AI threatens to undermine cybersecurity for systems that make our hospitals, banks, and government run. And that could make these sort of outages much more common and damaging,” said congressman Greg Casar of Texas, who called for increased regulations on artificial intelligence.

The Board of Peace, which is overseeing the US-brokered ceasefire in Gaza, told the United Nations Security Council today that the ceasefire will collapse if steps are not taken to uphold the full peace agreement.
“The distance between what has been agreed and what is happening on the ground nonetheless remains the principal risk to the process,” says the report, a copy of which was obtained by The Associated Press.
It adds, “Restrictions imposed by Israel on the entry of supplies and equipment, compounded by funding shortfalls, continued to impede preparedness and response efforts,” calling for immediate “scaled-up entry of shelter and winterization materials.”
The Board delivered its first report to the United Nations in May, at which time it warned: “A ceasefire that delivers nothing visible cannot be sustained indefinitely.”
Defense secretary Pete Hegseth said the Pentagon “will pass a full, clean audit by 2028” in response to news that the defense department failed a financial audit for the ninth year in a row.
The Defense Department was first audited in 2018 and given 10 years to pass an annual audit. An inspector general report released yesterday showed the agency had failed audit again for fiscal year 2026. The news comes as the Trump administration is seeking a record $1.5tn for defense spending.
Despite failing the audit, Hegseth wrote in a social media post: “We are more than halfway there – huge, historic progress so far.”
As defense department CFO Jules Hurst told Reuters, a majority of the Pentagon’s units passed audit for the first time. According to Hurst, 14 of the Pentagon’s 25 stand-alone reporting entities passed audit in the 2026 fiscal year, up from 11 the prior year.
