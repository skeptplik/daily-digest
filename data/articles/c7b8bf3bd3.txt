At its heart this is a story about trust.
Trust in doctors and other health professionals at NHS Grampian to provide the highest standards of treatment and care.
Trust in their managers to effectively deal with problems when they arise.
And trust in the Scottish government to step in if that does not happen.
What we have learned about the operation of NHS Grampian in the last two weeks raises huge questions in all of these areas.
First we had a scathing report by the watchdog Healthcare Improvement Scotland, which found serious shortcomings with NHS Grampian's leadership and oversight of clinical governance.
There was particular concern about patterns of repeated and avoidable harm, and the board's ability to learn from serious adverse events.
The Scottish government has sent in a support team to help the current management at NHS Grampian make improvements.
Questions have been raised about the suitability of some of those appointed to carry out this work given problems they have faced in their own areas of responsibility.
The watchdog's report was followed by a powerful intervention from a surgeon-turned-whistleblower who works at Aberdeen Royal Infirmary.
Dr Mohammed Habib said he had been raising serious concerns about liver and pancreatic services at the hospital for 10 years.
He released a previously unpublished review from 2022 that looked at 45 patients in the care of this specialist unit over the previous seven years.
It said that 31 had died, and 19 of them were considered to have died of avoidable consequences of surgery or intervention.
This is the key detail in this story.
Nineteen people died and their deaths were potentially avoidable.
This finding was not made public by NHS Grampian or those in the Scottish government who knew about it.
This is where this story becomes political - because Dr Habib tried to get the attention of ministers.
He raised his concerns with the Scottish government including, he said, writing to the first minister on three occasions.
At Holyrood question time, John Swinney acknowledged that the issues raised were of the "gravest concern".
He said Dr Habib's messages had received "appropriate responses" from officials.
It seems they directed him to a national whistleblowing service and to the police.
This is what, in my view, makes this potentially the most dangerous political moment for John Swinney since he took over as first minister in 2024.
A surgeon writes to him because 19 people may have died unnecessarily as a result of how they were treated by the NHS and nothing seems to happen.
We have since been told that Swinney's office did not make him personally aware of this correspondence.
That in turn raises questions about the operation of the highest political office in devolved Scotland.
After two decades in power would the SNP administration not have an established system for deciding what should and should not be seen by the first minister?
And if a pattern of seemingly avoidable deaths in the NHS is not serious enough to be escalated to the top then what is?
The potential for political disaster
Swinney seemed to recognise the inadequacy of his parliamentary answers fairly quickly.
Soon after question time his team said he had asked the Scottish government's top civil servant to review how correspondence is handled.
This too was only the beginning of a realisation within government that this had the potential to develop into a political disaster.
By Saturday, Health Secretary Angela Constance had decided to announce an independent review into who knew what and when, what they did with the information - and why.
Before the weekend was out, she was on BBC Scotland's The Sunday Show promising 100% transparency and accountability.
She seemed angry to have been left carrying the can for something that should have been dealt with more robustly at a far earlier stage.
We have now learned that the review will be led by an experienced King's Counsel, and the likelihood is that they will come from outside Scotland.
Constance has said that any clinical input they receive will definitely come from outside Scotland.
The KC will be able to scrutinise the actions of current and former ministers, civil servants, and those working in the health service.
Constance said their findings would be published in full and that any urgent matters that arise during the review should be reported directly to relevant bodies.
The minister also said it was "categorically false" to say that no action was taken following the 2022 report, which was drawn up by University of Edinburgh Professor Stephen Wigmore.
Constance said liver and pancreatic surgery was suspended for a time, then resumed under the professor's supervision until he was satisfied it could continue without his oversight.
It was stopped again on at least two further occasions and patients requiring complex surgery have been transferred to NHS Lothian since early this year.
More scrutiny in parliament
It has been reported that former Health Secretary Humza Yousaf and current chief medical officer Sir Gregor Smith were aware of the 2022 review.
Yousaf has made clear he supports full scrutiny and "further dramatic action" in NHS Grampian. Sir Gregor has yet to make any public comment.
The review process will not necessarily contain this crisis of confidence.
More patients and their families may come forward to share their experiences, or perhaps others within NHS Grampian will choose to speak out.
The involvement of the campaigning lawyer Aamer Anwar, who is working with Dr Habib, increases the likelihood of this type of follow-up.
There will undoubtedly be further parliamentary scrutiny, with the possibility of key figures being called before a Holyrood committee.
It is worth remembering that the police are also taking an interest and have yet to determine whether or not a formal investigation is merited.
Some have hinted at the potential need for a judge-led public inquiry, although these tend to take a very long time and cost a huge amount of money.
There are already three health-related public inquiries into Covid handling, major hospital building projects in Glasgow and Edinburgh, and botched brain surgery in Dundee.
There is a clear determination by government and opposition at Holyrood to see a much faster remedy to the complexity of problems in NHS Grampian.
A turnaround in healthcare delivery and management in north east Scotland is being pursued alongside radical plans to overhaul how the NHS is run across the country.
The government is proposing to merge the existing network of regional health boards into just two for the whole of mainland Scotland.
There will be those who argue that this provides an opportunity to create a more robust system of supervision and a more rapid response when things go wrong.
Others fear that this kind of centralisation could result in local deficiencies being overlooked.
This could become an important feature in the debate over NHS reform.
In the meantime, the Scottish government has to convince the public and political rivals that it has got a grip of what's happening in NHS Grampian.
That seems essential if patients are to have confidence that they can place their trust in the vital services it provides.
