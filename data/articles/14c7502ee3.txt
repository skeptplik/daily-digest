They promise to find the best Black Friday deals, source Christmas gifts and find the ingredients for a romantic dinner.
This week’s UK launch of the Muse AI assistant by the owner of Facebook and Instagram promises to transform how we shop – and how retailers make money.
Meta has teamed up with high street names including Marks & Spencer, Sainsbury’s, JD Sports and Currys for what the Silicon Valley firm claims is a seamless, secure and time-saving tool.
“From doing the Christmas shopping and searching for a cheaper energy deal, to sorting the school calendar … Muse can get it done for you,” Meta said.
But for companies that have painstakingly transformed from bricks-and-mortar retailers into online emporiums, AI agents pose risks as well as opportunities. Will AI agents kill window shopping, ignore vast online ranges and send shoppers straight to the checkout? Will retailers be forced to discount heavily to stand out? How do they avoid being steamrollered by Silicon Valley tech titans in commercial negotiations?
John Roberts, the founder and chief executive of online electrical goods retailer AO.com, said the impact of AI agents – at present – was “relatively low” but their use as shopping assistants was clearly “a direction of travel”.
He said AO was positioning itself to get recommendations from AI agents in the same way it generally attracted customers, by offering decent prices and good service, which was winning good online reviews on sites such as Trustpilot.
“We need to work out how we are the best option for whichever AI agent you use,” Roberts said.
Working out where to invest is a challenge. Some smaller firms are almost ignoring the developing channel, waiting to see how and where it might go. However, holding back on involvement with the developing technology – such as online shopping in the early noughties – has killed off brands in the past.
Sharon Iles, a senior apparel analyst at the analytics and consultancy firm GlobalData, said those brands that have chosen to partner with Muse would get an early view of how people shop, what they bought through an AI agent and which products got recommended, which could help brands make their listings more visible.
But she said retailers “must continue to prioritise their own channels to protect what an AI agent cannot replace”.
If agents bypass retailers’ websites, said Iles, this could mean companies “miss out on the impulse items that shoppers often add while browsing”, while those selling more complex items such as apparel could find issues with the choice of fit and sizing, “which could lead to higher return rates”.
“Retailers’ own channels should remain the core of their business, and they must give consumers more reasons to shop directly,” she said.
Retailers including Sainsbury’s and Tesco have used their loyalty schemes and websites to build sizeable businesses that monetise customers’ data and sell advertising space to brands.
However, retailers cannot ignore the agents. Muse has proved successful in the US and is ranked first and second respectively on Google and Apple’s free app store, having been downloaded millions of times.
UK residents may be more cautious about agentic technology than those across the Atlantic, but the technology is entering the mainstream. This year, a third of people planning to shop during Black Friday sales intend to get help from AI – up from 19% last year, according to a recent YouGov poll.
To fans, the likes of Muse or rivals such as Google’s Gemini or ChatGPT’s Dots can be a personal secretary helping organise the boring details of life, but to others they are an untenable busybody, parsing their personal data and monetising it.
For Muse to work as Meta envisages, it not only needs access to your email, calendar and shopping apps but details of relationships on apps such as WhatsApp, Facebook and Instagram, your preferences and even your financial and health information.
Sainsbury’s suggests its customers can “tell Muse about their week” and the AI agent will help build a shopping list to provide for dinner parties, different dietary requirements or suggest menus and recipes.
Mark Given, the chief technology and marketing officer at the supermarket chain, said the retailer was “starting carefully, listening to customers” and using what we learn to keep making it better”.
“AI can be genuinely useful, taking on routine tasks like checking offers and building the basket, and helping customers find great value and inspiration more quickly,” he said.
In the UK, Muse must ask for your approval before making a purchase or sending an email, while a separate “security agent” checks the AI tool’s actions against the permissions you have granted.
Meta said Muse had no visibility into people’s passwords or payment methods, and it did not share users’ conversations with advertisers.
However, almost half of shoppers using AI (48%) are concerned about biased or sponsored recommendations, according to YouGov. Rival research firm Kantar found only 23% trusted a chatbot’s recommendations.
Meanwhile, some retailers are territorial about which AI agents get to trawl their data. Amazon has blocked AI agents from its store, although it is reportedly in talks with Meta about allowing access. Last year, Amazon sued the AI company Perplexity over its shopping feature that automates placing orders for users.
Research from the advisory firm KPMG highlights consumer caution. It found just 12% of those planning to shop during Black Friday – a largely online sale event in the UK – intended to use an AI tool (such as prompting ChatGPT or Gemini) specifically to find deals, slightly lower than the 15% using it last year.
Linda Ellett, the head of consumer, retail and leisure at KPMG, said trust would remain an important factor for shoppers and, while some would be happy to give access to their data to “make life easier” and enjoy the ability to “have a chat with an agent” to organise tasks, others would be much more cautious.
With about 70% of shopping still taking place in stores, and more than that in many categories, AI agents have some work to do to kill off the high street.
Brands made familiar through advertising, a store presence or social media content will continue to be important.
Most consumers use AI as only part of their search, to compare prices on kitchen equipment, home electronics or food, for example, or seek recommendations on complicated products such as electronics or supplements.
The advisory firm OC&C said: “For now, AI often helps shoppers validate a choice rather than create an entirely new preference.”
Mark Howell, the director of European retail at Rithum, an e-commerce firm that works with Asos, M&S, Aldi and Shein, said: “Retailers aren’t going to be comfortable handing checkouts entirely to bots until questions around responsibility and liability are resolved.
“Agentic commerce is both real and overhyped. The technology is clearly going to become part of retail, but the legal and commercial realities are much more complicated than people acknowledge. In Europe especially, retailers are asking a simple question: if the bot gets it wrong, who’s liable?”
Retailers and brands said that AI agents were having only a tiny influence on shopping behaviour but most larger businesses already had teams ready to support “generative engine optimisation” (GEO) tactics. These include: making sure they appear in Reddit forums, an important source for some platforms; ensuring AI models can access correct and more detailed product information in text as well as photographs; and responding to reviews on Google or Trustpilot.
While some have suggested that the use of AI agents could diminish the power of brands and loyalty schemes built up by companies with advertising and paid-for search, Roberts suggested agents might well be influenced by these factors.
The AO boss said that if an agent could see there were better deals via a loyalty scheme, then the technology could act as “an accelerant to membership not something that bypasses it”.
In future, industry insiders believe, ChatGPT, Amazon, Meta and Google are likely to try to monetise their AI platforms with some form of paid search or featured ads.
“I am pretty sure agents will start clipping the ticket from an advertising perspective,” said Roberts.
