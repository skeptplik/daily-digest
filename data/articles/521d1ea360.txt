Call of Duty publisher Activision has filed for a new patent allowing it to train NPCs on real-world player voice chat. The patent describes an AI-driven system that listens in on player voice chat following key gameplay moments and later recreates it using in-game characters.
The patent, spotted by Patentlyze, was filed in July and published in November. According to the filing and its diagrams, the system is a self-learning neural network that recognises major gameplay moments - such as kills, longshots, deaths and so on - and records the player's reaction and voice chat from the moment just before and then after they happen. These can be as simple as brief celebratory shouts, groans or yells, or as complex as trash talk.
The system compares those captured player reactions and then correlates them with coinciding gameplay events in order to understand their significance, essentially learning as it goes what triggers certain reactions. The AI then teaches itself to quickly identify those when they occur in-game and replicate them.
Although the replicated voice lines don't directly repeat players' own chat, the system does keep a profile of each player it analyses so it can deliver a response tailored to them, including sticking to the same spoken language, which it uses large language models (LLMs) to help identify. The patent describes a filter of sorts to keep NPCs from learning offensive language such as slurs.
According to the patent, the system's reach extends beyond in-game voice chat to also potentially access voice comms from major apps like Discord.
Activision's goal is to create NPCs that can speak naturally, rather than re-use the same pre-recorded lines. The application seems to be limited to multiplayer games, including those with co-op. Think of the generic voice lines spoken by AI-teammates like "cover me" and so on in a PvE game, or the many other similar ones in battle royale and PvP matches that contain dozens or hundreds of players.
Incidentally, the patent talks of replacing those generic messages with learned ones for players who join a match without a microphone, but players can also seemingly allow their in-game avatars to always use generated voices.
As the game industry continues to grapple with the use of generative-AI in game development, Activision's patent seems to be going directly to source, recreating how those LLMs and AI systems got their knowledge to begin with: from humans.
