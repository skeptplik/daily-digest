Few things make me feel like Matt Damon aging in that endlessly memed shot from Saving Private Ryan quite like seeing a formative part of my childhood turn 25. My hair is thinner. My bad vision is somehow even worse. My eyes look tired. I am old.
So old, in fact, that the upcoming 25th anniversary of the Xbox and Halo: Combat Evolved immediately made me think about the LAN parties my brother and I used to host in middle and high school. There were a dozen or so teenage boys, four TVs, and enough Ethernet cable to turn my parents’ house into a tripping hazard that could also burst into flames at any moment. I’ve played countless multiplayer games since, but none have come close to the majesty of those experiences.
Welcome to the Nexus
At some point during my obsession with Halo, I discovered that you could connect four Xbox consoles through a router and play across four TVs. You didn’t even need an internet connection. For some mysterious reason, my parents already owned several Ethernet cables that were dozens of feet long. Maybe they’d wanted a wired connection in every room. Maybe there was a sale. Whatever the explanation, we had the infrastructure.
Thus began an exciting era during which my parents kindly allowed us to host massive, very loud sleepovers dedicated exclusively to playing Halo. The living room served as the Nexus. Yes, I called it that, because I’m a huge nerd. It had the biggest screen, the best seating, and the spare router we used to connect everything.
One or two Ethernet cables snaked up the stairs to the bedroom my brother and I shared, where I had a little gaming nook with a modest TV, several consoles, and a beanbag chair. We usually set up another TV in the dining room, pushing the table into a corner. When my parents eventually finished the basement and built my brother his own room down there, we expanded to a third floor.
The first time we did this, I think we had about 10 people, most of them my older brother’s buddies. Some were quite good at Halo. Some were newbies just looking for a fun time. Most were somewhere in between. Probably my favorite thing about these parties was that, for the most part, I destroyed them all with ease.
My best friend, whom I’ll call Connor, was also in attendance. He’s a brilliant guy who’s great at games and introduced me to Magic: The Gathering. His parents were strict about sugar, though, and never let him drink soda. We thought it would be funny to let Connor solo an entire 2-liter bottle of Sprite.
It was the first time I’d ever seen a human being turn absolutely feral. As the night wore on and some players began falling asleep with controllers still in their hands, Connor was scream-cackling as he killed their defenseless Spartans. Sprite turned the nicest guy I know into the Joker.
As word spread, the parties grew in size and extravagance. Before long, four decent-sized TVs became a firm requirement, so we could approach the 16-player limit. Ethernet cables stretched out of the Nexus in every direction. We’d coordinate ahead of time who would bring extra consoles and who could supply controllers for people who didn’t have their own.
For one particularly memorable overnight, my parents bought a pizza from Ronzio’s, a local chain, that was the size of a table. There were also cookies, Doritos, candy — especially around Halloween — and, of course, soda for Connor.
We’d play everything from free-for-all Slayer to team matches and Capture the Flag. If we had an odd number of players, I always had to suffer the handicap of being on the smaller team. Sometimes we’d randomly select captains and pick teams like it was gym class.
We kept this up beyond the launch of Halo 2 in November 2004. If anything, the new mechanics and polished graphics only upped our enthusiasm. But as we reached our later years of high school and got busier, gathering everyone became harder. Meanwhile, Halo itself was helping make all that effort less necessary.
Halo 2 made getting together optional
There’s something bittersweet about the fact that the sequel we were so excited to play also helped destroy our parties. Halo 2 let us play together without leaving our houses, which made meeting in person less necessary.
Halo 2 wasn’t the first shooter to offer online multiplayer, but its approach helped establish expectations for how console multiplayer games should work. Its party system let friends assemble a group and stay together between matches, while automated playlist matchmaking handled finding opponents. Combined with Xbox Live’s friends list and voice chat, it made organizing a game remarkably easy.
You could get home, turn on your Xbox, and join your friends. Nobody needed to borrow a TV or run a cable up the stairs. For a group of teenagers whose schedules were getting more complicated, that convenience was irresistible.
I understand why we stopped. Once everyone had jobs and other commitments, finding a few hours to play online was a lot easier than organizing an overnight that also required rearranging my parents’ furniture. Nobody had to bring a TV. Nobody had to figure out whose controller was missing. We weren’t risking Connor’s health and sanity. We could just play.
But 25 years later, so much of what I remember about Halo has nothing to do with beating the campaign on Legendary, or that one time I got a Double Kill with a single sniper bullet in Halo 3 that went through two heads. It’s the Ethernet cables running up the stairs, the absurdly large pizza, and the angry accusations of “screen-looking” from people who were absolutely doing it themselves. It’s hearing someone scream from another floor when they got killed, or the playful cussing between matches during our potty breaks. And, of course, it’s Connor discovering what two liters of Sprite could do to a person.
Getting everyone into the same house was a ridiculous amount of work, and I love my parents for not just letting it happen but supporting it. I’m also incredibly grateful to have come up during an era when that kind of thing was possible.
