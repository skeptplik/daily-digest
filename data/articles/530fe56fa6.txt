Retail veteran Mindy Grossman knows a thing or two about transformations in business, which is part of the reason she has formed a bond with Daniel Heaf, CEO of Bath & Body Works, who is in the middle of transforming the iconic retailer.
“We first met when Daniel invited me to Columbus for his first global leadership meeting at Bath & Body Works, and it was a conversation about transformation, brand, strategic purpose, culture and consumer obsession,” said Grossman, who today is vice chair and partner of Consello Group. Grossman was in conversation with Heaf at the Beauty Inc Catalysts conference in New York City last Tuesday. “What started as a fireside chat became a friendship because we’re both so aligned on the power of transformation.”
Transformation, Grossman believes, is probably one of the most overused words in business and often misunderstood. “People think they put a sign on the wall and they’re transformed. But it’s large, wholesale, radical change, especially in today’s environment,” she said.
Indeed, when Heaf was appointed CEO in May of 2025, he was brought in as a change agent. While the retailer has many positive attributes including a loyal customer base, 1,900 stores in North America and 600 stores internationally, Heaf saw it as a business that was a bit frozen in time.
“Even though we have this loyal customer base that has been with us for a very long time, they, too, are excited about change,” he said. “We have just started firing up the product innovation engine of Bath & Body Works — the creators inside our company — creating new, amazing fragrances and products, and it really started in the last quarter. We launched four new [product] franchises….and we have been blown away by the results of those.”
Still, Heaf, a veteran of Burberry and Nike, was wary about implementing change. “We’re bringing new product franchises to a business, and I was nervous about how well received they would be,” he said, “but they have delivered beyond our expectations, particularly with consumers who have shopped with us for years.”
In addition to innovation, Heaf’s strategy includes improving Bath & Body Works’ digital capabilities and expanding distribution with the likes of Amazon and Ulta Beauty. As for learnings during Year One, a key one is that changing daily habits is hard, both internally and externally, said Heaf.
Drawing on her experience at Ralph Lauren and Nike, Grossman said that when it comes to change at legacy brands, you can be provocative but have to be careful not to be polarizing.
“It was much harder than I thought it would be,” said Heaf of strategizing the transformation of the retailer. “When I worked in the past at Nike and Burberry, [they] had these unbelievable archives, and you could go back in history and study the origins of the brand. That archive doesn’t exist at Bath & Body Works. It sort of exists in modernity, being only 35 years old. So I had to go back through the internet and the communities and fan pages and Reddit threads of consumers who have documented the journey of this brand.”
From his own research, he found that the brand’s DNA was threefold: great fragrance, great quality and great value. “So the whole genesis of the transformation is about delivering those three things in new ways. It’s about how we deliver fragrance in a modern way, how we signify and update our formulas to bring quality, and how we deliver that to the consumers in new ways,” said Heaf.
Grossman stressed that during a transformation, it’s hard to change the business until you change the culture.
“Our job is to enable other people to change,” said Heaf. “As I think about Bath & Body Works and how strong its heritage is, but also how rooted in time it was, we had to give our teams not just the strategy — which they have — but the confidence, belief and freedom to deliver that change every day.
“We have to be so comfortable knowing that transformations, particularly in the world we live in right now, aren’t going to be perfect, but staying still is not the right answer,” he continued. “We have to be a catalyst in the way that we back people. We have to be able to take risks and fail and learn and move on. That is the true nature of being a catalyst.”
Moving onto the subject of technology and innovation, Grossman said that AI is the fourth industrial revolution, but it also has the ability to create the next human renaissance. Heaf described himself as an AI optimist but cited the need to be realistic about the fact that nobody knows the answers to these questions yet.
“People like me can come on stages and quantify what technology will deliver, and yet I had dinner with someone who works at a frontier model last week, and a phrase stuck with me: ‘We have no idea about the outcomes of our technology,’” he said. “So, if someone who is working in that industry — who is a creator in that industry — is still unable to predict the impact of the technology that they are building, we are somewhat downstream of that, and so we have to prepare for all kinds of different outcomes.”
Nevertheless, Heaf is optimistic in both a professional sense and a consumer sense.
“I hope that the technology is not going to lead to mass unemployment, but quite the opposite: that jobs get better,” he said. “I think about our creative teams at Bath & Body Works and about all the jobs they love doing, and all the jobs they hate doing. If you’re a creative director or a photographer, I don’t think you come to work thinking, ‘I’m so excited to take this image and cut it into 300 different sizes, and then to upload it into a set of websites in order to make it work across all the paid media channels.’ AI can take that job away, so that they can spend more time doing what they’re passionate about, which is being creative.”
