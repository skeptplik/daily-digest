Back in my malleable twenties, I had a big phase with John Coltrane's version of “My Favorite Things.” It was a deep enough crush that it got me thinking about what my own favorite things were, and what made them favorites.
It was a nice thing to talk about with people, and of course, my list always had a bunch of kitchen gear and cookbooks on it. It's not too hard to draw a line from that time to becoming a food writer and kitchen gear reviewer. Finding the best and figuring out what makes it great became a career-defining quest.
Incredibly important on this path was finding (or maybe stealing?) my mom's copy of The Best Recipe cookbook from Cook's Illustrated, one of the charmingly nerdier wings of America's Test Kitchen. That book applied the scientific method to recipes to create "best" versions, documenting their dead ends and successes en route to the perfect flan, mashed potatoes, or boiled green beans. As they put it right on the cover, "Would you make 38 versions of crème caramel to find the absolute best version? We did."
When I worked as a line cook, I always wondered about the food I was making: Why this combination of ingredients? Why that technique? Here was a book that answered those questions while bolstering my kitchen foundations.
When it came out in 1999, the internet wasn't yet where you went for dinnertime inspiration, meaning you'd find a recipe in a cookbook or magazine or newspaper, or dig around in the recipe box. Now, you can spend hours researching different “best” methods to make a recipe, wondering if you'll know perfect when you see it.
“It's a trap to think like that,” says Ella Quittner, a culture and food writer, and author of the new cookbook Obsessed With the Best, a spiritual descendant of Best Recipe. “Perfect food doesn't exist. For an individual, it exists for the moment and a place in time.”
Perfect on a leisurely Saturday afternoon is very different from perfect on a busy Tuesday evening at 6 pm.
But it's different when Quittner is coming up with a recipe.
“I do a huge amount of research through cookbooks, magazines, websites, cookbooks from the 18th century, or something from TikTok last week,” she says. “I'll take my findings and try to braid them together. I don't want a Frankenstein dish. I want deep flavor, not too fussy. It should seem like the right amount of labor for the result you're seeking.”
On the simpler side, she boldly updates Marcella Hazan's famous tomato sauce. Quittner admiringly explains how in Hazan's seemingly simple tomato-butter-onion-salt version, it uses an onion “to flavor the sauce like it would in a stock.” It also teaches “the power of simmering an acidic ingredient with a fat ingredient over low heat over time.” She calls it a fundamental flavor-building technique where “the fat rounds out the acid and the acid helps taste the fat.” Or, as she says, “acid lifts, fat deepens.” For her update, she quarters the onions and caramelizes their flat surfaces in butter, adding depth and sweetness. Building on something her parents did when she was a kid, she lays down a dab of ricotta before the sauce-coated noodles go in the bowl, a luxurious pasta trick I am adding to my forever repertoire.
For her cookbook's “overachiever” chocolate chip cookies, she pulled out all the stops by tweaking the quantities of flour, sugar, and chocolate, working through cause and effect for each change. She made the cookie dough “dozens and dozens and dozens” of times to reach her ideal, weaving in ideas from pastry chef and cookbook author Natasha Pickowicz and pastry chef and food writer Stella Parks. She uses a little buckwheat flour “for grit and warmth and nuttiness,” and adds milk powder to the brown-butter-making process to “jack up the caramel-toffee notes to a five-alarm brown butter disco rave.” She also has you cream the butter and sugar for a long time, which lends chewiness. All that weaving and attention to confection is a high-effort, high-reward cookie akin to what you could find in a high-end bakery.
At America's Test Kitchen, they go to similar lengths, but on a comparatively industrial scale, using what chief content officer Dan Souza refers to as a “crystal-clear process with specific goals.”
They dive into their “massive” cookbook library, look at what's online, then pull five representative recipes from that research, weaving them together with their own expertise to create a working recipe. “Typically, that's fine, and then we iterate,” Souza says. From there, up to 25 staff members might contribute to the recipe, a process that can take six weeks before it is sent to a subset of their army of—get this—75,000 volunteer recipe testers.
“They can change what we do,” Souza says. “It helps make the recipe bulletproof.”
Even ATK’s version of “best” changes as its subscribers become more sophisticated. Souza talks about how his team’s banana bread has changed over time, and now, there is twice as much banana and more brown sugar. Also: “We sent our bananas off to a lab to learn about ripeness.”
Even Dan's mom has leveled up.
“When I was a kid, my mom used to make pizza on a sheet pan, and it had lots of cheese on it. It was a pizza, and I loved it,” he says. “Now she's got a baking steel, and her pizza is at a completely different level.”
Sometimes, to get to that level, or simply get the hang of something new, the language in the recipe or headnote needs to get you there.
“Sometimes people write casual, chatty, recipes. Sometimes it's like a list,” says Sarah Billingsley, food and lifestyle publishing director for Chronicle Books.
She loves it when she finds an author who has cooked something so many times that they have “a casual and unique way to describe a step.” Instead of “cook until browned,” it might be “cook until the edges of the leeks start to frazzle.”
As we talk, we get into a mutual appreciation of Ukrainian author Anna Voloshyna and her cookbooks. Billingsley draws my attention to the descriptions in the baked milk recipe in Voloshyna's new book, Ukraïne, where you learn that young chefs in Ukraine are popularizing a classic recipe, baking milk and nothing more in a Dutch oven for 8 to 10 hours and using it in desserts, coffee, and to make fresh cheeses.
Since few stateside readers will have made it or encountered the dense brown skin that forms on its surface, Voloshyna offers near-poetic counsel: “Don't break the skin until after the milk is out of the oven and has cooled. You will know the milk is ready when it smells nutty and the skin on top is a deep golden brown.”
Regardless of how you write a recipe, you need to know what a perfect version of a dish tastes like in the first place. That's on full display in chef Pim Techamuanvivit and coauthor Andrea Nguyen's new book, Cooking Thai.
Techamuanvivit has earned Michelin stars for her restaurants in Bangkok and San Francisco, and Nguyen has written several award-winning Vietnamese cookbooks. Together, Nguyen says, they strove to “get the reader to Pim's palate” even if that reader doesn't have a perfect understanding of the cuisine.
To do this, sometimes you need a power combo like these two to act as what Nguyen calls a chief arbiter of taste.
“You need someone to tell you this is what's good,” and as she said this, I imagined her handing me a small bowl of the pork and sour leaf curry on page 176. “Pim was raised in that culture, and she'll tell you she's a home cook, but she knows the technique. She is the perfect person to create a cookbook that will be around for years.”
To help get their readers there, Nguyen points out how the book has a front section with photos of the exact ingredients called for, like an image of earthy, medicinal galangal with the gentle plea: “Please do not substitute ginger,” and a photo of Golden Mountain Seasoning Sauce that a shopper could look for online or at the market. You can play around once you get the hang of things, but until then, listen to the experts and spring for the Golden Mountain. Do not substitute ginger for galangal.
“Pim and I, we're in our mid-life. We've been around. Good food doesn't need to be elevated. It's already perfect,” she says. “A recipe is like driving instructions. Sometimes you take a detour. A perfect recipe has guardrails to help you complete and succeed and reach your destination.”
