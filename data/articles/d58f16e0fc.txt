Sophie Okonedo is one of those performers whose very presence can make a scene feel more alive. The Tony Award-winning British actress projects both warmth and watchfulness, moving swiftly from humor to hurt, or authority to vulnerability, without announcing the shift. Even in a supporting role, she has a way of making you lean in.
This winter, she leads two independent features opening theatrically after an international festival run: Clarissa, by brothers Chuko and Arie Esiri, and Mouse, by the husband-and-wife team of Kelly O’Sullivan and Alex Thompson. Though shot nearly a year apart, the two films are arriving within weeks of each other—a quirk of the release calendar that makes for an unusually timely double feature.
Mouse, out now, is a coming-of-age story set in 2002 Arkansas, in which the teenage Minnie (Katherine Mallen Kupferer) develops a complicated relationship with her best friend’s mother (Okonedo) after an unexpected tragedy. Meanwhile, Clarissa—in limited theaters December 18—transposes Virginia Woolf’s novel Mrs. Dalloway to contemporary Nigeria, a project that brought Okonedo to a country she hadn’t visited in two decades. (Her late father, whom she was mostly estranged from, moved back to his home country when she was young.)
Filming in Lagos proved consequential in ways that extended well beyond the work itself. “The journey I had in Lagos was so immense, personally and professionally, that I remember thinking on the wrap day that even if nothing else happens with this film, I’ve already gotten everything I wanted,” she tells me.
Both films were made on tiny budgets, and, as Okonedo points out, a conversation about one inevitably opens up a conversation about the other. So, we talked about both.
Vogue: Let’s start with Mouse. How did it come to you? What was it about your character Helen, or about directors Kelly and Alex, that made you say yes?
Sophie Okonedo: I didn’t read anything about them. I read the script completely cold. By the time I got to the end, I rang my agent and said, “I’m absolutely doing this. I don’t know who’s directing, I don’t know what’s happening with the film, but I’m doing it.” So he sent me their last film, Ghostlight. It’s such a brilliant film and it was made for pennies. These are two really interesting filmmakers who are talking about America in a way I haven’t seen—very quietly. There are no explosions, no big movie stars, and it’s deeply significant. Kelly’s writing is subtle and real. But films like this can slip by without anyone noticing. I have to galvanize everyone and get them behind it, because I think it’s so good.
Helen ends up mothering her daughter’s friend, Minnie, in a way that’s unnerving at times, as if she’s grieving and replacing her at the same time.
It’s totally inappropriate at times. She even says it at one point: “Oh my God, I’m the adult here.” She uses Minnie to ease her own grief, then backtracks, then moves forward again. It’s a messy situation, but there’s hope in it. The film is funny, too. Kelly has that gift for finding levity in dark situations. But they never get you to play the comedy. You play the seriousness of it all and it’s funny as it goes along.
I realized after watching the film that the woman playing Katherine’s mother is her real-life mother. What was that dynamic like on set?
She wasn’t even meant to play the part at first. Tara [Kupferer], her mother, is a theater actress. She runs a theater company in Chicago and was there as Katherine’s guardian. They’d cast a great local actor, but on the first day we realized it wasn’t right. By the second day, Tara stepped in. Everything was so homegrown. My baby in the film is actually Kelly and Alex’s baby. They removed all the blockers you normally have as an actor on a film, where you spend three hours in hair and makeup. Sometimes you need those things, but this film didn’t. You just rock up and start filming. It was very seamless.
You shot on location in North Little Rock. Had you been to Arkansas before?
Never. I got there a little early to check it out because I was playing an outsider, but an outsider who’d lived there for 15 years, so I had to know the place and feel familiar with it. All the locations came from [O’Sullivan’s] childhood; you could ask her, “What happened here?” and she’d tell you. A lot of people with small parts in the film knew Kelly from high school and were now playing mothers. The dance we see at the memorial was one Kelly actually performed herself. We even brought back the dance teacher who taught her so she could teach it to the two girls in the film.
Does playing an outsider give you something to work with? You didn’t do the method thing, keeping yourself apart from everyone, right?
No. I totally get that some people do, but I’m too shy to stay in character! I feel too silly. What I do is think very deeply about every single word I say in the script before I arrive. Because I’m theater-trained, the script is my bible. All answers will be there. I spend a long time thinking about everything I say and everything everyone else says, and I go through it many, many times. An internal world builds up inside me. I can’t really explain how it happens. When I get to set, I forget any notes or journals I’ve made. I drop it all, empty myself, and see what happens. I feel buoyant and flexible, not stuck in any particular way.
Mouse is so specific about 2002, down to the Michelle Branch music cues. I was a bit younger than the characters then, but it all felt right. What did setting it in that moment bring to the story?
I’m older, but Kelly was that age in 2002, so she had all the references. She knew exactly what she was listening to, what she was thinking and feeling. The main difference is there were no cell phones back then. If you wanted information, it had to come to you through someone taking action. The jogger has to tell Minnie something, then she has to run to the house to see if it’s really happened, then she has to look for a phone book. Now it would just be someone posting “RIP Callie” on Instagram. It’s so much more interesting to film people taking an action than all these constant shots of phones and texts, which I see in movies all the time now. It’s so boring.
In Mouse, Helen and Minnie come from very different backgrounds. Class is also woven into Clarissa, where you play the titular role. Was that a connection you were conscious of while working on the two films?
Class is massive in Britain. We live and breathe it. It runs through every vein of everyone’s body here, probably more than anything else, so I’m very aware of it. But there’s another weird parallel between the films: Mouse is directed by a couple and one of them is the writer. Clarissa is directed by two brothers and one of them is the writer. With both, I had an instant, visceral response. I didn’t need to think about the whys and wherefores. It was just yes, yes, yes. With a lot of other work, I have to get myself into the right position or work it out. What’s also amazing is that the scripts for Clarissa and Mouse barely changed between that first draft and the start of filming—the same was true of Janet Planet. These are filmmakers who have thought deeply about what they’re writing. There weren’t a million drafts or endless revisions; all three films were already in a remarkably evolved state from the start. Even though Mouse looks improvised in places, I’m sticking to the script.
But parts of Mouse were improvised, right? I’m thinking of the scene where Helen attends a grief group.
It was a real grief group. I knew the man handing me coffee at the beginning was an actor, though by coincidence he was also in a grief group. I was just outside the room; they put two cameras in there and someone called action. I walked in, and during the scene it slowly dawned on me—someone had forgotten to tell me these weren’t actors!
You were essentially caught on camera.
The scene is short, but that take lasted an hour and 10 minutes—the exact length of the group. If it were scripted, I probably would have talked and broken down. But I couldn’t speak; their stories were so extraordinary. I was still talking to them afterward, thinking the cameras had stopped, when I realized they were still filming. Alex will do that. You don’t even know. There’s no “cut.”
Both films are concerned with motherhood, though in very different ways. In Mouse, there’s an absent daughter; in Clarissa, she keeps her living daughter at arm’s length. Did these characters speak to each other in any way?
It’s a good question, because I find that when I do a part, it usually feeds into the next one. The next role I played after Clarissa was Natalya in Turgenev’s A Month in the Country, in London. She’s a woman of a certain age looking back, wondering whether she made the right choices, and she has a very separate relationship with her son. I was thinking that a lot of Clarissa was in her.
I read that Arie and Chuko drew on their own mother in shaping the film, and that you got to spend time with her. How did that influence your performance?
When they cast me, I wondered if I could do this. I told them I didn’t recognize the Lagos in the script and that I had such a fractured relationship with the country. They said, “If nothing else, we’ll bridge that for you.” That meant a lot. Before filming, Chuko invited me to stay with him and his mother. I ended up staying the whole shoot and got very close to her. I used a lot of her, more than she realizes perhaps. Like Clarissa, she employs staff [in her home] and I observed how she interacted with them. I didn’t want to turn her into a caricature out of Downton Abbey. You can just be real with them, the way she was.
You also just don’t see stories like this coming out of Africa, and I say that broadly. The subject matter is usually very narrow: Africa is depicted as war-torn, impoverished. Here was a film about people. There’s a backdrop of war and social and economic circumstances, but that isn’t the main thrust. I long to see films where we as Africans, as Nigerians, get every part of ourselves represented, just as our white Western counterparts get films about everything. It’s the same with queer films and Black films: they shouldn’t only be about being queer or Black. That shouldn’t have to be the whole story.
And on top of that, Clarissa is character-driven and not plot-driven.
The thought of going to Lagos and making an arthouse film on 35mm was incredible. It’s an impossible book to film. People have tried many times, and mostly it doesn’t work. I was taking a punt. I just wanted to be part of it. I hardly say anything, but say a million words with my face.
What is it like to project that kind of interiority? It’s sort of a paradox: Clarissa is deep in thought but she also doesn't let you in much.
She’s hard to read, but you have to make her really easy to read for the camera. She’s protective, but you have to let the camera in. I didn’t think about it that deeply. I just have that sort of face. I’m really sensitive, hypersensitive, which is so difficult in my normal life, but it’s great for acting. My grandmother used to say, “Your face changes like the wind.” Everything shows. Chuko or Arie would only have to whisper the slightest thing in my ear, and it would show.
People always say to watch an actor’s eyes, but I noticed so much happens in the lower half of your face. It holds everything.
That’s why I could never do any of that stuff to my face. I’d be too frightened! I know I only have to think the glimmer of a thought and it’ll show. I don’t know what happens to my face, because I don’t really watch myself, but doing something that would stop that would be the end of my career.
The story famously centers on your character throwing a party. Are you more of a host or a guest?
I’m quite a good host, actually. I don’t do it often because I go overboard and it’s exhausting. I love cooking and making lovely things. I used to spend the whole time panicking in the kitchen, but I’ve learned it doesn’t really matter what you serve. It’s about how you engage with guests. They just want you to be part of the party.
Did you watch any of the other adaptations of Mrs. Dalloway to prepare for Clarissa?
I watched about three minutes of one with Vanessa Redgrave and realized we’re not doing this type of film, so I didn’t carry on. Chuko and Arie would have watched everything, though. They're complete cinephiles.
Did they give you suggestions on films to watch to prepare then?
They probably did, but I forgot because my brain is a sieve. They gave up on me. They’d mention really famous films and I’d say I hadn’t seen them. I don’t know what I do with my time. I don’t fill it wisely. I just seem to mess around most of the day, and then it’s gone!
It sounds like you’re living your own stories instead of watching other people’s on screen.
You’re putting it nicely. As my husband says, I spend a lot of time daydreaming. I’ve got a really nice view. [She turns her laptop to the window to show me the view.] I spend a lot of time just looking out at the trees with my dog.
After this double-header year, what are you looking for next?
Because I’m getting older, I don’t see an eternity stretching out in front of me anymore, and I find myself asking how I want to spend my time. I’ve decided that my heart is really in independent films and theater, so I’m going to wait until the right thing comes along. You might find me still unemployed a year from now. Nothing has come up yet that’s been worth getting on a plane for.
