In October 2025, four Black high school students were sharing Cool Ranch Doritos after football practice, winding down and waiting for their rides home. It was then, in the lull of a moment any teen with after-school activities knows well, that the school’s AI gun detection system suddenly flagged one teen holding a bag of chips as a potential gunman.
Within two minutes, Baltimore County Public Schools officials who reviewed security camera footage linked to the alert determined there was no actual threat. They recognized the Doritos bag and canceled the alert, which notified not only other school officials but also the Baltimore County Police Department that no response was needed.
The false alert could have ended there—before a traumatizing campus clash with police—but it didn’t.
According to a lawsuit filed by the current and former students, Taki Allen, Yui-Osa Ogbeide, Devon Thompson Jr., and Keith Winfield III, due to “both technological and human errors,” school officials called police to the scene anyway, and the cops responded with force.
At least six patrol cars arrived with lights flashing, and some cops emerged with guns drawn. The teens froze and cooperated, confused and scared for their lives, their lawsuit said.
“No one told the students what they were suspected of,” the lawsuit said. “Officers separated the students, ordered them to their knees on the concrete, handcuffed them, and searched their persons and property.”
Only after the cops failed to find a gun did students learn that it was the school’s AI gun detection system, Omnilert, that triggered the police response. Allegedly, one of the responding officers muttered, “stupid AI cameras” and “fucking Omnilerts,” while another told the students that “AI is not the best.” Finally releasing the students, one officer seemed to acknowledge that they had put the teens in danger.
