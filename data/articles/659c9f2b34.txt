As It Happens
Pig kidney transplant patient marks 1 year without dialysis in medical breakthrough
Doctors say Molly Falco's one-year marker is a major milestone for the growing field of xenotransplantation
It's been more than a year since Molly Falco underwent experimental surgery to receive a kidney harvested from a genetically modified pig, and she says she has no regrets.
Before the surgery, she says she could barely move the three feet between her at-home dialysis machine to her bed without becoming exhausted. But now?
"I feel good. I feel like I have a lot of energy," the 58-year-old New Hampshire woman told As It Happens host Nil Köksal. "I still go kind of slow, but I'm not nauseous. I'm not dizzy. I'm not feeling like throwing up all the time."
Falco is one of only a handful of patients in the world who have received a pig kidney transplant, and on Sunday, she became the first to go a full year without rejecting the organ.
Doctors say this is major milestone in the growing field of animal-to-human organ transplantation, known as xenotransplantation. But they caution it could be years before this kind of procedure is available to patients outside of clinical trials.
"It's not the finish line," Dr. Leonardo Riella, who oversaw Falco's treatment at Mass General Brigham Hospital in Boston, said. "It's sort of a stepping stone."
'I'll do anything to get off dialysis'
Falco, who suffers from kidney failure, says she signed up to be considered for clinical trial when she put her name on the waitlist for a human kidney transplant.
"I thought it was really cool and, you know, I'd be thrilled to be a part of something that's helping the advancement of medicine and science," she said. "I never expected to get called."
When Riella, Mass General Brigham's director of kidney transplantation, reached out to her, she'd already made up her mind.
"I just said yes before he finished asking," she said. "I said, 'You know what? I'll do anything to get off dialysis.'"
It was an odd experience, she says, being on the frontier of something cutting-edge.
"Because it was new, the first thing they said was: We have no idea what will happen. And every day you have it, we learn something," she said.
Six pig kidney transplants have been performed at Mass General Brigham Hospital since 2024. One patient died shortly after surgery from unrelated heart disease. Two of the transplanted kidneys lasted between eight and nine months before the patients' bodies rejected them, forcing them to return to dialysis. The two most recent patients received their transplants this year and still have them.
The total number of pig kidney transplants performed worldwide is unclear, but Riella says a couple have been performed at other U.S. hospitals and that China is also experimenting with xenotransplantation.
He says Falco and the others taking part in these trials are "true heroes."
Too many patients, not enough organs
Riella says the gold standard of treatment for late-stage kidney disease is a human kidney transplant.
The problem, he says, is there just aren't enough of them.
While patients wait for a kidney to become available, they undergo dialysis, a way of artificially simulating the function of the kidneys by removing extra fluid and waste products from the blood.
The side effects of dialysis can be debilitating, he says, and the wait for a transplant can last years.
"Every week we are removing patients from our waiting list because they either get too sick, and we cannot move through transplant anymore, or they die while they're waiting for the promised kidney," he said.
Dr. Leonardo Rielli is the director of kidney transplantation at Mass General Brigham Hospital in Boston, and the head of a new clinical trial into xenotransplantation. (Mass General Brigham Hospital)
Dr. Deepali Kumar, program medical director of the Ajmera Transplant Centre at Toronto General Hospital, says Falco's story provides hope for the roughly 30,000 Canadians currently on dialysis for end-stage kidney disease.
"It's actually a very exciting milestone in the transplant field," Kumar said. "The fact that it's lasted for a year is incredible."
According to the Canadian Institute of Health Information (CIHI), 3,174 Canadians received life-saving organ transplants in 2025, while another 212 people died on the waiting list. Of those, 77 were waiting for kidneys.
Kumar cautions that xenotransplantation is not currently being performed anywhere in Canada, as there are a lot of unanswered questions about the long-term impact, eligibility criteria and post-surgery care.
"We have taken a more cautious approach and we'd like to see the longer-term outcomes from the clinical trial that's going on in the United States," she said.
Lilly the pig kidney
Early next year, Riella is expected to lead a clinical trial involving 33 patients who will receive pig kidneys from eGenesis at 10 U.S. medical centres. The biotechnology company also provided Falco's kidney.
He says there's a long road ahead, but he's hopeful that, one day, pig transplants will keep thousands of patients off dialysis as they wait for human kidneys — or, better yet, become the go-to long-term treatment.
"What's remarkable about the technology of how these pig kidneys are generated is that there are endless numbers of things that we could potentially modify in their genes … to make it even more compatible to humans," he said.
Falco, left, pictured with her family. She says her life has improved dramatically since she's been off dialysis. (Submitted by Paul Falco)
Falco, meanwhile, has named her pig kidney Lily, after the flower her daughter crocheted for her while she was in the hospital. If her body eventually rejects it, she'll go back on dialysis and await a human kidney.
But she hopes she won't have to part with Lily any time soon.
"I've committed to keep the kidney as long as I can," she said.
