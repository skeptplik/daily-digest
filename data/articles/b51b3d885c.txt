Bereaved families are borrowing money or forgoing a service with mourners because financial help available from the state no longer covers even half a typical funeral director’s fee.
“The funeral expenses payment (FSP) was originally meant to cover the cost of a simple funeral. Today, that is a pipe dream,” said Lindesay Mace, a co-manager of Down to Earth, a funeral costs support service run by the charity Quaker Social Action.
For eligible households in England, Wales and Northern Ireland (Scotland has its own version), the benefit covers “reasonable” cemetery or crematorium fees plus up to £1,000 for “other funeral expenses”.
While the first element of the payment has had to keep pace with inflation, the £1,000 cap has risen once in 23 years and has been frozen since 2020, despite rising fees for the elements of a funeral that it covers.
Mace said: “Successive governments have caused the payment to devalue so much that it is failing bereaved people. We speak to people who are unable to focus on their grief due to the financial stress of having to top up the payment.”
The helpline supports about 1,000 people a year and recently assisted Carol, who was grieving the unexpected death of her daughter. She received the benefit but, despite having a “simple” funeral, says it did not cover the cost, leaving her £1,000 short. “I’m still trying to scrape together money to put a headstone on the grave,” she said.
While many of the main social security benefits are protected from losing value over time, others are not.
As well as the capped part of the FSP, the bereavement support payment (BSP) has also been frozen since its introduction in 2017. It provides short-term assistance after the death of a partner.
The freezes have “led to substantial real-terms falls in their value”, according to a recent report by the Social Security Advisory Committee.
Currently, the higher rate of bereavement support, which is available to claimants with children, comprises a £3,500 lump sum and up to 18 monthly payments of £350, totalling £9,800. Had it increased in line with the consumer prices index (CPI) it would be worth £13,448, the report says.
Meanwhile, had the £1,000 cap on funeral expenses increased in line with CPI, it would now be worth about £1,300.
Funeral director charges have risen substantially in recent years and the cost of essentials such as mortuary care, the coffin and hearse now averages £2,605, meaning the state help meets less than 40% of this bill, the report notes.
As a result, it says, individuals “often face large shortfalls, which they must make up themselves in order for funerals to go ahead … many are forced to make difficult decisions, including selling the valuables of their deceased loved ones or turning to illegal money lenders.”
Terry Tennens, the chief executive of the National Society of Allied and Independent Funeral Directors, said: “The concern is it pushes people down the unattended cremation route because it is cheaper. There’s no memorial, and that has its consequences … when you are grieving and there’s no place to remember and gather.”
MPs will debate the BSP on Monday after a petition calling for it to be uprated annually and extended beyond 18 months received more than 108,000 signatures.
A Department for Work and Pensions spokesperson said the BSP was intended to provide short-term financial support. “Further help with everyday living costs may be available through benefits such as universal credit, which provide targeted, means-tested support to those in greatest need.”
