After it was leaked earlier this week, the European Commission has formally presented its draft for the Digital Fairness Act today. If adopted, the Act will have a massive impact on the games sector, particularly where virtual currencies are involved.
Crucially, the Digital Fairness Act will take the form of a European Union Regulation, meaning it will apply directly across all EU member states, without the need for national transposition. It will also partly override existing national laws on unfair commercial practices, and it is designed to complement the EU KIDS Act – which is still working its way through the legislative process, and which will also have a big impact on the games industry.
Dark patterns
The first area of relevance for game developers concerns dark patterns in online interfaces (Article 10). These will be prohibited outright. What exactly qualifies as a dark pattern will be defined by a new blacklist, the precise contents of which are not yet known, but which the Commission will be empowered to expand over time.
Article 12 prohibits the targeting of minors based on profiling, which is not a new restriction: equivalent prohibitions already exist under other laws. In a similar vein, Article 13 prohibits personalised pricing based on profiling where it is reasonably likely to cause harm to minors or vulnerable consumers. But the most immediately consequential provisions for games companies relate to paid-for virtual currencies (Article 14).
Virtual currency and loot boxes
Under the Act, companies will be required to display the corresponding real-money value of virtual currencies. That's a demand consumer protection organisations have made in the past, such as in a report from the BEUC (Bureau Européen des Unions de Consommateurs) in 2024, and it is at the core of the recent cases opened by the CPC Network against various games firms.
For products directed at minors, paid-for virtual currencies must be disabled by default, and can only be activated upon parental or guardian approval. Whether the obligation to disable extends to the purchase mechanism alone or also to the in-game functionality itself is a question that merits closer analysis.

The treatment of paid-for variable reward systems – commonly known as loot boxes – follows a similar logic (Article 15). Such systems must not be offered to minors at all. Where they remain available to adult consumers, companies must ensure transparency, specifically by disclosing the types of items available and the probability of obtaining them, and must provide effective tools enabling consumers to disable these systems entirely.
Pay-to-win
The prohibition on pay-to-progress and pay-to-win mechanisms for minors (Article 16) may prove to be one of the more complex provisions to implement in practice. The regulation covers in-game or in-app purchases that are necessary, or are presented as necessary, for in-game or in-app progress or success.
The interpretation of these concepts is far from settled – and so is the precise legal consequence. For instance, the question of whether adult consumers may still purchase items conferring competitive advantages, and what that means in a mixed-age game environment where minors are also present, remains open. Will it mean that adults can buy strong items and thus gain an advantage over kids?
Addictive features
Under Article 17, companies will be required to allow consumers to disable features that encourage compulsive or excessive use.
The list of covered features includes automatic content play and uninterrupted consumption (think doomscrolling mechanics), push notifications not directly triggered by user activity, mechanics that incentivise engagement at regular intervals – such as login rewards or engagement streaks – and features that simulate interpersonal relationships to encourage continued use. As with the blacklist for dark patterns, the Commission will be able to extend this list by delegated act, which means the scope of the obligation is not fixed at adoption.
Subscriptions
The regulation also introduces a package of contract fairness measures (Articles 20–22) with direct relevance for subscription-based games and services. Free trials may only convert into paid contracts with the consumer's express agreement, and new information obligations will apply regarding the end of the trial period, termination conditions, and renewal terms.
Separately, companies offering online contracting will be required to implement a mandatory online contract termination function and a withdrawal function (Articles 23 and 24), making cancellation as easy as sign-up.
Enforcement is backed by fines of up to 6% of worldwide annual turnover
On customer service (Article 25), companies providing post-contractual online support must, upon request, ensure that consumers can reach a human being. Finally, enforcement is backed by fines of up to 6% of worldwide annual turnover, with the regulation applying from 12 months after entry into force.
Impacts on the industry
The definition of minors for the purposes of the Digital Fairness Act is any person under 18. Whether a product is "directed at minors" or "used by minors" is to be assessed on the basis of objective indicators – with age ratings explicitly listed as one such indicator.
This is a significant choice. Under this approach, a game with an 18+ rating will face fewer restrictions than a game rated for all ages. For developers, this might create an incentive to pursue higher age ratings.
On the positive side, the Digital Fairness Act aims to harmonise rules on the European level, which will lessen the struggle for companies to understand and meet the various requirements of differing national laws across Europe. For example, it introduces unified transparency requirements for influencers (Article 11), which, unsurprisingly, are of a rather strict nature.
The European Commission obviously feels that the industry has not done enough 
But overall, the Digital Fairness Act, if adopted, will force a dramatic change in the way large parts of the games industry conducts business. The European Commission obviously feels that the industry has not done enough to calm the concerns of consumer groups, despite continued warnings. On the other hand, the industry feels that the Commission does not sufficiently understand how games work, what PEGI and USK have already done, and that at least some of the demands cannot be reasonably implemented – and that others might not help consumers at all. We are now left with a draft which is not easy to digest – neither for the industry, nor for regulators, and whether it actually helps consumers remains to be seen.
