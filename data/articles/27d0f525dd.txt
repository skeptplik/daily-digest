Beijing has said it reserves the right to take “necessary measures” after Donald Trump’s administration proposed new tariffs on goods from China and other major trading partners, triggering a global backlash.
Among the suggested levies on 14 major trading partners announced on Wednesday, China is potentially facing one of the biggest hits. It would face the highest capped rate of 20%, which in some cases will be stacked on top of other import taxes for a total rate of 45%.
“China urges the United States to correct its erroneous practices and return to the right track of resolving issues through dialogue and consultation,” the Chinese commerce ministry spokesman, He Yadong, said at a regular briefing. “China will closely monitor developments and reserves the right to take necessary measures to firmly defend its legitimate rights and interests.”
Brazil was also singled out in the announcement after trade talks stalled.
Brazil’s government said on Wednesday: “Brazil will not yield to this type of threat”, adding in its statement that the “unilateral decision constitutes an unacceptable action within the framework of the ongoing bilateral negotiating process”.
It voiced particular indignation at US proposals for higher import taxes for raw and refined sugar and a new 10% tariff on aircraft, a change that would hit Embraer, Brazil’s largest aircraft maker.
It added that Brazil would continue to negotiate “without ruling out the possibility of adopting retaliatory measures in response to the illegal and unjust imposition of tariffs on Brazilian products”.
The Trump administration said that the new tariffs would be consistent with trade deals that the US reached last year with dozens of countries. They are being imposed under a law that allowed the president to target countries the US had found to engage in unreasonable or discriminatory trade practices. Jamieson Greer, the US trade representative, told CNBC that many US trading partners had adopted “market distorting practices that tilt the trade balance in their favour while eroding America’s manufacturing base”.
The Trump administration’s new tariff rates would be capped at lower levels ‌agreed under trade deals that have already been concluded or remain under negotiation.
The administration will seek feedback from affected industries and hold a hearing on 25 January, suggesting the final duties won’t take effect until February or March.
Trump met his counterpart Xi Jinping in Shenzhen last week but the escalation in tariffs will not be a surprise to China, Dan Wang, a director on Eurasia Group’s China team, told AFP news agency.
The US and China last year reached a trade truce but that is set to expire on 10 January 2027. Trump and Xi are expected to meet again in Miami on the sidelines of the G20 in December.
“It has become a pattern before every major top leader summit,” Wang said, adding that she did not think the new measures would derail relations between Washington and Beijing.
“Xi has a high incentive to keep bilateral relations stable leading up to the 21st (Communist) party congress in October” 2027, Wang said.
A spokesperson for China’s embassy in Washington rejected allegations that China was ‌a source of excess capacity.
“The so-called issue of ‘industrial overcapacity’ does not really exist and should not be used as a pretext for political manipulation,” the spokesperson said, urging the two sides to jointly deliver on agreements secured between Trump andd Xi.
Earlier, China’s foreign ministry restated its opposition while calling for the US to resolve the issue through dialogue. “A tariff war or a trade war serves no party’s interests,” the spokesperson Guo Jiakun said.
Greer told CNBC that the administration was proposing a “broad exemption” for $30bn worth of Chinese goods in line with the deal. “We’ve talked to our trading partners about this – they know what we’re proposing,” Greer said. “I think a lot of this is baked in.”
Some US analysts criticised the duties as too weak to change China’s trade practices.
China “is a bigger source of excess production than the rest of the world combined,” said Derek Scissors, a senior fellow at the American Enterprise Institute. Yet Wednesday’s proposal “barely raises existing tariffs on China and will make no difference to the country’s trade behaviour.”
The EU said it rejected the premise of new tariffs and expected the US to stick by a deal struck last year. “The EU and the US share a real concern about global overcapacity, much of it state-driven and concentrated in third countries. We regret the lack of joint action,” the European Commission spokesperson Olof Gill said.
Vietnam’s trade ministry said it was working with the US to complete its trade deal as soon as possible and would coordinate with relevant US agencies.
It added it was “preparing and submitting rebuttal comments on the preliminary findings as well as on inaccurate submissions and allegations ‌made by interested parties”.
In response to the US announcement, the Indian government denied it had any structural excess capacity in any sector, adding that it remained committed to an early conclusion of a trade deal between the two countries. Greer has said the tariff on Indian imports would be capped at 17% if a trade deal was struck.
Taiwan was also singled out for new tariffs but Taipei on Thursday said it was confident it would keep its existing 15% tariff rate.
Taipei and Washington signed trade deals in January and February of ‌this year, which secured preferential treatment for semiconductors and other tech products that account for more than 70% of Taiwan’s exports to the US.
Other countries and regions affected by the new tariffs include Bangladesh, ‌Cambodia, the EU, Indonesia, Japan, South Korea, Malaysia, Singapore, Switzerland and Thailand.
Tokyo’s top government spokesperson, Minoru Kihara, said it was “regrettable that a proposed measure targeting Japan has been announced”.
Singapore’s government said it was “very disappointed” with the proposals and denied it was protecting its domestic market or subsidising manufacturing.
Trump’s latest round of tariffs may be here to stay despite the Democrats’ electoral sweep in the US midterms. Some Democrats will probably support some of the tariffs, particularly on China. Trump can veto any legislation that seeks to overturn the import taxes.
However, the Democratic senator Ron Wyden of Oregon, who will take over the finance committee next year, said he would “use every tool at my disposal to end Trump’s tariffs chaos”.
He added: “This is another blatant attempt by Donald Trump to reconstruct his illegal global tariffs. Global overcapacity is a serious problem, but Trump’s unilateral price-hiking tariffs are not the solution.”
