The Alps have a particular hold on the imagination. There are the snow-dusted peaks, naturally, and the villages tucked into their folds, with timber chalets, candlelit restaurants, and views that could make even the most committed city dweller reconsider their priorities. But the pleasures of an Alpine escape extend well beyond a day on the slopes and unlimited raclette.
Vogue’s Guide to the Best Hotels in the Alps:
- The Design-Forward Newcomer: Hôtel Saint-Georges, Megève, France
- The Buzzy Après Spot: Experimental Chalet, Verbier, Switzerland
- The Old-School Luxury Resort: Badrutt’s Palace Hotel, St. Moritz, Switzerland
- The Art-Filled Chalet: Le Coucou, Méribel, France
- The Starry Spot: Gstaad Palace, Gstaad, Switzerland
Across France and Switzerland, the region’s hotels run the gamut from storied grand dames and old-world mountain inns to contemporary retreats where thoughtful architecture and considered design take center stage. Some trade in old-school glamour, others in rustic charm or a welcome degree of seclusion, but the best are worth giving up a powder morning for and staying in. Whether you’re here for serious skiing, fireside dinners, or simply the pleasure of waking up to a panorama of peaks, these are the Alpine hotels in France and Switzerland worth planning a trip around this winter.
France
Les Fermes de Marie, Megève, France
Les Fermes de Marie, a hamlet above Megève, was put together in 1989 by Jean-Louis and Jocelyne Sibuet from Savoyard farm buildings dismantled elsewhere and carted here, stone by stone. The result is a welcome abundance of old timber, antiques, wool, plaid, and animal skins, with fires burning in the appropriate places. (The Sibuets also run La Bastide de Marie in the Luberon and Villa Marie in Saint-Tropez, but this is their grande dame; no small thing from a family this good at hospitality, especially where children are concerned.) Here, they are dispatched to Le Hameau des Enfants, a free club with its own children’s concierge, where 5-to-10-year-olds are kept busy with cooking and ski lessons.
Breakfast, served in the heart of the hotel, in the main restaurant with an enormous fireplace, is not to be slept through (on the menu: homemade jams, Alpine cheeses, farm butter, and exceptional bread). From 2 to 6 p.m. the lounges and library put out homemade Savoie cakes, whipped-cream waffles and the house hot chocolate, after which the bar moves on to wine with slates of local cheese and charcuterie. By evening the main dining room settles into traditional Savoyard cooking by the fire, including a house pâté that has earned its permanent place on the menu. Downstairs, the hotel’s stube is given over entirely to cheese—fondue included—though for dinner in town, the family also owns Le Vieux Megève, a Savoyard institution in the village with as many locals at its tables as guests. The spa’s outdoor hot tubs are best used in falling snow, when the hamlet looks sealed inside a snow globe, and the terraces come heaped with sheepskins and blankets for an evening apéritif under the heat lamps.
Amenities: Spa, restaurants, pool, kids’ club, shuttle, on-site ski rental
Editor’s tip: Stop by L’Épicerie de Megève, the Sibuets’ grocery in the village. The breakfast jams are on the shelves, as is honey from the hotel’s own hives and wine from the family’s Luberon estate, all of which makes for a perfect souvenir.
Address: 163 Chemin des épis, 74120 Megève
Hôtel Saint-Georges, Megève, France
The Saint-Georges is the latest addition to Megève’s hospitality scene. Located right in the center of Megève, a few steps from the pedestrian rue Charles Feige and a short walk from the lifts, the old building has been reimagined by the English designer Luke Edward Hall as a kind of Alpine fairy tale, complete with all the decorative flourishes that the genre demands.
Savoyard folklore provided the starting point, but the 29 rooms take the idea in a cheerfully eccentric direction: Pierre Frey fabrics, painted and patterned headboards, boldly colored bathrooms, floral tiles, deep carpets, and hand-painted murals jostle against carved wood and antiques. On weekend evenings, there’s live piano beside the fire in the bar, lending the place the air of an old mountain inn in which one might reasonably expect a wolf, a princess, or at least a particularly off-kilter uncle to appear before dinner. Le Trappeur, the hotel’s bistro, serves classic French cooking with a few Savoyard dishes, while the spa takes a more elemental approach, built around hot and cold contrast therapy. Families are well catered to, with dedicated rooms and activities for children who may find themselves rather more at home here than in the usual hushed Alpine palace.
Amenities: Spa, restaurant, bar, kids’ club
Address: 159, Rue Monseigneur Conseil, 74120 Megève
Le Coucou, Méribel, France
Le Coucou occupies a large wooden chalet on Méribel’s Belvédère, beside the Rond-Point des Pistes, arranged so that the windows look out over the slopes and a blue run begins just beyond the ski room. It is ski-in, ski-out in the fullest sense: Staff are on hand to unbuckle your boots, lest the final indignity of the day prove too much.
On arrival, one encounters a domed ceiling painted by Matthieu Cossé with owls perched among trees against blue mountain peaks, which establishes the level of seriousness with which the decor is to be taken. Pierre Yovanovitch designed more than 130 pieces of furniture and lighting for the 55 rooms and public spaces—including teddy-shaped armchairs—and set them among terracotta walls, sheepskin, and upwards of 160 artworks, Calder and Chillida included. The bar, done up in yellow, blue, and cream, looks directly onto the pistes through floor-to-ceiling windows. There are indoor and outdoor pools, a Tata Harper spa, and two children’s clubs, should the adults require a little peace. For dinner, Biancaneve serves Italian food; Le Fumoir seats sixteen around a fireplace for fondue; their flagship restaurant Beefbar extends onto a terrace. The whole enterprise is unapologetically theatrical; and though one might reasonably ask how much theater a ski holiday requires, the answer, apparently, is quite a lot.
Amenities: Spa, restaurants, bar, pools, kids’ clubs, ski-in, ski-out, on-site ski rental
Address: 464 Route du Belvédère 73550 Les Allues Méribel
Saint Roch, Courchevel 1850, France
Courchevel has never been short of hotels willing to make a spectacle of luxury. Saint Roch, opening in December on the Bellecôte piste, is Maisons Pariente’s case for the opposite approach, one the family already made persuasively in Paris with Le Grand Mazarin and Méribel’s Le Coucou. Architects Virginie Friedmann and Delphine Versace have recast a longstanding hotel as something closer to a well-traveled aesthete’s chalet, its 37 rooms dressed in dark wood, mohair velvet, agate and onyx by way of Russian dachas and Persian interiors. Pierre Mesguich’s mosaic frescoes and Atelier Toporkoff’s stained glass do more for the place than any amount of gilt would. The living room alone, with its deep sofas and fireplace, makes a decent case for skipping the afternoon session.
Come evening, visit Prunier, the Parisian caviar house’s first alpine restaurant. To unwind further, head downstairs, where you’ll find a ceramic-tiled pool and Tata Harper treatments that take their cue from Ottoman hammams.
Amenities: Spa, pool, ski shop, ski-in, ski-out, on-site ski rental
Address: Piste de Bellecote, 73120 Courchevel 1850
Bourbon Hotel, Courchevel 1850, France
Opening in December from Chapitre Six, the group behind Megève’s Saint-Georges, Bourbon Hotel seems to have concluded that the last thing the resort needs is another chalet. Instead, it takes its cues from a London members’ club, with dark woods, deep velvets, and low lighting. It sits in Courchevel’s Golden Triangle, with direct ski-in, ski-out access to Les Trois Vallées, which is useful, though the more interesting offerings involve what happens after a day on the slopes.
Simonetta, the hotel’s Italian restaurant, will be led by Simone Zanoni, whose Paris credentials include Le George at the George V; this will be his first restaurant in the mountains. The bar pairs cocktails with vinyl sessions, a combination that suggests the après-ski hours here are to be taken at least as seriously as the skiing itself. Below ground, a spa built around contrast therapy offers the usual opportunity to repair the damage before heading out to do it all again. There are 28 rooms and three suites, and pets are permitted on request. Whether Bourbon can make the London-club formula work at altitude remains to be seen, but it is at least a departure from the expected wood-and-antler routine.
Amenities: Spa, restaurant, bar, ski-in, ski-out
Address: 286 Rue Park City, 73120 Courchevel
Les Airelles Val d’Isère, Val d’Isère, France
Les Airelles occupies the Place de Neige, where the village meets the mountain, with direct access to the pistes and the Solaise and Olympique gondolas, as well as a large sun terrace for watching skiers descend. Its 41 rooms, suites, and penthouses have been conceived in the spirit of a medieval Alpine residence, furnished with antiques and artworks drawn from a collection of more than 4,000 pieces. Each has a balcony or terrace, and the penthouses come with their own butler. The Bernard Orcel ski room takes care of the business of getting dressed for the mountain, and, on one’s return, offers hot drinks and crêpes. Dinner can be had at Matsuhisa or Palladio, followed by a stint in the Guerlain spa—the largest in Val d’Isère–or the private cinema.
Children have a grotto-like pool of their own, while dogs receive monogrammed mats, personalized collars, and a dedicated walker to take them out while their owners ski. There is something delightfully over-the-top about a hotel that provides more elaborate arrangements for a dog’s afternoon than most people make for their own, but Les Airelles has never been especially interested in the modest pleasures of Alpine life. Its particular talent is to turn every practical inconvenience into an occasion for service, and every stay into a small performance of aristocratic excess.
Amenities: Spa, restaurants, pools, cinema, ski-in, ski-out, on-site ski rental
Address: 145 Rue de la Poste, 73150 Val d'Isère
Switzerland
Experimental Chalet, Verbier, Switzerland
The skiing is serious in Verbier, but so is the après. Experimental, the Paris group that built its reputation on cocktail bars, chose its first Alpine hotel accordingly, opening a chalet a short walk from the lifts and directly above Farm Club; otherwise known as the nightclub that has kept the village awake since 1971.
For those who have come to ski, the village shop Les Ruinettes delivers rentals to heated lockers downstairs, and a shuttle takes guests to the lifts. The 39 rooms, designed by Fabrizio Casiraghi, favor warm wood, deep colors, and clean lines over the typical heavy chalet style. This ethos extends to the food: Saint Bernard, the restaurant beside reception, gives Alpine classics a lighter touch. Upstairs, the Experimental Cocktail Club draws a mixture of hotel guests and outsiders, while the outdoor hot tub at the Biologique Recherche spa looks directly into the bar, allowing one to order a cocktail through the window without ever getting out of the tub. Starting this November, the bar is introducing a vinyl listening room built around Klipsch speakers, where record selectors stand in for the usual resort DJ.
Amenities: Spa, restaurant, bars, nightclub, hot tub, ski lockers, shuttle
Editor’s tip: On certain weeknights, guests can bring their own records to play in the new listening bar.
Address: Route de Verbier Station 55, 1936 Verbier
The Brecon, Adelboden, Switzerland
Welsh brothers Grant and Craig Maunder, who have been part of Adelboden since Grant first came here in the late seventies, own two hotels in the village; the Brecon, named for the Brecon Beacons, is the smaller, quieter one, housed in a timber building dating to 1914 at the more peaceful end of town. Its interiors look to the ’50s and ’60s, with leather, wool, stone, and crazy-paved Welsh slate underfoot. (Continuing the nod to the owners’ home, you’ll also find Welsh wool blankets lie folded on the beds.)
Each of the 18 rooms and four lofts are television-free; a decision that might once have seemed a little severe but becomes easier to defend when you realize the Adelboden massif occupying the entire window offers a much better thing to stare at. Grant’s wife, the American ceramicist Andrea Anderson, has thrown more than 950 pieces of pottery by hand for the hotel, a level of commitment to the breakfast table that seems almost unreasonable until one sees the results. The Brecon is adults-only and all-inclusive, organized around an open kitchen, with a sauna facing the mountains. While it’s not ski-in, ski-out, a free shuttle runs to the lifts, and a heated Art Deco pool awaits on your return.
Amenities: Restaurant, sauna, pool, shuttle
Editor’s tip: As an all-inclusive adults-only property, food, drinks, and spa access are included in the room rate.
Address: Dorfstrasse 88, 3715 Adelboden
Hotel Bellevue des Alpes, Kleine Scheidegg, Switzerland
At Kleine Scheidegg, beneath the Eiger, the Mönch, and the Jungfrau, the day ends when the last train leaves. The pass is accessible only by the Wengernalp railway, and once the final service has departed, the guests of the Bellevue des Alpes have the mountain largely to themselves.
The inn has stood here for generations: Christian Seiler bought it in 1840, and his descendants, the von Almens, still run it, with Andreas von Almen and his wife Silvia (a flutist), representing the fifth generation. The hotel was last extended in 1948, and little in the public rooms seems to have felt an urgent need for improvement since then. There is the grand piano, the old furnishings, and a bar dating to 1929, its swing door hung with photographs of the climbers and guides who used the hotel as a base camp for attempts on the Eiger’s north face. Guests would watch their progress through telescopes on the terrace, a rather more compelling form of entertainment than anything likely to appear on a hotel television. Clint Eastwood filmed The Eiger Sanction here in 1974, though the mountain itself has never needed Hollywood to establish its credentials. Skiing begins at the front door, and the setting is so imposing that the usual question of what else the hotel offers seems faintly beside the point.
Amenities: Restaurant, bar, terrace, ski-in, ski-out
Address: Kleine Scheidegg, 3823 Wengen
Hotel Chesa Grischuna, Klosters, Switzerland
Klosters acquired its Hollywood connections after the war, when American soldiers interned in Switzerland returned with their families and friends, and the film business followed. Greta Garbo, Gene Kelly, and Rex Harrison all appear in the guest book at the Chesa Grischuna, which earned the nickname “Hollywood on the Rocks”. The Guler family opened the hotel in 1938, and since 2022 it has been run by Zurich entrepreneurs Astrid von Stockar and Clemens Gregor. The entire building is a protected landmark, listed on Switzerland’s national register of historic places.
The thirteen rooms are furnished with antiques and local woodwork, and the old piano bar, whose walls are painted with winter scenes, is now a pasta bar—the natural gathering place after a day on the slopes. But the hotel’s best surprise lies below: a two-lane bowling alley painted by Alois Carigiet, the Swiss illustrator best known for A Bell for Ursli. Raclette is served in the adjoining tavern, which seems an entirely sensible way to recover from an evening of competitive bowling. One can understand why the Hollywood contingent came back: The Chesa Grischuna offers the sort of atmosphere that no amount of set dressing can quite manufacture, and has managed to preserve it without turning its history into a theme park.
Amenities: Restaurants, bar, bowling alley
Address: Bahnhofstrasse 12, 7250 Klosters-Serneus
Chesa Marchetta, Sils Maria, Switzerland
Sils Maria has attracted writers and painters for well over a century. (Nietzsche among them: His summer house stands directly across the road from Chesa Marchetta.) The village lies between the lakes of Sils and Silvaplana, just fifteen minutes from St. Moritz, though it belongs to a rather different tradition of Engadin life—one more interested in contemplation than conspicuous consumption.
Chesa Marchetta occupies four connected farmhouse buildings dating to the sixteenth century. Artfarm, the hospitality company founded by Swiss gallerists Iwan and Manuela Wirth, whose other projects include Scotland’s Fife Arms, spent four years restoring the property before reopening it in January. The architect Luis Laplace retained the original sgraffito—the intricate scratched-plaster decoration that distinguishes the old houses of the valley—and furnished the thirteen rooms with traditional Engadin furniture, antiques, and work by local artists and artisans. Art, in other words, is not an accessory here so much as part of the building’s grammar.
Upstairs, the library and bar form the social heart of the house, a richly layered room where books, beautiful works of art and carefully chosen furnishings make for an atmosphere closer to a collector’s private residence than a conventional hotel lounge. It is the sort of place that invites you to linger over a drink, surrounded by objects with their own stories, without ever feeling overly designed. Downstairs, the restaurant draws on seasonal recipes, an Italian-Swiss mixture that makes good sense in a valley whose geography has never entirely respected national borders. Lake Sils is a fifteen-minute walk away, while Corvatsch is the nearest skiing.
Amenities: Restaurant, bar
Address: Via da Marias 88, 7514 Sils im Engadin/Segl
Badrutt’s Palace Hotel, St. Moritz, Switzerland
There are luxury hotels, and then there are institutions whose very existence has helped define luxury. Badrutt’s Palace, which opened in 1896, belongs rather emphatically to the latter category, having spent more than a century establishing the terms of alpine grandeur. Its unmistakable tower rises above the center of St. Moritz, overlooking the lake and the expensive little theater of Via Serlas, where the international winter set gathers each year to perform its familiar rituals of consumption.
Inside, the atmosphere is grand hotel in the fullest, least apologetic sense: chandeliers, imposing public rooms, formal service, and interiors that understand the importance of making an entrance. The original rooms remain reassuringly traditional, and the lake-facing ones are the ones worth asking for (Hitchcock, for his part, kept Suite 501). The Serlas Wing, opened in 2025, offers a contemporary counterpoint, its 25 rooms and suites by Antonio Citterio and Patricia Viel finished in local stone, oak, and Loro Piana Interiors wool. A three-minute walk from the property leads to Chesa Veglia, a 17th-century farmhouse housing several restaurants and bars, including beloved favorites, like Pizzeria Heuboden, the Carigiet Fondue Stübli and the Polo Bar, among others.
Back in the Palace, Matsuhisa occupies what was once Switzerland’s first indoor tennis hall, which is set to debut a reimagined design this winter by Martin Brudnizki Design Studio, while a new bar designed by Milanese designer Giuseppe Porcelli will welcome guests in Le Grand Hall. Up on Corviglia, Paradiso, the hotel’s mountain restaurant, trades chandeliers for panoramic views, Alpine fare, and a livelier après-ski scene. The Renaissance Bar is where the evening tends to end, preferably with a whisky, a cigar, and a seat by the fire. The spa’s glass-walled infinity pool looks straight out at the lake; even the business of doing very little ought to come with a view.
