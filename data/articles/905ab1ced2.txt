Ousted Paraguayan president placed under house arrest
Arrest ordered shortly after Senate removed him as president
Ousted Paraguayan president Santiago Peña was placed under house arrest Friday on suspicion of breach of trust, shortly after the Senate removed him from office over the collapse of a major bank tied to his government.
All 44 senators present voted to remove Peña for misconduct in office, making him the second Paraguayan president ousted by impeachment since the country returned to democracy in 1989. The verdict came three days after regulators took over Ueno Bank, locking nearly half of Paraguay's more than six million people out of their savings and bank cards and setting off the country's worst political crisis in more than a decade.
Prosecutors ordered that Peña be held at his home until he appears before their economic crimes unit on Saturday.
"We have confirmed that he is present and has himself available to the authorities for all legal proceedings," said Silvia González, one of the prosecutors leading the case. Breach of trust carries a sentence of up to five years in prison, or up to 10 in serious cases.
The order also called for the arrest of Carlos Carvallo, who ran the finance company that became Ueno until Peña named him central bank chief in 2023. It was not immediately clear whether Carvallo was in custody.
Lawmakers accused Peña of using his political influence to steer hundreds of millions of dollars in public funds into Ueno while regulators overlooked risky insider lending. Peña has denied wrongdoing.
Vice-President Pedro Alliana was sworn in as president Friday to serve out the term, which ends in August 2028. Alliana, who also is under investigation in connection with the Ueno case, inherits a government under pressure to return depositors' money and punish those behind the bank's collapse.

In a farewell message released shortly before the verdict, Peña expressed hope his departure would spare Paraguay further harm.
"I do not want my presence to be a cause of greater division or instability," he wrote. "My responsibility is to put the country above my personal fate."
Growing public outrage
Public outrage has become more thunderous by the day. As senators deliberated Friday, crowds of protesters demanding their frozen savings back and accountability for Ueno's failure marched through downtown Asunción chanting, "Santi Peña to Tacumbú!" — a reference to the capital's main prison, where Ueno's founder and former CEO, Miguel Vázquez, is now being held.
"This was an enormous betrayal of trust," said Yinal López, a worker at the state electricity company, whose employee pension fund also had money at Ueno.
Peña's fall, from feted Washington ally meeting with U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent in late October to prisoner in his own home, came at dizzying speed.
Regulators seized the bank before dawn Tuesday. By Thursday afternoon, Peña had offered to resign as his conservative Colorado Party turned on him, led by former president Horacio Cartes, the party chief and Peña's longtime mentor.
By Friday, his interior minister, chief of staff, legal adviser and political adviser had all quit, and he was under arrest, with impeachment denying him the immunity he would have enjoyed if the Senate had accepted his resignation.
A telegenic technocrat and a former International Monetary Fund economist, Peña owed his political ascent to Cartes. The tobacco magnate, among Paraguay's richest men, made Peña his finance minister and twice backed him for president over the objections of rival factions.
'Like a Shakespearean drama'
Once in office, though, Peña quietly amassed a rival power base, analysts say. Ueno, flush with public funds, became its linchpin.
"It's like a Shakespearean drama. He tried to displace his mentor, his political patron," said Miguel Carter, director of DEMOS, a Paraguayan research centre. "But he did so on very fragile grounds."
Police stand guard outside of a closed Ueno Bank branch in Nemby, Paraguay, on Tuesday. (Cesar Olmedo/Reuters)
Cartes struck back. In September, senators loyal to him demanded answers from the central bank about Ueno's lending, citing leaked internal reports that warned its loans to related companies put its solvency at risk.
When Ueno collapsed this week, so did Peña's leverage, and Cartes, once sanctioned by the U.S. Treasury over alleged corruption, publicly called for the ouster of his erstwhile protégé.
"Cartes put the nail in the coffin," said Andrew Nickson, a Paraguay expert at the University of Birmingham. "Peña had no political base to rely on, and he was left high and dry."
