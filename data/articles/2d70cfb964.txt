The wife of a Japanese journalist arrested in Russia has made her first public comments since his detention last month, ramping up pressure on authorities to secure his release.
In a handwritten letter shared with The Moscow Times, Nozomi Imai called for her husband, Yuki, to be freed from a Moscow prison, saying she and his parents have spent “every day worrying about him.”
“It has now been more than a month since my husband was detained. Since that day, our family has remained trapped in grief,” she wrote in the letter.
Yuki Imai, a 34-year-old reporter for The Hokkaido Shimbun newspaper, was arrested on espionage charges last month in the Far East city of Yuzhno-Sakhalinsk. The incident marked the highest-profile detention of a foreign journalist in Russia since 2023’s arrest of Wall Street Journal reporter Evan Gershkovich.
The Hokkaido Shimbun and the Japanese government have denied Russia’s allegations that Imai was collecting intelligence related to Russia’s defense capabilities.
As The Moscow Times previously reported, a shroud of silence that initially hung over the case has started to dissipate in recent weeks. Former colleagues shocked by the charges against Imai have begun speaking out, sharing memories of working together and advocating for his release.
The statement from Imai’s wife represents the first time a member of his family has spoken publicly about the case. Her account reveals details about the arrest and Imai’s background that have largely been obscured by that silence.
According to Nozomi Imai, who herself is a reporter for the Mainichi Shimbun newspaper, on the day he was arrested, her husband was supposed to leave Russia, have a layover in Beijing and then arrive back in Tokyo the next day, capping off a two-year assignment as a foreign correspondent. He is normally communicative, and when she didn’t hear from him and he didn’t answer his phone during the morning of Oct. 13, she grew concerned.
Seeing a video widely circulated by Russian media of her husband being wrestled to the ground in a hotel room and placed in handcuffs, Imai’s wife said she “couldn’t breathe.”
“In the video, I could even see the souvenirs in his suitcase — the gifts he was bringing home for us,” she said. “I could not sleep even for a moment that night. When morning came, it still did not feel real. I could not even cry.”
Imai had been “so looking forward” to returning to Japan, she said. He was especially looking forward to seeing his 21-month-old daughter, who was born while he was away on assignment in Russia.
“He had even said excitedly that he wanted to be the one to pick her up from daycare on the very day he got home,” Nozomi recalled.
If convicted, Imai faces up to 20 years in prison. He is currently remanded in Moscow’s Lefortovo Prison, the same facility that housed Gershkovich, until Dec. 12, although it’s likely he will remain imprisoned for longer as he awaits trial.
Japanese Ambassador to Russia Akira Muto and other officials visited Imai in prison this week in what was only the second time that consular staff has been able to meet with the detained journalist. The visit comes just days after Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi appealed directly to President Vladimir Putin for Imai’s release in a meeting on the sidelines of the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit, according to Japanese media.
Imai’s wife said that she and her husband bonded over a shared interest in Russian culture. They watched Russian movies, listened in the car to the songs of the rock musician Viktor Tsoi and the pianist Denis Matsuev and discussed their favorite Russian poets and painters.
Imai’s interest in Japan-Russia relations, she believes, stemmed from his family history: his grandfather was born and raised on the island of Sakhalin, which lies less than 50 kilometers (31 miles) from Japan’s northern island Hokkaido, and Imai always felt he had roots there.
In her letter, Nozomi said that the episode has been deeply worrying for Imai’s elderly parents. She also lamented the possibility that Imai could lose precious time with his daughter as she is growing up, including celebrations for Christmas, the New Year and her second birthday.
She recalled a happy memory of visiting Imai in Yuzhno-Sakhalinsk not long after their daughter was born. He was thrilled that her first trip abroad was to Russia, and in preparation, he went to the store and came back with lots of children’s items.
As they were walking down the street, pushing her in the stroller, Nozomi said her husband was content.
“I wish moments like this could last forever,” he said.
The Moscow Times has republished Nozomi Imai’s letter below.
Dear everyone,
My name is Nozomi Imai. I am the wife of Yuki Imai, a Hokkaido Shimbun reporter who is being held by the Russian authorities. Since his detention, his parents and I have spent every day worrying about him. During this time, many people have expressed concern and offered us words of encouragement. Their kindness has given us strength, and I am deeply grateful.
Oct. 13 was the day my husband was supposed to leave Yuzhno-Sakhalinsk after completing his two-year assignment as a correspondent and begin his journey home. He was due to fly back to Tokyo, via Beijing, and arrive at Haneda Airport on Oct. 14.
He had been so looking forward to coming home. During his posting, our daughter was born. She is now 21 months old, and he loves her more than words can say. He had even said excitedly that he wanted to be the one to pick her up from daycare on the very day he got home.
And yet, from the morning of the 13th, I had a terrible feeling that something was wrong. My husband is the kind of person who always keeps me updated every step of the way — “Do you think I have enough souvenirs?”, “I’ve finished packing,” “I’m heading to the airport now,” “I’ve arrived at the airport,” “I’m boarding the plane.” But that day, I suddenly could not reach him at all.
I called again and again, but there was no answer. I kept praying and telling myself, “Surely he must already be on the plane.” Then came the news that he had been detained.
I saw the footage showing my husband being taken away. I could hardly breathe. It felt as if my heart were being ripped apart.
No one could remain calm after seeing a beloved family member with his arms bound behind his back. In the video, I could even see the souvenirs in his suitcase — the gifts he was bringing home for us. I could not sleep even for a moment that night. When morning came, it still did not feel real. I could not even cry.

Russia has always held a special place in our family’s life.
My husband has long felt that some of his roots lie in Sakhalin (Karafuto), where his grandfather was born and raised. Perhaps that is why he cares so deeply about Russia and Japan-Russia relations, and why he devoted so much time to studying and understanding them.
“I have to study Russian,” he insisted, persuading his parents to let him take an extra year to prepare for university entrance exams. During his five and a half years at university, he apparently shut himself away in the library and watched Soviet films endlessly.
He used to describe St. Petersburg, where he studied, as “a dreamlike place,” and he read Pushkin’s “The Bronze Horseman” again and again. He dreamed that one day he would travel there with his family and show us that special city.
After joining the Hokkaido Shimbun, wherever he was posted — Nemuro, Kushiro, or Tokyo — he continued attending Russian language classes. I believe learning Russian has always been a source of pure joy for him.
At one point, through the visa-free exchange program, he even hosted residents from the South Kuril Islands (the Northern Territories) in his home in Nemuro. He treasured the photographs from that time. In one of them, everyone is gathered around the table in his small, old apartment, smiling.
Russia is also what brought my husband and me together. We became friends as students because we shared a love of Russian culture. Whenever we met, we would talk about Soviet and Russian films we had seen recently, or novels by Russian writers we had read. On drives together, the car would be filled with the voice of Viktor Tsoi or Denis Matsuev’s powerful performances of Shostakovich. Over beer and whisky at izakaya pubs, we talked about Mayakovsky’s poetry and Kandinsky’s paintings.
Our wedding anniversary is Sept. 25, Shostakovich’s birthday. So whenever we celebrate our marriage, we are also celebrating his birth.
Last September, I traveled to Yuzhno-Sakhalinsk with our daughter, who was six months old at that time. My husband was overjoyed that her very first trip abroad was to Russia. He had gone to children’s stores and bought all sorts of things to prepare for our visit. As the three of us walked through Yuzhno-Sakhalinsk with our daughter in her stroller, he said, “I wish moments like this could last forever.” We took our daughter to the sea, the zoo, museums and many other places.
Those days have become irreplaceable family memories.
When his assignment ended, he told me what he had said in his farewell remarks: “I hope my daughter will one day do work that can serve as a bridge between Japan and Russia.”

It has now been more than a month since my husband was detained. Since that day, our family has remained trapped in grief.
When he returned briefly to Japan in September this year, he put together a potty for our daughter. It became her favorite, and now she can go to the toilet by herself. Her vocabulary has grown so much, too. Sometimes she looks down the dark hallway and says, “Papa’s not here.”
Like any little child, she says, “Me do it! Me do it!” and insists on trying to do things by herself. It fills me with joy, but also with deep sadness and loneliness, because I know that if her papa were here, he would be clapping with delight at every new step she takes.
The precious time in which a child grows up can never be returned to us. Christmas, New Year’s, our daughter’s second birthday — her papa may not be there for any of them.
My husband’s elderly parents are also living in deep fear. They worry whether they will ever see their beloved only son again in their lifetime. My mother-in-law, whose doctor has warned her of the risk of a subarachnoid hemorrhage, wept as she said, “I want to sit in front of the prison and cry out that they should take me instead of him.”
Yuki Imai is not only a journalist. He is a beloved son, husband and father.
Please — please return this precious papa to his little daughter.
I ask with all my heart for your help and support in securing his release as soon as possible.
