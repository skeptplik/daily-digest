Astronomers are pushing back against Elon Musk’s Starmind program to place a huge number of AI computers in space, which was given the green light to proceed by federal communications regulators earlier this week.
The American Astronomical Society said it was “disheartened and dismayed” by the decision by the Federal Communications Commission (FCC) decision to grant SpaceX a license to deploy as many as 1m satellites in low Earth orbit as a way of moving datacenters into space.
The society said the proposed satellite constellation “would significantly undermine decades of federal investment in astronomical facilities and the ability of astronomers and the public to enjoy and study the night sky”.
The American Astronomical Society has previously raised concerns about the cumulative impact of reflective, bright satellites on astronomy, both optical and infrared, as well as potentially harmful radio frequency interference affecting radio astronomy.
In addition, it said, “atmospheric pollution from the launches and reentries of so many satellites, as well as increased collision risks in orbit, remain significant concerns; both of these factors threaten research astronomy.”
US federal regulators approved a constellation of satellites to process data in space on Wednesday, saying that they would “support America’s booming super intelligence economy”.
The FCC said in a statement that regulators had approved 1m Starmind orbital satellites, or compute modules, built by Elon Musk’s SpaceX.
They said that the authorization “promises to bring the human race closer to a Kardashev II civilization – one that can harness the full power of the sun”.
“America is leading the world into the era of super intelligence,” said the FCC chair, Brendan Carr.
Proponents of the plan argue that datacenters in space are necessary to offset the electricity and water demands of Earth-based AI computing.
SpaceX argued in its submission to the FCC: “By directly harnessing near-constant solar power with little operating or maintenance costs, these satellites will achieve transformative cost and energy efficiency while significantly reducing the environmental impact associated with terrestrial datacenters.”
A report published by the government accountability office (GAO) in July said that space-based datacenters “could reduce the land, electricity, and water needed for data centers on Earth” but warned of “engineering and economic barriers to deployment”.
The GAO said datacenters generate excess heat, but space does not cool computing hardware efficiently and “could be a major engineering challenge”. Further, it said, “a significant increase in the number of satellites in orbit could be difficult to manage and cause collisions.”
Under the license approved on Wednesday, SpaceX must begin deployment no later than November 2033, deploy 10% of the maximum number – or 100,000 – no later than November 2035 and deploy 50% by November 2038, the FCC said.
Approximately 17,000 ‌satellites currently exist, but there are rising concerns about the amount of space debris in higher orbits. The FCC deferred SpaceX’s request to dispose of satellites located above 600km in space “graveyard” orbits.
Amazon’s rival satellite unit, Leo, and others have criticized the plan, suggesting SpaceX was unlikely to deploy all the satellites under the approval.
Opposition to space-based datacenters has also been raised in a report published earlier this month by Columbia University’s Center on Global Energy Policy.
The paper, Space-Based Data Centers Roadmap: The Case for Caution, said that space-based datacenters were likely to become a reality before the risks of large-scale deployment can be fully understood or governed.
“To put even 10% of the artificial intelligence data centers anticipated by 2030 into orbit would require more than 700 launches of large rockets every year, about two per day, in perpetuity,” it said.
In a chapter titled “Night Sky and Astronomy”, the authors warned large satellite constellations “could cause a dramatic transformation of night skies around the world” as well as “large debris fields in low Earth orbit, loss of life and property damage from de-orbiting satellites, depletion of the stratospheric ozone layer and increased global warming”.
It added: “The current governance regimes will not be sufficient to manage these risks, especially as addressing many of the challenges will need to be coordinated on a global scale.”
