This week offers a good opportunity to spot Mercury, the smallest planet in the solar system and one of the most difficult to observe. On 20 November, it reached “greatest western elongation”, the point at which it appears farthest from the sun in the morning sky. The planet remains well placed for observation over the next few mornings.
The chart shows the view looking east-south-east from London at 0630 GMT on 24 November. Mercury appears low above the horizon, while the much brighter planet Venus shines higher in the sky, close to Spica, the brightest star in the constellation of Virgo, the virgin.
Because Mercury never strays far from the sun, you will need an unobstructed view towards the horizon between 0600 and 0615 GMT, when the sky will still be relatively dark. Mercury should already be visible as a moderately bright, star-like object below and to the left of Venus.
Binoculars may help locate the elusive world, but be sure to stop using them before sunrise to avoid accidentally looking towards the dangerously bright sun.
For observers in the southern hemisphere, Mercury will appear lower above the horizon and so will be more difficult to spot.
