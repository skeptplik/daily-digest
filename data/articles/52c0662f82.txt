What was it that Nvidia’s board was saying about there being no chance of Jensen Huang coming back?
The position – a mere six days ago – was supposed to be definitive. The exit of the co-founder came after “disclosure of personal behaviour inconsistent with the company’s code of conduct, standards and cultural norms”, said Harvey Jones, who had been rapidly installed as chair of the chipmaker. The board “does not intend to reinstate Jensen as CEO” after he “voluntarily resigned”, added Jones.
The contribution of the US treasury secretary was equally forceful. Scott Bessent was already focused on the need for a new-broom external hire to replace Huang amid the scrutiny over Nvidia’s compliance with US export controls. “They need to go outside the company,” he said, calling for “a dramatic shake-up”.
Those statements are now inoperative, in the famous phrase of Ron Ziegler. The board says it has “every confidence in Jensen” and his “candor and ability to lead Nvidia”.
What prompted this humiliating U-turn? At least Jones and his fellow directors didn’t bother to disguise the fact they’d been lent on. There was “input from senior levels of the United States government”, which can only mean somebody above Bessent. Sure enough, Donald Trump said he convinced the board to reinstate Huang, calling him “a great patriot and national treasure” and adding that the US government had no reason to think his return posed a threat to national security.
The most jaw-dropping detail is that Nvidia’s board hasn’t even completed its investigation into reports that Huang was “the target of a foreign intelligence effort”, the only formal reference to his alleged affair with Alice Huang (no relation), the former boss of a Singapore-based company suspected by US officials of smuggling Nvidia chips into China.
Why even bother continuing the inquiry when we can safely guess that it will conclude that the boss did nothing seriously wrong? As for Huang’s two alleged affairs with Nvidia subordinates – relationships that would usually be a sackable offence at a US company – the board will probably never reference them again.
Huang, with his big tech petition-signing crew shouting their support, has proved too big to fire. At Open AI in 2023 Sam Altman was fired and reinstated four days later. The process has taken only slightly longer at Nvidia.
The clinching factor, one can speculate, was the fall in AI share prices after Huang’s resignation. From Trump’s standpoint, a bursting of the AI valuation bubble represented a danger to the last two years of his presidency, so best to give investors (or most of them) what they wanted and get the “godfather of AI” back at the wheel of the circular financing carousel. After the hundreds of billions of dollars wiped off Nvidia’s valuation in recent days, $1tn was instantly wiped back on.
The gamble won’t necessarily work for ever, of course. Bessent, before starting his own U-turn on Huang’s position in recent days, was asking the right questions. Congressional scrutiny of Nvidia’s export controls won’t stop just because the company has belatedly formed a committee to oversee trade compliance and national security risks. One trusts Marco Rubio, the US secretary of state, will still be pressed for details of the famous intelligence memo he reportedly circulated in the spring identifying Alice Huang as a Chinese spy. Huang himself can no longer be taken seriously on the subject of export controls.
But it is Nvidia’s board that emerges looking idiotic. The directors have reversed the biggest decision they had to make. They have done so under political pressure, forfeiting any claim to independence. And, even as Huang returned, there was no real signal that his powers will be checked to create a semblance of conventional governance. The patsy directors should resign out of pure embarrassment.
