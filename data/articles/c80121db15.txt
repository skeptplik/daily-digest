A tropical storm warning couldn’t stop Mele Hamasaki and Daniel Croix Henderson from going on their first date. “I think we both had a special feeling about each other and were just way too excited to postpone,” Mele says of their May 6, 2021 meeting. “The connection was instantaneous and life-altering.” Sitting at Tantalus Lookout in Honolulu, the two discussed nature, their creative processes, and more. “As we both had hoped, the storm never came,” says Mele. “Instead, we were blessed with a stunning sunset and an empty park entirely for us!”
“All these years later, I look at the life we’ve built together—our individual and shared dreams coming into focus, the films we’ve co-created, the communities we’ve rooted in, and the curiosity we still bring to one another—and realize our first date was a microcosm of everything that followed,” adds Daniel, an actor and filmmaker.
On New Year's Day in 2025, Mele and Daniel returned to that very same spot to watch the sunset over a picnic. Daniel had an engagement ring on hand, ready to propose. “I kept checking my pocket over and over again on the day for the ring, realizing I’d never held an object so precious to me,” he remembers. “I’ll never forget the moment when she said ‘yes!’ Tears welled up in our eyes and I felt like I’d entered a portal.” Says Mele, “At that point, I knew I was going to spend the rest of my life with Daniel but I’ve always found the ritual and ceremony of making a life-long commitment to a person and their family really profound and courageous.” When they returned to their home, Daniel coordinated with Mele’s mom and aunts to have the space adorned with flowers and vines from her “Popo’s” house. Adds Mele, “Always the romantic, this guy!”
When the bride and groom chose where to tie the knot, they decided to maintain the same view as when they first met. A friend’s family offered their historic Honolulu home, which provided a dramatic backdrop of the city and ocean. “We live in town and have the most pilina (connection) as a couple to the ridges and waters of Honolulu,” explains Mele, who grew up on Oʻahu. “So having the wedding on the south side of the island was very important to us. Our intention for having it on the peak of the Lion’s Gate Portal was to fill the world with as much aloha as we could!” Notes Daniel, “Our friend’s family opening their home to our wedding was an act of kindness and generosity that we’ll always remember.”
As a designer and producer in her day-to-day life, Mele put her skills to work when planning the wedding. “As soon as we reframed wedding planning as pre-production, everything locked into place for us,” she says. “We hired ‘department heads,’ scheduled fittings and rehearsals, managed the budget, and sent out call sheets to our guests. I used my design skills to collage and mock up everything I could think of to ensure details were visualized!” The bride also partnered with wedding planner Karisa Ramolete Hayashi of Say I Do By Karisa to direct the show. “Karisa grew up right down the street from the venue, which felt very aligned,” says the bride. “It was important for us to work with someone who was connected to Hawaiʻi in the same way we are.”
The couple also called on their community to bring their celebration to life. “I was so moved by how many friends contributed their creative gifts and energy toward preparation,” reflects the groom. “Our family members and friends from my bachelor party came to the house before the wedding several times to help prepare. It took an island of love, creativity, and generosity to bring this all together.”
That community also guided the couple as they sought out their wardrobes for the weekend. The bride kicked off the wedding weekend in an ivory look by Hawaiian designer Rocket Ahuna. She first connected with the designer when attending his debut show on the island. “He ended up designing an entire capsule wardrobe for me which I got to wear on my bachelorette in Kauaʻi and throughout the wedding weekend!” Mele says.
Mele admits she struggled to find a ceremony dress for a full year and almost wore a vintage dress she didn’t truly love. “Luckily, my very bossy cousin Aja Dang demanded I keep trying and reminded me that I should never settle,” says Mele. “My instinct told me to check out Loho Bride in West Hollywood one more time before it was too late. Aja and my friend Helix came with me and encouraged me to try on the Calico Grace Gown from Bon Bride.” The gown featured raw stitching details, a bleached calico fabric—“perfect for the humidity of Honolulu summers”—and draping that lent a sexier touch to the shape. “This was the first dress that finally felt truly me,” the bride says.
To accessorize, Mele paired the dress with a pleated handkerchief veil from Danielle Frankel, “which brought the whole look together and made me feel like an ethereal queen!” The bride’s friend Karen of Jos Mundo gifted her a custom pair of bridal shoes that were a take on the brand’s Marikina Mule. “It was so fun to go through the design process with her,” Mele remembers. “It was the first time she had her team emboss initials inside the shoe—a customization they will now be offering to all brides!” As for beauty, Mele worked with Risa Hoshino for makeup, and Jonah Dela-Cruz to craft two hair looks for the night. She says, “He understood the assignment!”
Daniel knew he “had to look sharp” for his wedding day. “I wanted to wear something with enough formality to honor the moment, while also having a free-flowing, playful, and creative twist that felt danceable and like myself,” he says. The groom looked back through family photos for inspiration as well. “My stylist Henna Koskinen and I spoke a lot about these influences, and when I tried on the Dries Van Noten suit, I felt a special energy—and everything felt right,” he says. He paired his suit with a textured Cult Gaia shirt and Saint Laurent loafers.
For his second look, Daniel worked with Isabel Sicat, designer of Toqa, to create an ensemble filled with small talismans that connected him with the ocean. “Isabel’s team did extraordinary work—every shell, bead, and charm was hand-sewn into the raw silk shantung dyed tuxedo-inspired trouser and tailcoat-like shirt,” he explains. “The cowrie shell adornment was of particular significance to me—an amulet of protection which connects generations of my ancestors. Found in West Africa and along the coasts of Georgia and the Carolinas, [it’s] known as a symbol of prosperity, protection, and power.”
When Mele’s original plan for a second look fell through, Sicat was able to craft her a complementary look to wear as she danced the hula later in the evening. “Without hesitation, she immediately stepped up to the plate and hustled to create a custom heat-treated top and pleated wrap skirt that perfectly matched Daniel’s look,” says Mele. “It was a dream to wear.”
The wedding weekend kicked off with a welcome picnic in Kapiʻolani Park. Mele and Daniel produced a public art show in the same spot with artist Noe Tanigawa a year before that commemorated the 80th anniversary of the bombing of Hiroshima and Nagasaki. “Reflecting on this project one year later reminded us of the delicacy of peace and our responsibility to live in harmony with the natural world, with our relationships, and with ourselves,” she says. The welcome event was full of connection and crafts. “At each table we had a setup to make lei with string and needle, which nourished the many days ahead—adorning our ancestral altar and decorating a lot of the areas of the wedding,” shares Daniel. “Shoutout to Karisa’s aunty for the pounds of puakenikeni and crown flower from her yard!”
The next day, the couple came together with their friends and family at their ceremony overlooking Honolulu. Mele’s mom officiated, Daniel’s mom shared a passage, and their fathers served as ring bearers and also performed a water unity ceremony. “When we were up there between the Lion’s Gate Portal and pillars Ren of Renko Floral designed, it felt otherworldly but also very quotidian—like it was just us, holding hands in the park like we always do,” says Mele. Daniel remembers the entire ceremony as deeply moving and memorable. He shares that he felt, “Elated, floating, vibing, teary, hyperpresent—witnessing Mele in these moments was truly the most beautiful sight, and holding hands with her through it all was deeply grounding.” The newlyweds remember being filled with joy (and a bit of surprise at impact) as their loved ones showered rice over them.
The rest of the evening was filled with connection, happiness, and plenty of music. After speeches, the bride’s father recited a poem as the groom’s father played bass, Hawaiian music legend Brother Noland played his hits, the bride danced hula, and the groom sang “Knocks Me Off My Feet" with their band Hawaiian Soul. “What felt the most special was watching Mele dance hula,” says Daniel. “She danced as a kid but stepped away from it for over a decade, and only recently returned to the practice. It was special to witness her magic and talent in this way, and I’ll never forget her glow!” Says Mele, “It was a total portal of love, high vibration, and celebration!”
The after-party was held indoors in a space transformed by artist Bradley Capello into a “pink jewel box” filled with mirrors, disco balls, and a large Pegge Hopper cutout. “Dancing the night away on the lauhala mats that my mom and her lauhala friends wove and resting on the Tutuvi pillows they sewed was a truly magical experience,” shares Mele. “You could feel the aloha through your feet and tush!” A low-key beach day followed, giving friends and family time to say goodbye. “Soaking in salt water was the perfect way to process everything,” says Daniel.
Both the bride and groom share that they don’t take their wedding or their love for granted. “I think celebrating love and choosing love is a superpower,” says Mele.
