There are few places where the unwieldiness and malleability of time take center stage quite like when one is on an airplane. It is one of the few times when the world allows for a kind of self-indulgence: You can sleep, catch up on your favorite TV shows, watch a guilty pleasure movie, read a book, or listen to a podcast that you never had time for while on the ground, or you could even raw-dog the flight, just you and your thoughts, getting lost as you hurl through the air at 580 miles per hour inside a hunk of aluminum and other composite materials.
In 2010, the artist Nina Katchadourian was sitting on a plane at LaGuardia Airport in New York on her way to Atlanta, begrudging all of the frequent flying that was keeping her away from her studio and her work. “I thought, ‘Here I am again, about to spend three hours flying somewhere, and it feels as if this time already doesn’t count,’ ” she recalls thinking. “It’s garbage time.” But Katchadourian, whose interdisciplinary approach has led her to work with a wide array of mediums and materials (photography, performance, sculpture, “audio interventions,” a popcorn machine), called herself out. “I thought [it was] a really bad attitude for somebody like me, who claims as an artist to be interested in mundane things and everyday things and believes that things often deserve a closer look.” She challenged herself to “make things the whole way to Atlanta” with whatever she could find around her, photographed with her iPhone. The first “improvisations,” as she called them, included classic airplane objets like pretzels (see them resting comfortably on a king bed), straws (turned into bridges over a mountainscape, and the shiny buckle on the seatbelt (a perfect medium for a self-portrait). “By the time I got to Atlanta, I thought, ‘I need to keep doing this because something just happened and I need to see where it will go.’ ” She’d work on it until 2021, when the pandemic provided a natural pause.
Now the entire project has been published as a small tome appropriately titled Seat Assignment. The hundreds of images are a testament to how curiosity about the world can open and create an alternate universe that lives exactly within the limits of our own, and Katchadourian is open to both the humorous (a little puppet face made from a bread roll with blueberries for eyes and a strawberry tongue, politicians and businessmen with noses and beards made out of pieces of croissants and radishes, a pile of sweater that resembles a gorilla) and the sublime (a Stonehenge-esque architectural formation made from mint Life Savers, mountains dotted with sweater-fuzz black clouds, mysterious trees with twisted-napkin vines that wrap around them). On a particularly long flight from San Francisco to Auckland, New Zealand, she holed up in the lavatory and produced a series of portraits “in the Flemish style,” repurposing toilet-seat covers and paper towels into dramatic collars and hats. The portraits are uncanny, often capturing the masters’ chiaroscuro effect.
She quickly set some ground rules as she embarked on the project: She wouldn’t bring anything to “make” art on the plane, could only work with what she found, and would only use her iPhone to document what she’d made. “I felt like doing [those things] would make everything too easy, and it would make it all too much like ‘art,’ ” she explains. Curiously, during the years she worked on Seat Assignment, the quality of the camera on the iPhone improved so much that it being part of her rules became less important. “In those early days, people would say to me, ‘Well, why aren’t you using a real camera?’ ” she explains. “That question disappeared after a while.”
In a way, it seems like it was only a matter of time before Katchadourian embarked on an airplane project. As a child, she traveled every summer with her parents and younger brother from Northern California, where she grew up, to a small island off the south coast of Finland, where her mother’s family was from. The island was hugely influential on how she saw the world (“I’ve made a lot of artwork there, and I really feel like my approaches to artmaking have been learned there”), but so was the long flight itself. She’d make up games with her brother, trying to guess the movie that would play during the flight (back in the day when there was only one television screen for passengers to watch). On one occasion, her mother brought sheets and blankets and made a bed and pillows for the two young children on the floor beneath their seats. “They would never let you do this now, but I remember when it was my turn to sleep down there, looking to the side and seeing this landscape of legs that went through the whole plane. It was amazing, an underworld of under the seats.” She adds, “The whole plane was a place that we were all temporary residents of, and it wasn’t a place I disliked at all. It was this space that took us from one place I loved to another place I loved.”
This point of view has guided much of her work, like “Sorted Books,” another ongoing project she started in 1993, where she organizes book collections—whether belonging to a friend or an institution—into poems or literary fragments; “The Genealogy of the Supermarket,” which began in 2005, where she organizes all of the “people” that feature in grocery products (Betty Crocker, Orville Redenbacher, the Gerber Baby) into a genealogical tree; and “Mended Spiderwebs,” where she completed spiderwebs with delicate red thread. Though disparate, her projects always invite audiences to truly look at the world around them. “I think for the first 10 years it used to concern me a lot that I never did the same things two times in a row,” Katchadourian says. “After a while, I realized that’s actually a methodology. It’s not a deficit; it’s a way of working. A way of saying the medium is secondary to the approach.”
“I think a lot of projects for me have come out of a ‘what if?’ sort of question that I ask myself, and then I try to sort out an inquiry,” she says. Curiosity and happenstance, or the idea of chance, are essential in Katchadourian’s practice. “The weird thing about research is that you don’t always know that you’re doing it, so sometimes you’re reading or having experiences and only later do you realize, ‘Oh, all that curiosity was actually in service of trying to figure this thing out.’ ” She continues, “I think you have to take diversions seriously because they are showing you something back if you’re willing to observe yourself doing it. There’s something in it that often holds a lot of information.” It’s also what her work does.
