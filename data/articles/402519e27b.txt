"It's just outrageous. It's an injustice. As family members, we're absolutely disgusted. This is an insult," says Duncan Chard.
The families also say their grief has been made worse by how police have handled their loved ones' remains and belongings since the fire.
'Issue the damned alert!'
Michael Chard built his house in BÃ©dar from scratch. "He called it Casa Micas," his son said. "It was his place in the sun, it was his little paradise."
On the morning of the fire, Michael's partner Christine had met friends and was due to go to the hairdresser. "Then everything changed... the whole world changed for them," he said.
Duncan Chard said his father, who had two artificial knees and walked with a stick, left his house with Christine as soon as they saw the flames. They left their phones and passports behind and tried to escape by car, but the road was blocked.
"This is an action of a brave person who was fighting to the end," he said.
Andalusia's emergencies chief, Antonio Sanz, has said the region decided not to use the system that sends emergency alerts to mobile phones because it could not be tailored to specific communities.
Officials have said some residents needed to evacuate while others were safer staying in their homes. They also argued an alert could have caused panic and traffic jams.
The families reject that.
"Issue the damned alert!" said Chard. "Ring the bells, send the police guy out with his horn, go door to door, do everything."
Thomas-Wolf Verdonckt, a Belgian virologist whose father Stanislas, 63, died, was on the phone to him as the flames approached.
He says the victims "never received advice on what they should do, and certainly not warnings that they were in danger", and that the authorities' claim that everyone in the area was given adequate warning is not true.
"The only contact that the government managed to make with the victims was a couple of minutes before the flames engulfed everyone. One police car drove by with the sirens on. There was no talking, there was no messaging."
BÃ©dar's emergency plan was last approved in 2019, and regional officials say it was in the process of being updated. The municipality is classified by the Andalusian government as being at risk from wildfires.
Of the 15 people who died in BÃ©dar, 14 were foreign nationals: nine British citizens, three Belgian, one French and one American. The other victim was Spanish.
In the days after the fire, Sanz faced a backlash for appearing to blame the victims. He said some people who made the decision to leave "at the last minute, took the wrong path".
Verdonckt has been trying to find answers ever since the blaze.
"The morning afterwards, the Spanish officials brought out... a version of events which was simply the complete opposite of what I experienced," he said.
He also believes a public inquiry is needed to prevent a similar disaster happening in Spain in the future.
"They are putting the safety of citizens in second place. That's simply not okay."
'A huge hole in our lives'
Danni Gillam-Kirton, an assistant headteacher from Nottinghamshire, lost her parents Pete and Fran in the fire.
They had lived in BÃ©dar since 2018. Fran, a former headteacher, ran a quiz night that raised money for the local school and church.
"They were fantastic parents, my biggest champions and cheerleaders," said Danni. "They leave a huge, huge hole in our lives."
She says her mother texted her at about 19:00 to say they had packed in case they had to leave.
"They were waiting for instructions, and those instructions didn't come," she said.
Her parents died on the part of BÃ©dar known as English Hill, one of the places where, families say, the authorities' only contact came minutes before the flames arrived.
Gillam-Kirton says her grief has been made worse by the way the Spanish authorities have behaved since.
Local police had told the family that Pete and Fran's car had been "meticulously searched" by forensic specialists and that she would find nothing.
But in August, a month afterwards, Gillam-Kirton and her husband found what appeared to be human bones in the burnt-out car in which her father died. "I picked up what I recognised to be... part of a pelvis or a hip joint," she said.
Gillam-Kirton fears they were her father's remains.
Within minutes they also found her parents' mobile phones, jewellery, house keys and money. Police had earlier told the family that no personal belongings had survived.
Spain's Guardia Civil police have been approached for comment.
The fragments were eventually sent to Madrid for testing. The family says police have now told them no DNA was recovered and that the bones were those of an animal.
But Gillam-Kirton says experts, including a forensic anthropologist who worked on the Grenfell Tower fire investigation, believe the fragments were human.
"We still don't know who or what they are," she said.
Gillam-Kirton complained to the Guardia Civil. She says the reply told her only that the matter had been passed to the courts, and offered condolences for "the death of your relative", in the singular, when in fact she had lost both her parents.
"It feels like they're going to hope that we exhaust ourselves," she said. "Either the burden of grief is going to be enough for us to give up, or that we're going to financially exhaust ourselves."
Gillam-Kirton says there is "a real lack of empathy when you're dealing with families that have lost so much".
The one thing she wants above all is for someone to acknowledge what went wrong: "No-one seems to be willing to say 'do you know what, we made a mistake and for that we are sorry'."
The death toll in BÃ©dar was the highest from any single fire in Spain during the summer of 2026, and one of the largest in the country's history.
Many victims were trapped as they tried to escape the village, as survivor Malcolm Timbrell told me outside his devastated home in July.
The cause of the disaster, also known as the Los Gallardos fire after the neighbouring town where it started, is the subject of a criminal investigation.
In July, the Guardia Civil said it was believed to have been started by a fallen power line, but that explanation has been called into question.
The BBC has seen a letter in which the Andalusian government refused a request from Socialist MPs for its official reports on the fire. These included any documents explaining why no alert was sent to mobile phones.
The letter, signed by Sanz in September, said the documents could not be released because the criminal investigation is under secreto de sumario, a court order keeping the case file confidential.
Gillam-Kirton says the criminal case is focused on how the fire started, and that it will not necessarily examine the emergency response unless families pay lawyers to put that evidence before the judge.
"How can you have faith in these measures that they're putting in to mitigate future disasters if you don't know fully what happened the first time around?" she said.
"That's why you have a public inquiry," said Duncan Chard, "because the public need answers."
