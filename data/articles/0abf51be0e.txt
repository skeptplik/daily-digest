While puffer jackets have dominated winter wardrobes for years, this winter an alternative is emerging: the fuzz-trimmed opera coat.
Originally worn over formal evening gowns, new interpretations come in floor-grazing and cropped styles and are being worn with everything from minidresses to jeans.
Jennifer Lawrence wore a Dior opera coat, complete with a furry shawl collar, to the premiere of The Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping this week, while Kendall Jenner has been photographed in a maxi yellow version, which she wore with slouchy trousers. Having fuzzed their way down various catwalks, Staud, DÔEN, Zara, Next, Free People and Marks & Spencer are all stocking the new “it” coats.
Rixo’s floral embroidered and faux-fur trimmed version, worn by both Lawrence and Kate Moss, has sold out several times.
“I think the appeal of these coats is that they feel both luxurious and wonderfully nostalgic,” says Orlagh McCloskey, the cofounder and creative director of the London-based brand.
April Ru Wan, whose debut collection for herlabel Ouch Studios featured an opera jacket, said: “They’re playful and they keep you warm.”
J’Nae Phillips, a trend forecaster and the creator of the Fashion Tingz newsletter, said part of the appeal of these jackets was “a renewed appetite for clothes with a sense of character, craftsmanship and history”. She added that something “a little theatrical” was valuable in “an increasingly homogenised fashion landscape”.
Emily Chan, the contributing sustainability editor at British Vogue, said opera coats felt perfect for now because “there has been a real move towards maximalism – people are looking for more statement pieces, in contrast to the quiet luxury trend that dominated previously”.
A robe coat from the New York-based designer Conner Ives catalysed this textural moment. His silk vintage robes have been worn by celebrities, including Moss, Zoë Kravitz and Sienna Miller. His most recent show opened with a gold, cherry blossom-embroidered version worn over jeans and a T-shirt.
Opera coats are the kind of garment that car boots, vintage shops, Portobello Road and Camden market have in spades. On the designer resale site TheRealReal searches for fur-trimmed jackets and coats are reportedly up 33% year on year.
And with the Ives versions going for more than £6,000, many people have taken to DIY jobs. On TikTok, fashion fans have added fuzzy trims to vintage kimonos and Tang jackets. Alongside some other iterations of the furry coats, these have skirted issues of cultural appropriation.
For Chan, who is British-born Chinese and has written about her discomfort surrounding the Tang jacket becoming a trend, there “needs to be an appreciation of the history of these garments – there is a fine line between cultural appreciation and appropriation”.
Ru Wan said: “It’s complicated. Nothing brings me more joy than seeing people appreciate my culture after it was looked down on for pretty much my entire childhood.”
Growing up in Canada, she said she could remember “the distaste in people’s eyes when they realised I was Chinese. It took a long time for people to see the beauty of traditional Chinese garments.”
But, she added: “It’s never too late.”
There have also been sustainability considerations. Chan, who runs a sustainability Substack called That’s Sus, said it was great that people were using vintage and pre-existing pieces to create their own versions of the coat, rather than buying knockoffs that were likely to be discarded at the end of this trend cycle.
She said: “The majority of faux fur options on the market are made from fossil-fuel derived synthetics, which don’t biodegrade and release microplastics into the environment.
“It’s important to only buy into the trend if it’s something you’re going to wear for years to come.”
