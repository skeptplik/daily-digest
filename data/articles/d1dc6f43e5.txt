What would you get if you combined the chaotic top-down action of old-school Grand Theft Auto 2 with the chaotic top-down action of Vampire Survivors? That's the question Soldat and King Arthur's Gold developer Transhuman asked when it started working on 2024's roguelite shooter Maniac. The result is a delight, yet Maniac remains frustratingly underappreciated.
Your goal is simple: survive for as long as you can or until the 20-minute clock hits zero. Throughout that time, your secondary objective is to create as much mayhem as possible - painting the road with the blood of your foes, slamming cars into buildings, performing drive-bys as you rampage across the procedurally-generated city. The more carnage you cause, the higher your Wanted level, until you're fending off police and the military.
Maniac's debt to GTA is clear, and its chaotic action is brilliantly realised as it channels the compelling rhythms of a Survivors-style roguelite, where randomness in items and level layout makes each run fresh as you strive to unlock new characters, progressing a little further each time. It's a relatively slight experience, as its £4 price tag reflects, featuring a single compact map, just six characters, and barely any permanent progression. But Maniac makes a spirited argument for a return to a simpler time when GTA was all about shooting cops, stealing cars, and indiscriminately murdering pedestrians rather than the 'everything' game it is today.
Maniac is also a game that feels like it's trying to make good on an idea many of us had when we first played GTA 1 & 2 all those years ago: what would a better-looking, more advanced version of that look like? And for developer Michał Marcinkowski, Maniac's genesis also harkens back to an earlier time. "When I was 12, I used to come back from school and play the demo of GTA 1," he tells me over email. "It was top-down and time-limited, and all I'd do was try to cause as much chaos as possible within the 20 minutes of the demo."
And there's perhaps a hint of a 12-year-old's exuberance to Maniac. There's the bright colour palette and gleeful chaos of its physics-driven mechanics, the adrenaline rush its aggressive enemy AI creates, and its absurd roster of playable characters, which range from Little Girl to Hotdog Guy. "Each [character] is a bit of me and a bit of you," Marcinkowski explains. "We all have a drunk Santa in us. We all have a pathetic hot-dog-suit guy inside of us. Each character failed at something and is lashing out."
As each new Grand Theft Auto game pushes further into realism, the idea of recreating the rampages seen in classic GTA feels increasingly unpalatable, but Maniac's colourful cartoon aesthetic means it can get away with more. One Achievement, with a flourish of old-school bad-taste humour, challenges players to destroy five school buses using an airstrike ability called Santa's Little Helpers. And that level of irreverent, chaotic lunacy escalates the deeper into each session you get, particularly as your Wanted level climbs: if you antagonise law enforcement enough here, they eventually break out a game-ending nuke.
"This comes back to being 12," Marcinkowski says. "Nukes are just cool. I wanted the game to end with a bang, and what's more maniacal and exaggerated than a nuke? So that idea was with me for more than 20 years."
Core to Maniac's design is the ability to change from a Survivors-like auto-shooter to a standard shooter where you're able to control your aim. The latter option is, I think, the more fitting one, particularly given how much driving you do. If you squint, it almost feels like a GTA 2 clone. And while the Vampire Survivors inspirations are plentiful - including its now-standard three-option upgrade system - the GTA elements are clearly the more prevalent.
It might not play exactly like a GTA game, but it certainly captures the series' early days spirit. And Marcinkowski says he's surprised more developers haven't tried to riff on GTA: "You'd expect the most popular game in the world to have hundreds of clones, but that's not the case." There may be a reason for that.
As big as modern-day GTA is, clones are apparently much less popular. "When you look at the sales data, particularly of top-down GTA clones, they don't sell well", explains Marcinkowski. "I found that out way too late, and I thought Maniac wouldn't be a hit." His publisher was initially equally buoyant too. "But then they studied the market and stopped being so hyped. That's how it felt as we got close to release. The wishlist count wasn't very high."
"I was expecting 10,000 [copies sold]," Marcinkowski tells me. "I actually started making another game two months before Maniac came out because I thought I'd go broke." But then the game launched, and early worries proved unfounded. It sold more than 100K copies, comfortably blowing past its maker's modest expectations, putting it in the top one percent of games released on Steam.
It's a surprise success story built on the foundations of Marcinkowski's youth, and as we chat about GTA, he reveals he's less keen on Rockstar's modern games, which lack that classic gameplay drive. "I always liked the counter-culture side of GTA, which is why I put so much of it into Maniac," he elaborates. "GTA 6 seems to have become pop culture, so it's lost its appeal for me." Marcinkowski's heart, it seems, is old-school.
