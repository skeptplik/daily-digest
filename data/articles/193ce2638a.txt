As the news spread from one mathematician to the next, panic set in. On 6 October, OpenAI, the maker of ChatGPT, released more than 700 preprints written by AI, claiming to have solved more than 300 major open problems in mathematics. Some researchers stayed up all night combing through the papers that the company had posted, anxious to know whether their entire research programmes had been rendered obsolete overnight.
Depending on who you ask, this was either the most progress or the most catastrophic development ever to happen in one day in the history of the field — or both. Either way, mathematicians realized that their profession would never be the same. “It’s probably the end of mathematics as we know it,” theoretical physicist Miranda Cheng said at an emergency meeting on mathematics and AI at the Royal Society in London in late October. Many of the papers cracked problems by combining ideas from disparate parts of the discipline, leveraging a universality of knowledge that no human could ever match — and leaving even some of the world’s smartest people feeling completely outdone.
Since then, one of the field’s greatest outstanding challenges, the Riemann hypothesis, also appears to have been solved with AI assistance. And mathematicians across the world have joined frantic collective efforts to confront challenges raised by AI’s advances, from how to evaluate and publish research papers to how to raise future generations of mathematicians. Some see the risk as existential. If mathematical problem-solving becomes mainly a machine endeavour — seen as a utility like water or electricity — then their profession could lose its status in society and human expertise might start to dwindle.
“It’s a challenge like we’ve never faced in mathematics — or in academia as a whole,” says Simon Machado, a mathematician at the Swiss Federal Institute of Technology (ETH) in Zurich.
Math-pocalypse now
The bombshell paper drop in October came on the heels of earlier shocks to the field. OpenAI, which is based in San Francisco, California, released a batch of ten important results in August and, in early September, followed up with a historic solution to the Navier–Stokes problem about how fluids flow. The firm had been making noises to indicate that much more was to come. Still, many researchers were surprised by the enormity of the 6 October release. “To just see it as a giant list, without the care and thought and the love that goes into doing this work on a normal basis, it was really jarring,” says Catherine Cossaboom, a master’s student in mathematics at the University of Oxford, UK.
Perhaps no source has documented the moment better than Proofs and Prompts, an online forum that publishes essays on the impact of AI on mathematics. An 8 October post titled “100+ reactions to 100+ solutions” collected immediate reactions from dozens of students and career researchers to OpenAI’s 6 October drop. Some wrote poems, others quoted the writers Leo Tolstoy and Stanisław Lem, still others posted fragments from the Gospels (in the original Greek). One contribution reinterpreted the lyrics of ‘Jolene’ by the late country singer Dolly Parton:
And I can easily understand / How you could easily take my math / But you don’t know what it means to me, AI
The tone ranged from the enthusiastic to the angry and the despondent. “Some of them made me really feel heartbroken,” says Albane Théry, an applied mathematician at the University of Warwick in Coventry, UK, who is one of the volunteer moderators of the forum.
Few subfields of mathematics were spared and some were thoroughly ravaged. “Not a single one of the major open problems I have publicly mentioned throughout my career was left untouched,” Fields Medal winner Hugo Duminil-Copin wrote to Proofs and Prompts. “I expected that one day we would be surpassed, and that it would happen systematically. But yesterday’s announcement hit with a force I had not anticipated.”
Duminil-Copin, who is at the University of Geneva in Switzerland, specializes in the theory of transitions in complex systems — such as those that determine how information or infection filters through social networks or how metals can acquire magnetization. (OpenAI’s rival Anthropic, also in San Francisco, had already claimed to have solved a major result in this field in late August.)
Another field that bore a massive impact was the theory of groups — some of the most ubiquitous structures across mathematics, physics and other disciplines. For example, the possible ways of shuffling a deck of cards — and what happens when you revert a shuffle or combine several together — constitute a group, as do the infinite possible ways of rotating a solid object in 3D.
OpenAI’s August batch already contained the solution to a key problem on groups. Soon after its release, during a YouTube interview, Francesco Fournier-Facio, a group theorist at Heriot-Watt University in Edinburgh, UK, mentioned two other problems that mathematicians could now attack with the help of AI. Within days, he was emailed by a PhD student in computational biology who had used OpenAI’s large language model (LLM) GPT 5.6 Sol to solve one of those problems.
Fournier-Facio says that OpenAI’s October dump was even more impactful for him personally. It included the solution to a problem on which he had been actively working, called the Boone–Higman conjecture. Overall, OpenAI released solutions to perhaps eight of the top ten problems that Fournier-Facio had dreamed of solving during his career, he told Nature.
But the most stunning AI result relates to what many researchers see as the most important open problem in all of mathematics. The Riemann hypothesis, a question that was posed in the mid-nineteenth century by the German mathematician Bernhard Riemann, centres on whether prime numbers are scattered across the set of all whole numbers in a way that is essentially as unpredictable as it could be.
AI assistance seems to have cracked it. First, OpenAI proved a partial result, called the quasi-Riemann hypothesis, in its October dump. Then, on 20 November, Anthropic mathematician Levent Alpöge announced that he had found a proof of the full Riemann hypothesis, working with Claude, the firm’s AI assistant. However, that was quickly followed by a non-AI assisted proof by Rome-based mathematician Alessandro Masullo, which he had been working on for years. “If this fully checks out, it is clearly a historic milestone in mathematics,” says number theorist James Maynard, a Fields Medal winner at the University of Oxford.
An AI golden age?
Maynard and many other researchers are excited by AI technology. It is “quite likely to unleash a golden age of mathematical discovery”, he said at the meeting. Another mathematician, Antoine Tilloy at the Mines Paris engineering school, part of PSL University, uses the metaphor that doing mathematics with AI is like riding a dragon: the researcher is still in charge, but the dragon gives them supernatural powers.
But the speed at which AI proofs are emerging has already produced an immediate negative reaction. For one, mathematicians, who have long prided themselves on their field’s culture of openness, are now wary of discussing their work in public. “People are even scared to put out an abstract for a talk they are about to give on ongoing work,” says Jakub Dobrowolski, a PhD candidate at Queen Mary University of London. “Nobody wants to share new ideas because of the speed at which somebody else can take them and write a paper,” says Peter Sarnak, a number theorist at Princeton University in New Jersey.
And although established researchers say that they are both thrilled and anxious about reinventing their discipline with AI, younger generations are much more fearful. As Maynard and other established mathematicians acknowledge, the technology already poses severe problems in relation to how to assess merit when peer-reviewing mathematics work that might have been done mostly with AI, and how to train and educate the next generation of mathematicians.
“How do we train people if a good PhD thesis can be prompted out of an LLM? The response from people who are in permanent positions tends to be ‘We’re going to see and discuss’,” says Théry. “But the timescale for that could be too long for postdoc or PhD candidates who will be out of a job in nine months.”
Many early-career researchers told Nature that this uncertainty is their main source of anxiety right now. “While it is clear that faculty are working hard to find long-term solutions to the problems we are all facing, there doesn’t seem to be as much guidance on what to do in the meantime,” says Jad Hamdan, a postdoc mathematician at the University of Oxford. AI poses “more danger the younger you are, and the less experienced you are”, adds Dobrowolski. The toughest problem could be working out how to write a proposal for a research programme that might span two or three years, or more, Dobrowolski says. “Is it realistic? Now I feel like whatever I propose might just expire within a year.”
A UK survey of graduate students and academic mathematicians last month by the Martingale Foundation in London found that PhD students in particular are much less optimistic about AI’s impact than are established researchers. Asked if AI would “weaken their development as mathematicians”, 89% of students agreed, compared with only 68% of academics.
