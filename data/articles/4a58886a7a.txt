A failure in a system that allows communications between the Federal Aviation Administration, air traffic controllers, and pilots was responsible for the country’s most disastrous nationwide ground stop in air traffic since September 11. The ground stop lasted five hours.
The system, the FAA Telecommunications Infrastructure or FTI network, is the private communications backbone for the air traffic control system. The FTI is in the midst of a major, multi-year modernization project, which involves replacing a decades-old network of copper wire with high-speed fiber-optic cables. The FAA has said the update will reduce outages and outage-related delays, and make the system safer. It will also be equipped to more easily handle newer aircraft, including drones, electric vertical take-off and landing aircraft (eVTOLs), and other “air taxis.”
The system is managed by the government contractor L3Harris Technologies. On its website, L3Harris says that its “team’s deep knowledge of the US national airspace allows us to upgrade systems without disrupting air traffic operations.” A spokesperson for the company did not respond to a request for comment.
At the time the ground stop was lifted on Friday evening, over 6,300 flights within, into, or out of the US had been canceled, according to the aviation tech company FlightAware; 9,500 had been delayed. Freight and mail flights have also been affected.
Delays will continue, US Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said in a briefing at the White House. “It will take us some time to unwind what has happened.”
In May, L3Harris said it had completed 50 percent of its modernization project. Seventy percent of FAA sites have been upgraded to fiber, the company said earlier this month. The Trump Administration has said it aims to overhaul the nation's Air Traffic Control System by the end of 2028.
In August, the FAA tapped AT&T to upgrade the FTI, the private FAA communication network, to an entirely new system. That contract is worth $73 million, though its worth is likely to climb into the billions over the years. Previously, Verizon had been selected to build and operate this new system. L2Harris is meant to operate the legacy system until the upgrades are in place.

FAA workers have not always been pleased with L3Harris’ work. The contractor makes working with its system “a more frustrating process than it needs to be,” an FAA employee told WIRED last year.
A September report published by the US Government Accountability Office (GAO) raised concerns that the FAA’s attempt to accelerate long-delayed modernization plans “could be prioritizing speed over costs and air traffic disruption.” It found that the FAA does not have a “well-documented” lifecycle cost estimate for its programs, and that, rather than a master schedule guiding the sprawling project, has 11,389 schedules that are not integrated.
