More than 5,200 deaths in England in 2026 were linked to summer heat, the highest provisional annual total since records began a decade ago, according to the UK Health Security Agency.
The government agency said 5,258 deaths were associated with extreme heat between May and September 2026.
The figure is substantially higher than the previous record of 2,985, recorded in 2022, and follows totals of 2,295 in 2023, 1,311 in 2024 and 1,504 last year.
This year’s summer was the hottest on record, bringing record-breaking temperatures and prolonged dry weather. Temperatures reached 38C (100.4F) in Norfolk in June, while a peak of 38.2C was recorded in Gloucestershire in August. England also experienced its driest July on record, with just 7mm of rain.
The greatest number of deaths was during the heatwave between 21 and 28 June, when an estimated 2,401 people died. More than 3,000 heat-associated deaths were recorded across the first two episodes of the season, in May and June.
The agency said the summer had seven “heat episodes” lasting a total of 41 days, the highest number of episodes and heat-episode days since monitoring began in 2016. The June heatwave triggered only the second red heat-health alert ever issued in England.
Dr Ross Thompson, UKHSA’s principal environmental health scientist, said: “Climate change is already affecting people’s health in England, and as the climate continues to warm we can expect periods of extreme heat to become longer, more frequent and more intense.”
“That is why it is vital that individuals, communities, health and care services and local organisations are prepared, not just during a heatwave but in the longer term. The decisions we make now will shape the health impacts people experience in the decades ahead.”
UKHSA said heat early in the summer could be particularly dangerous because people and public services may not yet be prepared. It said heat-health measures should be in place from the start of summer, rather than focused only on the hottest weeks.
The Greenpeace climate campaigner Maja Darlington said: “Thousands of deaths from a heatwave is not a freak accident – it’s a policy choice. We wouldn’t tolerate deaths on this scale if they were caused by a terrorist attack – there would be inquiries and people held accountable. As long as politicians keep allowing fossil fuel companies to get off scot-free and failing to heat-proof our schools, hospitals and care homes, tragedies like this will keep happening.
“The government has some big decisions to make. Giving a green light to drilling at Rosebank, watering down EV targets and backing Heathrow expansion would only lock in hotter, deadlier summers. It’s time to change course and start making the fossil fuel companies pay for the changes we so desperately need to stay safe.”
Emma Howard Boyd, chair of the National Heat Risk Commission and professor at the Grantham Research Institute on Climate Change and the Environment, said: “These figures are a stark warning. One summer in England has already seen 5,200 heat-associated deaths, against a Climate Change Committee projection of up to 10,000 annually across the UK by 2050. Extreme heat is a growing public health emergency. We know the risks and what needs to be done. The time for urgent action is now.”
UKHSA said adapting homes, neighbourhoods and health and care systems to higher temperatures would be increasingly important, alongside cutting emissions to limit future warming.
As death registration data for the period is not yet complete, the agency said the figures remained provisional.
