Rembrandt van Rijn's "The Night Watch" is one of the most famous paintings of the Dutch Golden Age. Amsterdam's Rijksmuseum, where the work is on display, hails the artist's 1642 masterpiece as "the turning point in his career and as the superlative example of his creative genius."
In the painting, Rembrandt made dramatic use of light and shadow to guide the viewer's eye to key figures. And unlike static group portraits typical of his era, the artist depicted Amsterdam's civic guards in mid-action, as they prepare to march out.
Beyond the sense of movement featured in "The Night Watch," the painting itself is also alive in a way that's rarely taken into consideration — through the microscopic organisms living on the canvas.
The research project "The Night Watch Lives" explores exactly that question: What kinds of life forms inhabit the surface of the famous painting?
The study is a collaborative scientific and exhibition project between the Rijksmuseum and ARTIS-Micropia — an Amsterdam museum dedicated to the world of microorganisms.
It's part of the broader "Operation Night Watch" restoration and research project described as the "biggest and most wide-ranging ever study and restoration of Rembrandt's most famous painting," started in 2019. Conservators and scientists in this project have been examining every aspect of Rembrandt's masterpiece using cutting-edge technology, seeking new insights into its materials, condition and history — and now, its microbiome.
Putting microbes in the spotlight
The idea behind "The Night Watch Lives" was to "put microbes in a positive spotlight," Eline van Bloois, who led the research, told DW. "Honestly, if you're asking me — I'm a microbiologist, so I'm biased of course — but they really deserve more positive attention."
Over a period of six months, van Bloois will be taking samples once a month directly from the famous painting at the Rijksmuseum, accompanied by the people working on the restoration of "The Night Watch."
Using small sterile discs gently pressed against selected areas of the painting, the samples collect microscopic material from both varnished and unvarnished sections.
These samples are then cultivated in laboratories, allowing previously invisible microbes to grow into visible colonies that can be studied and identified.
At the "The Night Watch Lives" exhibition, visitors can view Petri dishes containing the cultivated microbial colonies. A life-size reproduction of Rembrandt's masterpiece also shows where the samples were taken, and which microorganisms were found there.
A study in progress
At the opening of the exhibition on November 11, the installation included the findings of two of van Bloois' sampling sessions. Four more will be added over the upcoming months, allowing the museum's visitors to follow the results of the study as it unfolds. That means the exhibition's visitors "will be figuring out what I've found as I'm figuring it out myself," explains the microbiologist. "We really take them through that journey with us."
People often fear microbes or associate them with disease, even though the vast majority of these microscopic organisms are actually harmless or vital to human survival.
Some of the organisms found on the painting "are very commonly found on human skin, for instance, and they're a healthy part of the microbiome; most humans actually have these bacteria with them," says van Bloois, adding that the presence of these microbes on the painting made sense, since humans are often near the work.
But another bacterium stood out in the samples: Microbacterium saccharophilum, a species first identified at a sugar refinery in Japan in 2013. How this microorganism ended up on the Dutch "Night Watch," or when, is a guessing game.
"It's part of the fun and part of the frustration in microbiology. You cannot ask the microbes where they originate from," says van Bloois. "It would make our lives a bit easier, but it's also fun to have a little bit of a mystery."
The samples also revealed bacteria from soil, often found in plant roots. Could these organisms somehow harm the painting? Could plants start growing on the work?
Van Bloois explains why this is highly unlikely: On the painting, the bacteria can form spores that are inactive, but for the study, the researcher "provided them with all the nutrients and the right temperature and the right humidity that allowed them to grow again."
Still, studying these organisms may eventually contribute to understanding how paintings age, how environmental conditions affect cultural heritage, and how museums can better preserve priceless works of art.
As to whether the microorganisms could date back to Rembrandt's time, the microbiologist says that's also "highly unlikely" — but here again, microbes cannot reveal their origin story.
When art and science meet
As part of the larger "Operation Night Watch" team, van Bloois is definitely inspired by this "group effort where everyone is just really enthusiastic about telling these stories and adding to the knowledge surrounding 'The Night Watch'."
The microbiological samples revealed by the laboratory process resemble abstract art in miniature: colorful clusters, delicate textures and intricate patterns created by living organisms.
And as the study is unfolding publicly, it also feels like an artistic performance of its own.
The project also perfectly embodies the full name of the museum where the exhibition is held: Natura Artis Magistra, a Latin phrase that translates to "nature is the teacher of art and science," as the microbiologist points out: "It's really cool to see science and art all come together in this project."
The exhibition "The Night Watch Lives" runs at ARTIS-Micropia in Amsterdam until the end of May 2027.
