What you need to know
- Delegates from nearly 200 countries failed to reach an agreement despite prolonging the talks
- The summit's president acknowledges that a deal was 'not adopted'
- The final draft presented at the summit in Antalya, Turkey omitted any mention of fossil fuels
- The EU says it cannot accept a deal that 'greenwashes fossil fuels'
- Oil and gas consumption remains a major flashpoint amid soaring energy prices and tensions over the US-Israeli war with Iran
- UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres said he was 'deeply disappointed' by the outcome
These are the major developments from the closing phase of the UN's COP31 Climate Summit in Antalya, Turkey, on Friday, November 20 through Sunday, November 22:
Save the Children advisor tells DW calls lack of COP agreement 'disastrous'
The failure of governments to agree on a transition away from fossil fuels and increase climate finance at COP31 will have devastating consequences for children and future generations, says Jessica Cooke, senior climate change and education adviser at Save the Children.
Speaking to DW, Cooke said the summit marked a rollback on gender equality and human rights, with girls particularly affected by the lack of progress in climate negotiations.
"If governments can't agree how to tackle the climate crisis, they can't agree on how to leave the world in a liveable state for children and future generations," she said.
"This is, put simply, disastruous and a moral abjection from our collective responsibility to safeguard the the rights and well-being of current and future generations," she said.
Cooke said climate-related disasters were already disrupting children's education, health and family lives. She cited a recent visit to Malawi, where a storm had destroyed a classroom, forcing children to study in a temporary tent in extreme heat.
According to Save the Children, an average of 330 million children a year have experienced severe climate disasters since 2022. Separately, UNICEF estimates that one in 10 students worldwide had their education disrupted by climate-related hazards in 2025.
Cooke said girls, particularly adolescent girls, were especially vulnerable because of existing inequalities and the impact of climate disasters on their education and sexual and reproductive health and rights.
"I saw a lot of parties pushing back not only on gender but also on human rights," she said, adding that girls were not adequately represented in climate negotiations.
She urged world leaders to give children a greater role in climate decisions, both at international conferences and within their own countries and communities.
Germany says switching to clean energy would ease pressure from high fuel prices
People from all over the world are suffering due to high prices of fossil fuels, Germany's Environment Minister Carsten Schneider said after the COP31 breakdown. He added that clean technologies such as electric vehicles, heat pumps and modernizing energy industries would free the people from this dependence.
"I understand that this poses a threat to the business model of those countries that sell us oil and gas. But electrification will make the world a more peaceful and stable place," he said.
Previously, Schneider accused the oil and gas industry of dragging its feet on climate change, referencing the last-minute draft proposals which steered clear of fossil fuel phaseout.
"Some oil-producing nations realize that the situation is becoming serious for them — that is why they are blocking it so vehemently," Scheider said from Turkey.
Separately, the head of the German Environment Agency Dirk Messner said the world was divided in two camps, with some countries focusing on averting climate change and others defending oil, coal, and natural gas.
"A fossil-fuel-based economy can quickly become obsolete if the majority of countries stand together," Messner said.
He urged the nations protecting the climate to continue boosting cooperation. At the same time, Messner warned that the world would not become climate-neutral by the middle of the century without nations like Russia, Saudi Arabia and the US all supporting this goal.
"This is why the global climate dialogue must continue," he said.
EU's rejection of climate funding 'left bitter taste in many mouths,' climate campaigner tells DW
The EU's outright rejection to provide new finance commitments "set the tone" for the failed COP 31 climate summit, says Tasneem Essop, executive director at the Climate Action Network, a group over 2,500 NGOs from various parts of the world.
Talking to DW from Turkey, Essop questioned the EU's narrative of rejecting the proposed agreement for not mentioning fossil fuels.
Essop said that the EU was pushing back and obstructing discussions dealing with the issues of the Global South.
"Rich nations are responsible for providing public finance to developing countries so that they can take climate action, so that they can transition away from fossil fuels," Essop said.
"That requires financial support. And yet they came here without finance. So, it's left a bitter taste in many mouths about the action of rejection by the EU."
Essop also pointed to the UK, Australia, and European countries granting additional licenses to obtain fossil fuels.
"You can't say you're rejecting something because of fossil fuels and yet in your practice and your actions back home, you're doing exactly the opposite," she said.
She described the outcome of the summit, which went into overtime and then ended without an agreement, as "deeply disappointing."
COP31 lacked plans for developing countries, anti-fossil fuel campaigner tells DW
While oil producers like Saudi Arabia and Russia were criticized for blocking any mention fossil fuels in the draft COP31 declaration, one climate campaigner told DW that other wealthy countries also failed more vulnerable nations by not agreeing to a funded pathway for an energy transition.
Harjit Sing, strategic advisor to the Fossil Fuel Non-Proliferation Treaty Initiative said developing nations had big ambitions to turn away from oil and gas, but needed resources to do so.
"If you look at Canada, the UK, Norway, not to mention the US, they continue to expand fossil fuels, yet they point fingers towards developing countries who have a large population," Sing told DW.
"But the reality is that [the developed countries] were the ones who were blocking the conversation and the progress that we needed to create a just transition mechanism."
Sing said this lack of a funded plan for developing countries was more urgent than whether or not the final declaration mentioned fossil fuels.
"So not mentioning fossil fuels is just one part of it. The more important thing is we now need a plan, not repackaged national roadmaps, but a fair, fast and funded transition away from fossil fuels," he said.
"Fair: So rich countries move fast, [but] they are the ones who are historically responsible for the climate crisis. Fast, which is in line with science. And funded, so that developing countries get the right kind of support to make that shift."
A combination of climate change effects and insufficient funding would be "a life or death scenario" for developing countries, Sing added.
"Even at one degree temperature rise, they were facing so many disasters and we are already going to breach that 1.5 degrees Celsius of warming," he said.
Russia doubles down on fossil fuel stance, Saudi Arabia says 'all solutions' needed
After the failure of the COP31 summit over fossil fuels, Russia's delegate Sergey Kononuchenko argued that reducing the supply of oil without reducing the demand will cause "deficit, price shock and a switch to dirtier types of fuel" as well as "energy poverty," which will affect poorer countries first.
"For that reason, we cannot agree to any formulation on fossil fuel," he said.
Meanwhile, Saudi Arabia's representative referred to a last-minute proposal that called for expanding carbon capture capacities.
"We believe that climate change requires all solutions on the table," said Mohammad Ayoub, speaking for the Arab Group of nations at the summit.
"It makes no sense that solutions, concrete solutions... will be taken off the table."
While capturing carbon dioxide from the atmosphere is technically possible, critics say the technology is still in its infancy and lacks real-world infrastructure to move the needle on climate change.
Both Russia and Saudi Arabia had sought to omit any mention of fossil fuels in the final declaration, contributing the the impasse on Sunday.
UN's Guterres 'deeply disappointed' by summit
UN Secretary-General Antonio Guterres said he was "deeply disappointed" by the collapse of negotiations for a joint resolution at COP31.
"I share the very profound frustration of all those who expected governments to rise to the moment the climate crisis demands," Guterres said in a statement.
"There may be no consensus in the negotiating room. There is no ambiguity in the science," he added.
"Those who profit from the destruction of our planet must never dictate the terms of the fight to save it," the UN chief said. "The continued addiction to coal, oil and gas is fueling climate chaos, exposing economies to price shocks, and undermining energy security," Guterres added.
"Major emitters must lead. Developed countries must deliver on finance. And every country must do their part."
Marshall Islands: 'We've been let down on all sides'
Marshall Islands climate envoy Kathy Jetnil-Kijiner spoke out against fossil fuel interests after the conclusion of the summit with no deal.
The Marshall Islands are a chain of volcanic atolls and coral islands numbering over 1,100 islands in total in the Pacific Ocean. Like many island nations, they are severly threatened by the effects of climate change on the earth's oceans.
"We have been let down on all sides. We were presented with the worst possible response to overshoot, a response to enhance oil and gas. We will not be forced to accept false solutions," Jetnil-Kijiner said.
"We will not compromise our future to benefit fossil fuel interests. We will not accept an outcome that undermines our security and threatens our future."
Negotiations president 'very disappointed' over collapsed talks
Australian environment minister and COP31's president of negotiations, Chris Bowen, expressed disappointment that the sweeping deal he tried to broker ultimately collapsed.
As part of a compromise between the two countries, Turkey hosted the summit while Australia took a leading role over negotiations.
"Unfortunately, we were unable to reach agreement on a cover decision... And that's disappointing. Very disappointing," said Bowen.
"An obvious issue is the transition away from fossil fuels, where some parties insisted that must be mentioned, other parties insisted that if it was mentioned they would veto."
Nevertheless, Bowen highlighted some of the ways in which the summit has still had a positive impact.
"A lot of good things happened this COP. A lot of events which told the clean energy story. A lot of commitments made — like the more than 90 countries that signed the electrification pledge, for example. And all that is good work and a great thing," he said.
COP31 fails to agree climate deal amid fossil fuel rift
The COP31 climate summit has failed to agree on a final joint declaration.
During a plenary session, the summit's Turkish president acknowledged there would be no climate deal.
"I understand that there is no agreement on this text. For that reason, these decisions have not been adopted," Murat Kurum, who is also Turkey's environment minister, told a closing plenary in the Mediterranean city of Antalya.
The rift centered on whether to include the phaseout of fossil fuels in the final declaration. Scientists say the burning of fossil fuels such as coal, oil and gas is the main cause of global warming.
European nations, smaller island states and climate activists have criticized oil-producing countries for the omission.
Ghana says COP presidency has not 'shown respect'
Several nations continued to raise serious objections to the draft.
Antwi-Boasiako Amoah, chair of the African Group of Negotiators, took a shot at the presidency for how it has handled the negotiations.
"With all due respect Mr. President, you have not shown respect to a group of 54 countries (from Africa) and my country (Ghana) for that matter," Amoah said.
Panama rejects draft decision, saying it 'should be based on science'
Panama's representative said the Central American nation was amongst those who suffer most from the consequences and issue of climate justice.
Panamanian Environment Minister Juan Carlos Navarro said the talks were being held hostage by a "petroleum mafia" and need to be reformed.
He said science must be the basis for the cover decision, adding that those who contributed to this crisis must shoulder the responsibility to deal with it.
"We are running out of time," Navarro told attendees. "Panama cannot support this decision. The planet cannot continue to wait."
EU cannot accept deal that 'greenwashes fossil fuels': Irish minister
Ireland's Climate Minister Darragh O'Brien has said the European Union is "profoundly disappointed" with the current draft deal on the table at COP31 because it "fails to meet the moment" and that the bloc will not agree to it.
"We will not endorse a cover decision that does not respond to the science, that disregards the implementation gap, and risks deepening the chasm between global commitments and the action required to correct the overshoot and bring 1.5°C within reach," O'Brien said.
"We will not endorse a cover decision that does not even mention fossil fuels, the driver of climate change. And we will not accept negotiating backward in time. And we will not accept a COP that greenwashes fossil fuels," he added.
Former Greenpeace chief says no deal is better than current deal
Former Greenpeace executive Jennifer Morgan said it would be better to end the COP31 summit with no deal than a watered-down declaration.
Her remarks come amid deep divisions over the omission of fossil fuels from the draft declaration.
Morgan rejected the notion that it would be better to accept the current draft than have no resolution at all, as the Turkish COP president has suggested.
"I think this is the day for the first time in my 31 COPs that I thought it was better not to have a decision that sets us back, that goes against what small islands and vulnerable people are saying," Morgan added.
She praised Germany for standing up to pressures to accept the current deal.
"Of course it would have been better to have a strong outcome here with everyone moving forward, but I agree with the German minister who just said if the text stays the way that it is, then it should not be accepted," Morgan said.
The US climate activist blamed oil-producing nations for the impasse.
"Saudi Arabia's fingerprints are all over" the current draft proposal, she said.
The omission has created a deadlock at COP31, which was due to conclude on Friday but has been extended as delegates seek consensus.
Morgan also blamed this year's Turkish presidency for failing to bring everybody together and instead letting countries that rely on fossil fuels dictate the terms of the agreement.
"Last year we had Brazil with a very strong presidency. They worked all year to bring countries together and they managed. It was not easy last year," Morgan said.
"This year we had presidencies that, in my view, were not well prepared. They did not bring countries together (...) it's been shuttle diplomacy with no text for four days. That's not the way you get an agreement here," she added.
Germany rejects 'unacceptable' draft deal
German Environment Minister Carsten Schneider has said the latest version of the draft agreement is "not acceptable."
Schneider said that the draft decision "not only fails to provide an adequate response but actually takes a step backward. A step backward from previous decisions on climate protection is the last thing Germany can support."
Schneider said that delegates had been working hard for days to reach a unanimous decision on "the world's greatest challenge: human-induced climate change," noting that this summer had "shown that the nature of this warming not only costs lives — as in Nepal — but also changes our world for the worse."
"It is up to us, as humanity, to do something about it. That is why a conference dedicated to climate protection — whose sole purpose is to protect the climate — must send a clear signal that we are taking action against this threat."
Schneider said he still believed most nations are willing to work to enact climate protection, but singled out those who still rely on fossil fuels to power their economies.
"The overwhelming majority of nations are committed to climate protection. Some, however, are still making huge profits from oil and gas and are unwilling to scale back their production or to pioneer a clear roadmap for reducing greenhouse gas emissions in this area," he added.
France criticizes draft deal as 'backward' step
France has called a new draft deal proposed at COP31 a "backward" step.
French Ecological Transition Minister Monique Barbut criticized the deal for failing to mention fossil fuels, casting fresh uncertainty over whether nations will be able to reach an agreement.
"Time is ticking and we are not there yet," French Ecological Transition Minister Monique Barbut said in a statement.
