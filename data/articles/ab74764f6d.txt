Poles, shocked by a recent mass shooting in the heart of the historic city of Krakow, might have been comforted that their fractious political class was at least willing to mourn together. In the aftermath, the rightwing President Karol Nawrocki and the liberal pro-European Prime Minister Donald Tusk laid down candles together in the town’s main market square.
Unfortunately, everything in Poland ends up as fodder for the partisan political mill. Tusk’s interior minister said the shooter — who died in hospital — was “fascinated” by far-right ideologies. The leader of the Law and Justice party, which backs Nawrocki, called that a “blatant lie.”
Prosecutors were more cautious, saying that they haven’t yet found conclusive evidence. And yet the unseemly wrangling over the shooter’s motives suggests too few people are listening. Poland has long been in a political crisis — one caused not simply by disagreement over policy, but also by the absence of any lawyer, official or broadcaster that both halves of the country will believe.
