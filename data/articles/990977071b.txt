There's no denying that Harry Potter continues to be a phenomenon. After seven bestsellers translated into multiple languages, years of extravagant midnight release parties, eight big-budget films, a stage play, and theme parks, there's still enormous hype for both the upcoming HBO television show and an all-star re-recording of the audiobooks. Clearly, there is something about the boy who lived and his world that draws people in and keeps them captivated. All of this begs one very important question, however: how good are the Harry Potter books, really?
The answer to that question is, ultimately, a matter of personal taste, but the books do have their faults, from skimming mythology and using, at best, insensitive naming conventions to treating serious issues as fodder for gags and jokes. There's also the perennial question of whether the artist can be separated from their art. The good news is that there's a lot of fantasy in the world waiting to be read. Here are six fantasy books better than Harry Potter:
6 Magnus Chase and the Gods of Asgard: The Sword of Summer
Set in the same world as Percy Jackson and the Kane Chronicles (Magnus is Annabeth Chase's cousin), this book follows Magnus, an unhoused teen on the streets of Boston who discovers he's the son of Frey. After dying in battle, he becomes an einherjar and a resident of Hotel Valhalla. A Valkyrie named Samirah Al-Abbas convinces Magnus to help her stop Ragnarök.
While a fantastic adventure full of Riordan's signature charm and wit, Magnus Chase also touches on the ways so many people, including children and teens, are othered because of their gender identity, disability, housing instability, poverty, or religion. This book, however, never once uses any of these topics as the subject of a joke or a reason for the characters to be cruel to one another. That's not to say everyone who populates the world is perfect; they have quirks and foibles and they make mistakes. There are, of course, true villains. But the lack of casual cruelty allows Riordan to bring together a found family built on choice, mutual respect, and determination—a believable family that allows each character to explore openly and honestly what's been difficult and traumatic for them. Riordan uses impeccable research with care to create a story that pays tribute to it.
The Sword of Summer is the first book in a trilogy.
5 Six of Crows
Six of Crows is set in the same world as Bardugo's Shadow and Bone series. It follows a group of thieves hired to break into a heavily guarded prison to rescue a scientist experimenting with a drug that amplifies Grisha magic to potentially deadly levels. The crew is, of course, a motley bunch, all of whom have dark pasts they promise won't cause complications. In the tradition of every great heist story, those pasts do, indeed, make everything a thousand times more difficult.
These are also the stories that Bardugo does best: the smaller, more intimate ones. The stories with twists and turns and complications that leave characters trapped in the prisons of their own minds. The ones that mislead and distract until, suddenly, the scales fall away and the true double-cross is staring right at the intrepid almost-heroes, ready to crush them. No Harry Potter book ever made readers hold their breath the way the conclusion of Six of Crows will. It's probably a good idea to have Crooked Kingdom, the conclusion of the duology, standing by.
4 The Raven Boys
Blue, a girl from a family of psychics, has grown up being constantly reminded that she'll kill her true love with a kiss. That doesn't stop her, however, from befriending a group of boys from the local private school and joining them on their quest to seek out a legend: the Welsh king sleeping in the hills that surround them. Gansey, the group's leader, tells her that the first person to wake the king will get a wish. Blue accompanies the boys as they search, despite the danger and all the strange things that seem to happen when they go looking.
The Raven Boys is a marvel of atmosphere and mood. It gathers the reader in and holds them there, keeps them enmeshed in the world Stiefvater has created. Rather than bouncing around from setting to setting, it unfurls like the landscape it describes, presenting a magnificent vista that spreads wide before the reader and makes magic of what's right in front of them, accessible through their own senses. It also makes magic a horror-tinged phenomenon, something to be wary of, something beautiful but dangerous. Something with a price.. Like Magnus Chase, The Raven Boys blends fantasy and reality by also exploring income inequality and difficult home situations honestly but without ever shaming or humiliating the characters trapped in those experiences.
There are four books in The Raven Cycle, which is currently being adapted into a series of graphic novels.
3 Impossible Creatures
Christopher is not supposed to go near the water. Like many fantasy heroes, he disobeys and finds a drowning baby griffin. He saves it and is tasked with taking it back to the Archipelago, a hidden place where magical creatures are supposed to be safe. Once there, he meets Mal, a girl with a miraculous flying coat, and learns that magic is slowly dying, as are the inhabitants of the Archipelago. Mal convinces Christopher to go with her on a quest to discover what's happening and to save their impossible friends.
A middle-grade book, it will be a quick read for adults and a great book to read with kids of a certain age. This is also a great book for kids because, while the adults keep secrets from Christopher, his grandfather breaks the cycle and becomes a trusted adult—something that doesn't happen in many fantasy novels. Katherine Rundell is fantastic at setting scenes, and each one comes together beautifully, painting the Archipelago in bright swaths of color and incredible detail. There is a cliffhanger at the end of book one that may be a little upsetting; the good news is that books two of this planned five-book series is already out.
2 Legendborn
Bree Matthews hopes going to UNC early for a residential program will help her escape the grief of her mother's death. Her first night on campus, however, she stumbles into a demon attack and discovers a secret society whose members are descended from King Arthur's knights. When Sel, their mage, or Merlin, tries to wipe Bree's memory, he fails but unlocks abilities that suggest Bree too is a Legendborn.Initially turning down an invitation to join the group, Bree changes her mind when she discovers that a Merlin had something to do with her mother's death. What she ultimately finds out is unimaginable.
Fantasy is rife with Arthurian retellings, but Legendborn is something special. The modern college setting gives the story room to breathe and allows Deonn to make some fascinating innovations with the material. The story's exploration of different magic systems keeps Bree grounded in the world even as she's discovering her powers, which is essential for her growth both as a person and within the Arthurian framework. Each of the characters in Legendborn has a role that's been assigned to them, but they also have their own desires, and the conflicts and real-life stakes this interplay creates make the story that much more interesting. The exploration of grief is also important; it holds the narrative together and carries it forward, much as real grief shapes a life, especially after the loss of a parent. It influences everything that Bree does and every choice that she makes. It takes skill to pair fiction and reality so deftly.
The second and third books of the series, Bloodmarked and Oathbound, are out. An as-yet-untitled fourth volume is planned.
1 Darker by Four
Rui is an exorcist determined to avenge her mother's death. But one day, in the middle of a battle, her powers disappear. They reappear in Yiran, a boy from a wealthy family who would have had a perfect life if he had been born with abilities; now, suddenly, he does and as far as he's concerned, his new magic will fix everything. Nikai is a reaper whose boss, the Fourth King of Hell, has disappeared. Without the ten kings tending to their respective realms, the underworld starts to crumble, and the human realm won't last much longer. So when Rui thinks she's discovered a lead on the missing fourth king and a way to get her powers back, she makes a dangerous deal.
Darker by Four builds on mythology instead of sprinkling it in where it's convenient or where it sounds good. The story reflects a deep understanding of the source material, which is truly integrated into the narrative; the legends here are load-bearing. That doesn't mean they're cited word for word, but it does mean there's a fundamental respect. The story itself is also a lot of fun while dealing with some serious topics: grief, othering, and identity. There's also the matter of the magic itself: characters who can use magic quickly discover that it doesn't save them from their responsibilities and that, sometimes, it's a greater burden than a gift. When things are broken, they can't simply buy a new wand.Brighter than Nine completes the duology.
