The story so far: The battle for the most-sought-after commodity that underpins development of artificial intelligence technology just grew fiercer. On November 16, the U.S. Commerce Department issued an interim final rule (IFR) requiring companies to have licences to supply advanced American AI chips to data centre operators outside the U.S. with substantial computing power to businesses headquartered in two dozen countries, including China, Iran, and Russia. The new rule restricts American companies from freely shipping high-end AI chips to a set of five countries, including Indonesia, Malaysia, and Singapore, without the explicit approval of the U.S. government.
China has censured the move, calling it “yet another case of the U.S. overstretching the concept of national security, and abusing export control measures to interfere in normal trade and tech cooperation between China and other countries and go after China’s high-tech industry”. The interim rule came just days ahead of the meeting between U.S. President Donald Trump and China’s President Xi Jinping in Shenzhen, China on the sidelines of the November 18-19 APEC Summit.
The U.S. government’s policy on making advanced technology inaccessible to China has been a talking point among lawmakers in Washington for well over a decade, but Congress strengthened the extant legal framework only during the first Trump term when it passed the Export Control Reform Act in 2018.
It strengthened the U.S. government’s authority to control the movement of advanced technology originating in the country, and included curbing exports of sensitive technologies, including top-tier chips, with dual-use — both commercial and military — applications to countries such as China, Russia, and Iran.
A year later, the Bureau of Industry and Security (BIS) placed Chinese telecom equipment maker Huawei and its affiliates on the Entity List, effectively blocking their access to high-end semiconductors.
