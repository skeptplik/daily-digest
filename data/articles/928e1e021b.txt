“Girl, that one’s always a good one,” Malin Åkerman says. I’ve just told her how many times I’ve rewatched her 2008 rom-com 27 Dresses, in which she played the charming, wedding dress-and-crush-stealing Tess, younger sister to Katherine Heigl’s responsible Jane. Tess is, in my view, one of the great 2000s-era villains—though Åkerman doesn’t see her as such.
“She was just a young, stupid girl who fell in love, and it was really wrong. That’s your sister’s crush. Don’t do that, girl. Not cool.” She laughs. “But there was a life lesson in it, and she learned and she grew from it. It’s less villain, more just human.”
Some of that sympathy also exists in the way Åkerman plays the audacious, gun-toting, bisexual Texas queen Margo Banks in Netflix’s The Hunting Wives, based on May Cobb’s novel, which returns for season two this week. Margo has done some bad things, but she’s certainly looked good doing ’em—and former Democratic campaign strategist Sophie (Brittany Snow) can’t help but be drawn into her orbit. Their Bonnie-and-Clyde love story continues in the new season, and the stakes have never been higher.
Here, Åkerman talks to Vogue about the Margos in her own life, performing with Snow at the Las Culturistas Culture Awards, and why it’s the perfect time to watch a show about lesbians in Texas.
Vogue: It’s a great time to be releasing a show about Texas.
Malin Åkerman: It’s unbelievable. There was a great little picture, Dermot [Mulroney] sent it to us gals, and it was like, “The Hunting Wives Turns Texas Votes,” or something like that. All the lesbians came out [to vote]. I’m going to misquote it, but it’s out there.
Do you think there’s a future where Jed loses the governor race to–
…I was going to say, to Margo Banks? Maybe she takes over, the Lady Macbeth in the back just pulling all the strings.
Absolutely.
That could be really fun if he has someone that he’s up against, a powerful female that maybe Margo has an affair with. Let’s see.
Season two shows us a different side of Margo—she’s in a more vulnerable state. I’m curious what you thought of her season two arc. Where do you think her mindset is at the beginning of the show?
It’s really nice to see the power dynamic shift. Sophie came into her power and Margo’s softening and actually letting someone in, which is huge for a person like Margo, who has been a survivor and keeps everyone at an arm’s length. You see these cracks in her veneer that she’s created for her whole life, really. We start off in a pretty bad place for Margo in season two. She’s in the doghouse, so to speak. She does not like being there.
I loved playing with her vulnerability and really just softening, enveloped in Sophie’s love and protection. That was really nice because I just don’t think that Margo has ever really had that other than [from] her brother, in a weird way, who was the only person that she trusted. I feel like we finally get to see her trusting someone else. Because she doesn’t really trust Jed, that’s just a means to an end. She knows how to play him, she knows how to do her thing to get what she needs.
The Margo–Jed relationship is interesting this season. He acts like a man with power but he is also very vulnerable to her charms. What kind of discussions were you and Dermot having?
All men in power are vulnerable to women if they know how to play their cards right. You know what I mean? There are those women out there who know exactly how to get the guys, they know exactly the buttons to push. Dermot and I, we have a lot of fun conversations. We never like it when we have to play angry at each other because we really love each other. All of us, by the way, on this show, we hate when we have to hate each other. But Dermot and I definitely have much more fun when we get to be in love, so to speak. I don’t think Margo actually loves Jed, but it was fun to have those moments.
I just love playing Margo and all her agendas behind everything and trying to play it as real as possible, especially with Jed, because she’s really a player. She’s got Sophie on the side [while she’s] telling Jed that he’s everything. Yeah, Dermot and I have a lot of fun. Mostly we just goof around on set, to be really honest with you.
Well, you guys do have a rom-com that’s coming out in a few weeks.
Listen, Dermot asked me when we were on set—it was the last couple weeks of shooting [season one], it was his last day of shooting. He’s like, “I’m about to go do this rom-com on a cruise.” And I was like, “Nope, not doing it, never. I don’t ever want to go on a cruise, it’s not my thing. Too claustrophobic. Don’t want to be on for three weeks. Are you nuts?” And he goes, “Well, the first week it would just be us and you can bring your family and it’ll become this free vacation.” I was like, “Oh, okay. When do we start?” And he goes, “Four days after you wrap this show.” It’s like, Oh my God. But it was my son’s summer break, so I ended up convincing a girlfriend of mine, one of my best girlfriends who’s also a makeup artist, to come along with her kids on the trip. I don’t know how it’s going to turn out, but we had a blast making it.
We love a free vacation.
We had a little too much fun. We were like, “Is there even a movie in this? I don’t know, but we’re having a great time.”
I’m a big Dale Dickey fan, and she plays a fun role in this season. What was it like filming with her?
She’s phenomenal on every level. She’s a phenomenal human being—the nicest, loveliest human you’ll ever meet and a phenomenal actress. I remember seeing her in Winter’s Bone and just going, “Wow, this woman is just great.” And what a face—the realism in the expressions. And she’s just beautiful to watch when she works and she really is invested. She believes in her craft and she’s so collaborative and lovely. All the greatest things you can say about a person, I would say about Dale Dickey.
Thinking about Margo and Sophie this season, they’re both killers, so they can be on a more even playing field. But Sophie does have this secret at the beginning of the season…
The audience question is, do they really love each other? And listen, that’s an open-ended question. There’s no real answer to that. I think there is love there, for sure. I think on Sophie’s part towards Margo, it’s almost an extension of her addictive personality. Margo’s an addiction. Margo fluctuates between necessity and love because Margo’s just not honest with herself. She’s not open, honest, and vulnerable, so you can’t be that with someone else.
I felt like there were parts of this season where Sophie was scarier than Margo.
Totally. You let the beast out and you’re like, Holy shit, she needs something. She’s going to get it. That’s also what attracts these two to each other, is that drive. You get to see why they’re kind of meant for each other in this season. It’s not just Margo. Margo’s not just the crazy one and the wild one. Sophie’s got it in her, she just needs to be unleashed.
Have you had a Margo type in your life, or have you been that type? Someone who draws you into their orbit or brings out something in you that you’re like, Oh, I love being around this person?
Yeah, sure. I mean, I think it’s gone both ways. I think I’ve been in the shoes of a Margo and I’ve been attracted to it. I had a little bit of a fun time in between marriages, so to speak, and there were definitely people who were absolutely wrong for me, but I was very drawn in. Also knowing they were wrong for me, but just couldn’t get enough…it’s such a wild experience. And then I also played the part of drawing people in and just using them for what I needed. I knew it wasn’t going to become a relationship, and just having fun with it almost felt…I don’t know, it was a great time. I understand why Margo is having a great time, but [I haven’t experienced] anywhere near the extent of the deception and the games that she plays. But the draw, I can understand that, where there’s just this sexual energy that pulls you in.
After season one, there was some time where you weren’t totally sure if Hunting Wives was getting picked up again. Now you have a season three renewal already locked in. How does it feel to have that kind of stability?
It’s a fucking dream come true, it really is. I feel so, so lucky. I love this character, it’s a character of a lifetime. I love the people I get to work with. I couldn’t have wished for it…and it’s a steady job. If this goes for 10 years, I’ll be elated. Although maybe we can make Margo just chill a little bit on the working out and looking top-notch all the time, and tanned and everything. Maybe she just lets herself go a little. Let me just enjoy my pizza and my pasta on a daily basis, okay guys? Let’s not judge.
Do you know when you’ll go back for season three?
I’m flying out to Atlanta on November 29. We get started December 2. We’re premiering [season two] and then off we go, Season 3. I’m excited. I miss my Hunting Wives family.
I loved your and Brittany’s performance at the Las Culturistas Culture Awards. How did that come about?
Well, Bowen Yang was sat in front of us at the Emmys, where we ended up surprisingly presenting. We weren’t supposed to present, and so he was with us on that journey. It was so much fun. We were like, “Bowen, we have to present.” He’s like, “What? Right now?” He’s such a lovely person. From that, maybe that seed was planted in his head. We met Matt Rogers as well, and they’re such a great duo. They were the ones who came to us and said, “We have an idea. Hunting Wives and Heated Rivalry have been such a huge hit in the LGBTQ community that it would be really fun to do this, to have you guys open the whole ceremony.” We were super game for that. Like, “Okay, we’ll wear the hockey jerseys, but we need to bring in the Hunting Wives into it all.”
Do you think there is any kind of connective tissue that links The Hunting Wives and Heated Rivalry’s success beyond the queer relationships? Do you think there’s anything that both shows have in common?
I think it’s the forbiddenness of it all. That’s always something that we’re all drawn to because I think we all have our own secret thoughts that we don’t share with everybody. We all have our own fantasies, we all have our own things that we lust [over]. These shows kind of open them up. Heated Rivalry has a more important message in a way, because this is really something that the author was talking about. Being in such a macho world as hockey is, that’s a really brave thing, to come out publicly and to have a relationship publicly within that. I loved it, I thought it was really beautiful.
It’s different from Hunting Wives because Hunting Wives is…we’re a little more on the camp side. We’re true just raw entertainment and craziness, but we have that element of exploring sexuality in a town, in a world, where it’s frowned upon.
I’m sure that Margo and Sophie will be some people’s queer awakening in the years to come.
I sure hope so. Bring it on. Come on out, everybody. It’s a great place to be.
When you think back to when you were in maybe your early or mid-20s, were you dreaming of projects like this? What kind of arc did you want your career to have when you were very young?
Oh, gosh. Early 20s, I didn’t know what I wanted more than five minutes ahead of me. I was not one of those people who was planning ahead and goal-oriented. I was along for the ride. I really was letting life take me. I got swept away in this romantic-comedy film era with 27 Dresses and Heartbreak Kid and Couples Retreat. I was just enjoying every moment of it, not thinking about, Oh, this can come to an end.
After a few of those romantic comedies, I really started craving something that I could sink my teeth into a little more and to try my hand at drama. I started seeking out little independent films, which is something that I love watching: the little indies or foreign films. That’s when I remember The Ticket came along with Dan Stevens, and Billions came along—I feel like that’s a precursor to Margo Banks, Lara Axelrod.
Hunting Wives came at the perfect time because I had had that moment during COVID and after, where there were a lot of projects that were, I call them “yes projects.” I was saying yes because I needed to work, but I wasn’t feeling passionate about them anymore and it was really making me sad. I felt like projects that I really wanted to do, I couldn’t get in the room, and I started to have a lot of, I call them “head gremlins” talking to me, dark voices in my head. The voices telling me, Yeah, this is it. You’re expired. I mean, you had a good run. Say thank you, feel grateful. I just went, No.
For the past five years I’ve been working on my true passion project. Won’t say anything yet, but hopefully it’ll come to fruition. It’s something that I’ve built from the ground up and it’s written, I’ve got a director attached. We just need to get out there and get it made. But other than that, when Hunting Wives came along, it just felt like, oh, this was the hit that I really needed as far as getting passionate again about the work that I’m doing, because I just love this character and this job. It was incredible to not only be passionate about a character, but to feel relevant in my business again.
Do you think your definition of success has changed over the years?
I do think so. Becoming a parent changed all of that. Success for me, really, the most important success is the success of my family. And in business, I feel like I look back and I go, I’ve had a great career and how lucky am I if it continues? This is wonderful. So I’m happy for this show to have its success because all the people who are in it are so deserving. But success is a really…for me, it’s not linked to my work anymore. It really is linked to, am I successful as a person? Am I succeeding in my day-to-day life? Am I handling life well? In your 20s we talk a lot about being successful and a lot of it is linked to work. And then as we grow older and we realize what’s really important, then it shifts.
I’ve been asking myself those questions a lot lately, like, how do you define yourself outside of work?
Exactly. And that success is, how do people see you and how are you presenting to the world? Have you succeeded in becoming the person that you want to be, the best version of you? And where’s the work still need to be done? I have a 13-year-old boy who still wants to cuddle me at night and tells me he loves me [when I] drop him off at school. He is like, “Bye, Mom. Love you. See you later.” Thirteen. I’m like, “I have succeeded. This is a huge success.”
