War, fuel costs and aid cuts raise famine risk across 16 hotspots, UN agencies warn
Number of hunger hotspots increased from 13 in last June report
United Nations' food agencies are warning that hunger is set to worsen across 16 countries over the next six months, as conflict, soaring fuel costs linked to the war in the Middle East and steep aid cuts threaten to push more people into crisis.
In a report published on Friday, the Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and World Food Programme (WFP) said Sudan, South Sudan, Somalia, northern Nigeria and Yemen faced the gravest threats, driven by conflict, displacement, economic hardship and extreme weather.
All five have appeared on the list repeatedly since 2022. The Palestinian territories meanwhile, were moved down one level following the October 2025 ceasefire in Gaza, though 67 per cent of Gaza's population still faces crisis-level hunger or worse, and the report warned that the gains were fragile.
The number of hunger hotspots has risen from 13 in the previous report released in June, with Chad, Uganda, and parts of both southern Africa and central America added to the list, while Lebanon was removed.
In the latest joint report covering December 2026 to May 2027, the agencies warned that shrinking humanitarian funding was undermining efforts to avert catastrophe.
Aid for the five countries facing the gravest hunger threats fell by two-thirds between 2022 and 2025, said Conor Elliott, a senior official in FAO's emergencies and resilience office.
"These are huge changes in international support," he said, warning that the cuts were also undermining the collection of data needed to assess the scale of the crisis.

The report said armed conflict and violence were driving hunger in 12 of the 16 hotspots, while soaring food, energy and transport costs were further eroding people's ability to feed themselves.
The agencies said the developing El Niño weather pattern could aggravate the crisis, bringing drought and extreme temperatures to southern Africa and parts of central America, and potentially destructive flooding to parts of eastern Africa.
"There's going to be a very, very strong El Niño effect," said Elliott, warning that some areas were likely to swing from drought conditions to heavy rains in the coming months.
In Sudan, famine is a risk in 13 areas of North Darfur and South Kordofan through January 2027, with fighting disrupting food production, markets and aid deliveries.
The agencies also identified famine risks among displaced people in Somalia's Baidoa region and in parts of South Sudan, where escalating violence has compounded an already desperate humanitarian situation.
In Sudan, a risk of famine is expected to persist through January in 13 areas of the states North Darfur and South Kordofan, the report said. Nearly 19.5 million people, or 41 per cent of the population, faced crisis-level hunger or worse in the latest nationwide analysis. In two displacement camps in the country's southwest, acute malnutrition rates exceeded the threshold for famine.
Sorghum prices in Sudan more than doubled in a year, driven by the fighting and by higher fuel costs tied to the Middle East conflict. Diesel prices have also more than doubled from a year earlier because of the Middle East conflict, according to the report.
Drought linked to El Niño — a natural warming of Pacific Ocean water that disrupts weather worldwide — has added to the strain, affecting up to 55 per cent of cropland in Sennar and 70 per cent in Gedaref, two of Sudan's main farming states.
FAO deputy director-general Beth Bechdol told The Associated Press that El Niño-linked drought poses a major threat to Sudan on top of the war, compounding the conflict's toll on food production.
Fighting, shipping threats
In Yemen, the report says, hunger is likely to worsen as fighting which resumed in July spreads across multiple fronts.
The country has been at war since 2014, when the Iran-backed Houthi rebels seized the capital, Sanaa, prompting a Saudi-led coalition to intervene the following year.
The encirclement of the southwestern city of Taiz has created siege-like conditions there, and more than 300,000 people have been displaced since the escalation began, mostly in Taiz, Lahj and Aden provinces. In government-held areas, 1.8 million people face emergency levels of hunger, up from 1.6 million in the previous analysis.
No recent assessment exists for areas held by the Houthis, which have become harder for aid groups to reach as the conflict has intensified. The last nationwide projection, which ran through February, found 18.3 million people, more than half the population, were in crisis or worse.
Bechdol says the main obstacles to reaching Yemenis in need were political rather than geographic, noting that staff from other UN agencies had been detained. The Houthis still hold 73 UN staffers, including eight from the UN human rights office.
Yemen relies heavily on imported food and fuel, and the report warned that attacks on Red Sea shipping threaten that lifeline.
Wars continue to disrupt shipping in the Strait of Hormuz, the Red Sea and the Black Sea.
Aid has also fallen sharply as major donors have pulled back funding. Food and nutrition assistance to the five countries at the highest level of concern dropped by about 62 per cent between 2022 and 2025, and by 79 per cent in Yemen. Shifts among donor governments in recent years have taken billions of dollars out of humanitarian and development funding, leaving agencies unable to reach as many people as before, Bechdol says.
The predictability of funding matters as much as the amount because aid for farmers must arrive during the planting and harvest periods. With El Niño forecasts in hand, she says agencies should be prepositioning seeds and protecting livestock before disaster strikes, rather than responding to one crisis after another.
"When that window is lost, you don't get it back," she said. "We're funding losses and we're funding damage."
The agencies say Afghanistan, Haiti, the Democratic Republic of Congo and Gaza are also areas of very high concern, and that acute food insecurity is expected to worsen in the West Bank.
