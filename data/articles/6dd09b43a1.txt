Half a million years ago, prehistoric hominins in the eastern Mediterranean produced some of the earliest known stone blades, 100,000 years before the technology became established in the region. This discovery adds to growing evidence that early humans repeatedly invented new tools without necessarily adopting them, raising questions about what really drove technological evolution in prehistory.
Blades are thin fragments of stone that are at least twice as long as they are wide. In the region of the eastern Mediterranean known as the Levant, a blade technology tradition called the Amudian techno-complex became widespread around 400,000 years ago. This replaced earlier “Acheulean” tools, which included classic teardrop-shaped handaxes, as well as flakes – sharp pieces of stone that are removed from a larger rock and are cruder and more irregularly shaped than blades.
Yet after analysing hundreds of stone artefacts at the archaeological site of Jaljulia in Israel, researchers found traces of serial blade production 500,000 years ago that used a specific technique resembling that used in the Amudian system.
“We identify an earlier, small-scale manifestation of this method, which we term Amudian–like,” says team member Vlad Litov at Tel Aviv University in Israel. He say the ancient tool-makers were “culturally conservative”, and their initial flirtation with blade tools never flourished into an established practice.
“Despite reaching the techno-cognitive capability to make some blades, these hominins still adhered to their traditional Acheulean technology,” he says.
Yet the Jaljulia hominins weren’t the only ones to experiment with blade technology long before accepting it into their toolkit. At both Kathu Pan in South Africa and the Kapthurin Formation in Kenya, archaic humans began making small numbers of blades around 500,000 years ago, only to abandon the tools for hundreds of millennia.
“At Kathu Pan, we see a really remarkable [blade] technology, but then we don’t find it anymore, and the next blade production is about 70,000 years ago,” says Michael Chazan at the University of Toronto.
The identities of the various tool-makers remain unclear, and the blades at Jaljulia, Kathu Pan and the Kapthurin Formation were all created using different methods. This suggests that unconnected hominin populations simultaneously invented the technology required to produce blades, representing a case of convergent cultural evolution.
Litov suspects the blades didn’t initially catch on because the Jaljulia hominins preferred their Acheulean tools when butchering large prey like elephants. He thinks that when proboscideans began to die out in the eastern Mediterranean around 400,000 years ago, Levantine hominins were forced to change their hunting strategies, providing an incentive to switch to the smaller Amudian blades.
“We think the widespread adoption of blades was tied to a shift toward hunting smaller prey, which required more precise, meticulous butchery to maximise caloric yield, alongside delicate tasks like skinning,” says Litov.
Yet Chazan questions how much of an advantage these early blades provided. “Why would something that seems so highly adaptive [like the Amudian-like blades] not be sustained?” he says. “I think part of the answer is that they’re not superior. There’s nothing inherently better about a blade than a flake.”
Likewise, Steven Kuhn at the University of Arizona suggests that blades may have become truly useful only once humans learned to combine them with more advanced technologies to produce tools assembled from several parts, including those with a handle or “haft”.
“My opinion is that certain forms of blade production were advantageous in contexts where many tools were hafted,” he says. “Until hafted tools became important parts of toolkits, blades were just another source of sharp edges.”
