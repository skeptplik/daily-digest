Invading armies, subterfuge and conquest: How fire ant wars affect ecosystems
Ants disperse seeds, scavenge, turn soil and serve as a food source in their ecosystems. If local ants are displaced by invasive ants from elsewhere, the effects are widely felt.
JUANA SUMMERS, HOST:
Our next story chronicles an epic battle filled with invading armies, subterfuge and conquest, all on a scale that can fit in the palm of your hand. Here is science reporter Ari Daniel with a saga in miniature, starring ants.
ARI DANIEL, BYLINE: When it comes to helping out, ants more than pull their weight. Corrie Moreau is dean of collections at the American Museum of Natural History.
CORRIE MOREAU: Ants play such critical roles, like dispersing seeds, being scavengers, turning soil. They're also food for other organisms, right? They're a fundamental piece of the fabric of any ecosystem.
DANIEL: That fabric can unravel, however, if local ants are confronted by invasive ants from elsewhere.
MOREAU: One of the things they often do is displace native ant species.
DANIEL: But what happens when more than one invasive species shows up? Do they all ignore each other, compete for resources, and how might these dynamics evolve?
MOREAU: If we're trying to make conservation decisions or we're trying to do eradication programs, we need to try to understand what's going to happen through time.
DANIEL: These questions helped motivate a study that appeared in the journal Proceedings of the Royal Society B. It was led by ecologist Defu Chen of the South China Agricultural University. He was interested in the interactions between native ant communities in Southeast China and two nasty invasive species wreaking havoc globally, the first being the red imported fire ant.
DEFU CHEN: They are highly aggressive. They will immediately attack you.
DANIEL: And second, a honey-colored insect called the little fire ant. Both species pose a serious threat to native ants.
CHEN: They hurt and kill them, so I want to do some research on them.
DANIEL: Chen and his colleagues studied the ants in the lab and also outdoors in agricultural and urban settings. They found that initially both types of invasive fire ants battled one another, which insulated the native ants from more severe harm. Now, when little fire ants were outnumbered, they had a different strategy. Rather than fight, they went motionless.
CHEN: Playing dead.
DANIEL: And the red imported fire ants stopped assailing them. But in warmer weather, the little fire ants were able to grow their ranks more quickly. Once there were about four times as many, they then launched devastating and lethal attacks on the red imported fire ants.
CHEN: The two may coexist in the short term, but ultimately, one side will defeat the other.
DANIEL: The little fire ants then turn their attention to the native ants, which they displaced or wiped out. Chen says the results suggest it may be best to intervene early and counteract the invasive species before the native ants come under fire. As for entomologist Corrie Moreau, who wasn't involved in the study, she appreciates the findings, even if the management lessons don't apply across all landscapes.
MOREAU: They're doing this in a city. Often where we really want to make these conservation efforts is in places that are less touched by humans.
DANIEL: Still, Moreau recognizes the broader value of examining the complex and changing interactions between multiple species over time.
MOREAU: I think it's a call action to say we should be thoughtful about making predictions about what are the best solutions to protect biodiversity.
DANIEL: In other words, just because at the start a species may have a leg up or, in this case, six legs up, it doesn't mean it'll stay that way.
For NPR News, I'm Ari Daniel.
