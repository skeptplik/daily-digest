These days, Elias Matso is known for twisted corsetry worn by the likes of Sydney Sweeney and Jenna Ortega. But the first time he noticed the impact of a good costume, he was a kid watching Julie Andrews in Victor/Victoria. He sat by the TV watching the Oscar-winning actor perform “Le Jazz Hot!” in Art Deco gowns, decadent headpieces, and dramatic feathered capes. “I know she was acting in that scene, but I just felt like she was so happy to be wearing those costumes,” he tells Harper’s Bazaar. “And I was obsessed with that.”
Matso is now 24, and it’s easy to see the impact of that moment on his creative approach. A recent graduate of Polimoda in Florence with Charles James, Bob Mackie, Mugler, and Mr. Pearl on his mood board, he crafts avant-garde, couture corsetry. (It’s perhaps no surprise that he has a history of making costumes for drag performers.) His approach is personal and intimate, rooted in hand craftsmanship and collaborative dialogue with clients.
In October 2025, Sweeney made waves when she wore a crystal naked dress, pulled from Christian Cowan’s Spring/Summer 2026 collection and made in collaboration with the young designer. The piece featured twisted corsetry and a semi-sheer body that hugged the Euphoria star’s body—and, naturally, the public had quite a lot to say about it. “I did not anticipate that it would be as big as it was,” the artist admits. “It was really cool that the conversation left fashion and became a bigger conversation about women’s bodies and what modesty should look like or shouldn’t look like. And how a lot of the world doesn’t know what boobs look like.”
While the discourse surprised Matso, it reflected one of his own core beliefs: that “fashion is at least 50% the person who’s wearing the thing.” He keeps this in mind during fittings, ensuring clients feel comfortable and beautiful every step of the way. Below, he talks to Bazaar about his early references, his focus on craftsmanship, and the colorful steel-boning dress recently worn by Jenna Ortega.
You’re having such a moment right now, but looking back, what were your earliest experiences with design and fashion?
As a kid, I had a paper doll of myself, and whenever there were clothes that I wanted that I didn’t have, I would make them for the paper doll. It was pocket sized, and whenever I was bored—which was all the time, because I have attention deficit issues—I would pull it out and play.
Is Polimoda where you developed these techniques that we now see, like the twisted corsetry?
It is. It was my third year and I wanted to explore the tension that children hold, when they feel like they’re growing faster than they can keep up with, and feeling at odds with themselves, because that’s how I felt when I was a kid. There was this feeling, especially in the queer world, of being pulled in different directions, gender-wise. And so I had developed that idea, and then through sketching and conversations with my teachers, we started getting more and more towards this very literal idea of a twist. And then the collaboration that I did with Christian Cowan pushed it further.
How did the collaboration come about?
I was in New York over the summer before my senior year, and I was looking for jobs and not getting anything. I started looking into scooping ice cream at Van Leeuwen’s and stuff like that. And then, one day, I got a text from Alicia Keys’s stylist and a DM from Christian that was like, “Do you want to do some dresses for my show?” It was a good day.
The sheer finale dress for that show was later worn by Sydney Sweeney. Can you tell us the process of making that look?
It actually wasn’t originally supposed to be that fabric. We couldn’t find the fabric we wanted, and we couldn’t figure out how to put as many stones as we needed onto the fabric that we found. Then we just found this sheer fabric, and we were like, “Okay, it’s going to be the original dress, but it’s going to be completely see-through.”
I made it to fit this gorgeous six-foot-tall model from Finland. And this is why fit is so fun, because with that dress, there was this miracle where it fit her so perfectly one way, and then it fit Sydney, who has completely different proportions, in this totally other, also beautiful way. Often when things get pulled from a runway, they don’t fit like that. And it was just really cool to see.
Fittings are such an intimate thing. What are some of the conversations that you have with your clients?
I really like meeting people in person when I can because it just always creates a better outcome. I feel like the conversation is so much about your personality, but also your physiology, the way you’re built and the way you’re made up. People’s bodies respond differently. So it’s really important to do fittings and to check in with people before, during, and after the fitting, and ask, “How intense of an experience do you want this to be? What will make you feel the most beautiful, the most comfortable?”
Sometimes a little bit of discomfort is part of being comfortable, but you have to have signed up for that. That’s my thing that I’ve learned: Discomfort, if you’re prepared, can be part of it. But there has to be a conversation if discomfort is involved, or else we are forgetting that fashion is at least 50% the person who’s wearing the thing.
When you start a project, where do you begin? Specifically, where did the idea for the steel-boning dress that Jenna Ortega recently wore come from?
When I start a collection, I’m always looking through the archives of a museum, like the Whitney or often the Guggenheim, because you have less control over what you’re going to find. [For this piece,] I had found one painting that led me to Mark Ranger’s website. And we got in touch and then collaborated the whole year. It turns out we explore a lot of the same themes. Here, the dress was supposed to be about time and our body’s relationship to gravity and this idea of melting, not in a way that we’re trying to resist and slow down, but in a way that’s just natural and beautiful.
It’s made with steel boning. What was the actual process of working with the material like?
The goal was to make something unrecognizable, but using recognizable techniques. I already had a lot of steel boning because it’s the same stuff that’s inside all these corsets. And so I just got more, and then I started chopping it up into smaller and smaller pieces. I was able to create a relationship with Preciosa, who does crystals for a lot of big brands—I met them through a trip that they did to my school. I just came up with this weird technique that was, I guess, a test of my own physical and mental strength in a way.
I didn’t make it with Jenna in mind. I never thought it would have all these different lives and stages.
Your work has such a focus on craftsmanship. Why is this so important to you?
I guess I’m seeing it less and less valued in fashion. And even when something is hand-done and comes out gorgeous and successful, the people that wind up getting the praise for those things are not the people who do the thing, but the people who have curated those artisans—the artistic directors, the designers. My friends and I have just graduated, we’re looking for work, and I see my friends who have really singular skills getting so undervalued for their work and their time. That is something that is really frustrating to me. And in an age where so many things can be falsified via technology and the internet, I think things that you can’t fake are how something’s made, how something stands up over time, how something feels on the inside when you put it on. And designers care less and less about that, I guess.
That also comes from the speed at which a lot of stuff has to get made in today’s age. The care and the time you put into creating this piece is really rare and special. Is that something that you worry about being able to continue focusing on as you expand?
I’m in a transitional stage where I’m not sure what I’m gonna do next. Every day, I get people asking me to make dresses on this scale for free or for bafflingly low amounts of money. You know, I’m told all the time that there’s no budget for things. People want couture for no budget. People in this industry have no shame when it comes to asking for that. For emerging designers, there are so many of us that they know that if I can’t supply what they’re looking for, they will find another independent designer who has the resources or the funds or the ability to provide that thing for less than what it’s worth. I do worry about that. But this press is making me think that maybe there is a niche for something that walks the line between art and fashion and not in the aesthetic sense, which people talk about all the time, but in the financial and logistical sense. So that’s what I’m trying to figure out.
Also, I found that if people are asking for things for free, it’s very rarely the client themselves. It’s often their team. I love the art of styling, but that is another reason that I find one-on-one relationships and conversations with the person who you’re going to be dressing really important. It makes them understand the level of attention or care that they are interacting with.
You’ve dressed so many people now. Is there anybody that stands out that you feel like you’ve had a really good dialogue with?
This is still in process, but I’m working on a piece with Laverne Cox. I always wanted to work with her. And then starting to work with her, I was like, “I was right.” Because she has such appreciation and respect for the process of everything and the details and how everything works. She’s a really respectful and engaging patron, and we’ve had a lot of interesting conversations about the designers that we both look up to.
So, what’s next?
For me, I want to take the momentum that I have and do something that feels exciting with it. And at the same time, I am not sure that I have the experience to start a company, you know? I am in conversations with lots of people who I’ve always wanted to collaborate with, and it’s so exciting. Really, really exciting and mind-blowing. And at the same time, I’m reaching out to brands that I admire to see what openings they have because we all need stability. I’m trying to remain as flexible as possible.
