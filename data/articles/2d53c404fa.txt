At this point, the environmental effects of the meat industry have been well-established: one 2021 study found that meat accounts for nearly 60% of all greenhouse gases from food production, and a 2025 report estimated that animal agriculture was responsible for 12%-19% of total human-caused greenhouse gases.
According to the United Nations, one of the best things people can do to reduce their personal carbon footprint is shift from a diet heavy in meat to one that is more plant-based. Many of us know this, but in the US and UK, fewer than 5% of adults identify as vegetarian or vegan. One of the biggest obstacles, according to experts? People think it’s too hard.
“Adopting a whole new lifestyle can feel daunting, especially when you’ve been eating a certain way your entire life,” says Alyssa Fontaine, a registered dietitian and nutritionist, a vegan, and founder of Plant-Based Dietitians USA & Canada.
In her experience, people worry switching to a plant-based diet means missing out on essential nutrients and giving up on dishes and traditions they enjoy. None of this has to be the case, she says.
So how does one start eating a plant-based diet? We asked experts.
What is a plant-based diet?
A plant-based diet is one that consists primarily of – you guessed it – plants. This includes fruits and vegetables as well as nuts, legumes, whole grains and seeds. Typically, a plant-based diet excludes any foods derived from animals, like meat and dairy.
Is following a plant-based diet the same as being vegan?
Not necessarily.
Per the Vegan Society, the oldest vegan charity in the world, which coined the term in 1944: “Veganism is a philosophy and a way of living which seeks to exclude – as far as is possible and practicable – all forms of exploitation of, and cruelty to, animals for food, clothing, or any other purpose; and by extension, promotes the development and use of animal-free alternatives for the benefit of animals, humans and the environment.”
In other words: “Veganism is not simply a diet devoid of animal foods, but a social justice movement, value and belief system that includes animals when considering how others ought to be treated,” says Taylor Wolfram, a registered dietitian and longtime vegan.
A person eating a plant-based diet may not consider themselves vegan, and could still buy animal products such as wool clothes or leather accessories.
What are the benefits of eating plant-based?
Fontaine says she believes the three biggest benefits are health, environmental sustainability and animal welfare.
Health benefits: From a health perspective, plant-based diets are associated with a decreased risk of cardiovascular disease, a lower risk of type 2 diabetes, and a decreased likelihood of various types of cancer including prostate, breast and colorectal.
Plant-based diets also tend to be lower in saturated fats and cholesterol, and higher in fiber, antioxidants and phytochemicals.
In her work with clients transitioning from an omnivorous diet to a plant-based one, Fontaine says, the biggest difference she sees is in fiber intake.
“When we replace meat with plant-based protein sources, such as legumes, nuts and seeds, we’re also adding fiber to our diet,” she explains.
“Eating a fiber-rich diet can make a huge difference in improving digestion and bowel regularity,” she says, a significant benefit given that 40% to 50% of Americans struggle with digestive issues, according to a survey by the American Gastroenterological Association.
Environmental sustainability: It’s hard to overstate the environmental impacts. Damien Watson, spokesperson for the Vegan Society, points to a 2023 study that found that, compared with high-meat diets, vegan diets have been associated with 75% lower greenhouse gas emissions, 66% lower biodiversity loss and 54% lower water use.
Animal welfare: Besides their devastating environmental impact, industrial farms have been criticized for keeping animals in inhumane conditions.
“The biggest benefit of a vegan lifestyle is living in alignment with your values and treating animals with respect,” says Wolfram.
“For me, personally, being vegan means that I live my life, as much as I can, doing no harm,” agrees Watson. “That’s good for my emotional wellbeing, as well as good for my physical health, and that of other living creatures and the world we inhabit.”
What about protein?
One of the biggest misconceptions about eating a plant-based diet is that you won’t get enough protein, says Fontaine.
“‘Where do you get your protein?’ If I had a pound for every time someone asked me that question …” says Watson. His protein comes primarily from beans (including soy and tofu), pulses, peas, nuts, broccoli and “loads of other healthy plants”.
“Inadequate protein intake is not a concern on a vegan or plant-based diet when the diet is properly planned,” says Sue-Ellen Anderson-Hayes, spokesperson for the Academy of Nutrition and Dietetics, adding that nutrition deficiencies occur “in all dietary patterns” without proper planning.
For further proof, Wolfram points to the numerous high-performing athletes who have adopted plant-based diets, like soccer star Alex Morgan, Formula One driver Lewis Hamilton and NBA player Chris Paul.
How does one start eating plant-based?
Start small: You don’t need to change everything all at once.
“I recommend substituting some animal foods with plant foods,” says Wolfram. For example, try black bean tacos instead of beef, soy milk in your cereal instead of dairy, or tofu stir-fries. “It doesn’t have to be complicated,” she says.
“Baby steps,” agrees Anderson-Hayes: put progress over perfection, and remember that not every day will look the same.
Identify your go-to meals: One of the greatest challenges in transitioning to a plant-based lifestyle is not knowing what to cook, says Fontaine. “Most of us have our established meals,” she says.
Changing your diet requires finding new recipes, seeking out new ingredients at the store and learning how to prepare them. But, Fontaine says, “spending a little extra time on meal prepping and developing new cooking skills in the beginning can make a big difference”.
One thing she often suggests to vegan clients is the “vegan go-to 10 method”: finding 10 vegan meals you love and making them on repeat until preparing them becomes second nature. Once these are in place, planning meals, shopping and cooking becomes much easier.
More from How to start:
Research supplements and/or fortified foods: The one vitamin that vegans need to be particularly conscientious about consuming is B12, which helps support the nervous system and is not made by plants, says Watson.
Anderson-Hayes also suggests regularly consuming sea vegetables or seaweed for iodine, and fortified foods like nutritional yeast and soy milk for vitamin D and calcium.
Learn how to order at restaurants: It is easier than ever to eat a plant-based diet, says Watson. “It is rare now to find a restaurant that doesn’t offer a vegan option, and most supermarkets have whole sections devoted to plant-based food,” he says.
This can require some forethought. Anderson-Hayes recommends checking out restaurant menus beforehand to see what your options will be. Look for items like “black bean burgers, black bean and corn salad, hummus, falafels or falafel burgers”, she says.
Consult an expert: A plant-based diet can be “healthy, nutritious and sustainable”, but it isn’t “one-size-fits-all”, says Fontaine.
“Everyone is at a different stage of life and has different nutritional needs,” she says.
Because of this, it can be helpful to work with a registered dietitian, says Wolfram. If this is not in your budget, she also recommends reputable free online resources, like VeganHealth.org. “It’s written by vegan, registered dietitians,” she explains.
