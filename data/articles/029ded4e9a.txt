Was it worth it?
I let Apple’s always-listening AI summarize my life for two weeks
Apple’s Siri Recaps make a lot of tradeoffs to protect privacy. But I’m not convinced the benefits outweigh the costs.
I let Apple’s always-listening AI summarize my life for two weeks
Apple’s Siri Recaps make a lot of tradeoffs to protect privacy. But I’m not convinced the benefits outweigh the costs.
I’ve been asking myself this quite a bit over the last month or so while testing both the Amazon Bee Frontier and Siri Recap on the Apple Watch Ultra 4. Both enable a wearable to ambiently listen to your conversations and, using AI, summarize them for you. Why? So you can remember better and, hopefully, stay more present.
The main difference is that Amazon’s Bee takes a maximalist (and frankly reality-bending) approach, whereas Apple is going for a more minimalist, privacy-first take. Both, however, require explicit disclosures to the people around you about recording what they say, and potentially follow-up explanations about how exactly this all works. This past week, I’ve been testing the Siri Recap beta to see if Apple’s is a less dystopian experience.
Apple presents Siri Recap as a sort of backup notetaker. As a college student, perhaps you’re less concerned about writing down what a professor says and can just take in the lecture. Say you attend a parent-teacher conference solo. You can focus on the discussion and then whip out your phone later to reference what was said when explaining it to your spouse.
These are fairly innocuous scenarios. But given the discourse around privacy and surveillance gadgets this year, it’s easy to imagine how always-listening devices could be misused. Apple has taken great care to create a version of this that at least considers that bystanders may feel uncomfortable knowing someone else’s Apple Watch may be taking notes on what they say.
The feature isn’t turned on by default — you have to opt in. Users can then program when the feature turns on. For example, only at the office between the hours of 1 to 2PM on weekdays. Unless saved, all recaps are also set to self-destruct after your choice of one, three, or seven days. Within the summaries, there’s no viewable transcript detailing who said what and when. That makes these recaps more difficult to use as “receipts” for arguments, court cases, or blackmail. There’s also no raw audio file that’s ever saved. Not to get too into the weeds, but the audio gets processed and then immediately deleted through a secure exclave within the 2026 Apple Watch’s S11 chip that’s inaccessible to Apple and other third parties. The vast majority of the processing is also done locally. Whatever does get sent to the cloud is done through Apple’s Private Cloud Compute. Because the feature requires this hardware, it’s impossible to use on older Apple Watches.
It’s not perfect. Unlike Live Rewind, another audio intelligence feature, there’s no outward indicator or audio chime to signal recording intent. So, if you do decide to ethically disclose your usage, you have to be prepared to explain everything I just described if someone asks questions.
Maybe that’s worth it — if Siri can be trusted to be truly helpful.
The reality is that better privacy often means a less useful AI product. The more AI knows about you, the better the summaries from these devices tend to be. To preserve privacy, Apple prevents Siri from learning everything about you and the people in your life. For example, Siri Recap doesn’t train on your voice. Excellent for privacy! But that also means Siri has a much harder time discerning when you’re actually having a conversation. That leads to some funny but ultimately unhelpful recaps.
For instance, I was watching a TikTok in which a chronically ill creator was updating their followers about being sent to the emergency room because of sepsis. Siri recapped that video as if I, or potentially someone I knew, had been sent to the ER. Another TikTok weighing in on an AITA Reddit thread got summarized as if I had discussed a shady homewrecker named Dana trying to seduce her bestie’s husband by wearing a glittery blue bikini top at a concert. Because attribution was stripped, the summary could read as if I were the guilty spouse returning between 2 and 3AM, covered in Dana’s glitter. In both instances, the lack of attribution also made the resulting summaries hilarious and confusing.
Also, neither video was something I wanted or needed to remember. I suspect Siri summarized them because I was playing the audio out loud on my phone, which sits pretty close to my Apple Watch. There’s an easy workaround here. Siri Recap automatically turns off if you use headphones, meaning I could just eliminate recaps from podcasts, audiobooks, TV shows, etc., by always wearing my AirPods. But that’s me adjusting my behavior to accommodate Siri’s inability to discern when I’m talking.
Another quirk: Siri doesn’t know what I want to remember or find memorable from a given conversation.
I turned on Siri Recap during my briefing and demos for the new smart glasses from Google, Samsung, Gentle Monster, and Warby Parker. When I sat down to write, the resulting recap helped me remember the main topics discussed. I also found it useful that a handful of interview recaps were linked to a corresponding Voice Memos file. The thing is, Siri Recap heavily prioritizes restating the facts. That means bullet points tend to emphasize statistics, factoids, and dates. That’s useful in straightforward planning meetings, but less useful for what you might personally find to be a salient takeaway. For example, in an interview with a source, what I found most interesting was a personal anecdote and some strong opinions about RFK Jr. My Siri Recap from that interview omitted it in favor of more concrete details.
Meanwhile, personal conversations are rarely as straightforward. Work meetings often have agendas. Chats with friends and family can jump across a zillion topics in the span of 10 minutes. Plus, most of my conversations should be deleted from my brain to make room for the really important things. I didn’t need to see a detailed summary of what my spouse and I thought of the last episode of Nathan Fielder’s The Curse. (It was bonkers.) I’d love to never revisit a 20-minute argument with my cousin over hardwired versus battery-powered smoke detectors. (Too bad, the recap outlined the main points.) A recap of my spouse and me debating movie recommendations might be a useful recap, but it just says “seeking less anxiety-inducing media” and “refusing to ever watch Star Wars.”
My recaps were also a lesson in why conversational context matters. Sometimes, Siri correctly assumes that gossip is irrelevant to the broader conversation at hand and omits it entirely. Other times, its blunt, matter-of-fact recaps of jokes or my friends venting read like an unhinged Regina George from Mean Girls building an AI-generated Burn Book.
That inconsistency is why relying on AI to make judgment calls on your behalf feels unsettling.
I keep getting stuck on what problem these features are meant to solve. Tech companies usually say ambient listening is meant to help jog our memories or to help us stay more present. Which brings me back to my initial question: Was it worth it? Did Apple’s efforts to protect privacy prove we can have our AI note-taking cake and eat it too?
Outside a few work-related scenarios, I haven’t found Siri Recaps to be useful. Even writing this, I spent a lot of time scrolling to find a small handful of examples that I would want to discuss. On the plus side, I’m not terribly worried about people snooping through my recaps. Of the more than 50 recaps generated, I’m confident 90 percent would be gibberish for anyone but me.
Conversely, I found more of what Amazon’s Bee generated to be “useful.” But after seeing Bee’s raw transcripts and summaries of every spoken moment in my life, I believe we’re not meant to live that way. I’ve since nuked all my Bee data.
But even if I prefer Apple’s approach for privacy reasons, my gut says this tech isn’t the answer for jogging memories or staying present. Whenever I’ve forgotten things, the negative consequences have rarely — if ever — led to irrevocably changing my life for the worse. Usually, it’s a bitter lesson that’s forced me to grow and adapt. And anytime I’m not present in the moment, it’s usually because I’m on my phone.
Ambient listening wearables can be helpful when used in low-stakes, straightforward situations where everyone consents. But for me, it’s often in little ways that have many less-invasive alternatives. I mean having to explain to the less tech-savvy people in my life that my Apple Watch now has this feature that recaps our conversations. Having to watch how their faces mull it over. Having to muster the will to answer their questions about how Apple handles privacy with this feature versus other devices I’ve tested. Having to smile when my spouse jokes about censoring their humor because of my job. Having to write in my work Slack that when my coworkers see me in the office, I am testing AI devices that may be recording any conversation, and that I am happy to turn them off if it makes them uncomfortable. Having to weigh whether I have enough bandwidth or time before every conversation with strangers to be thoughtful and ethical.
Right now, there are no established cultural norms telling me how to easily pay this cost. All I know is that if I turn the feature off, I don’t have to think about it at all.
