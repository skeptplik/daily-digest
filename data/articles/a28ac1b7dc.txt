Since the broadest and deepest tariffs imposed by the Trump administration were invalidated by the Supreme Court in early 2026, the president’s team has made quick work of reconstituting the revenue they brought in through a range of other tariff authorities.
From 10-12.5 percent forced labor tariffs levied under Section 301 of the Trade Act of 1974 to 50 percent duties on Canadian goods levied under Section 338 of the Tariff Act of 1930, as well as existing global tariffs on steel, aluminum, autos and more imposed under Section 232 of the Trade Expansion Act of 1962, the administration has used various trade tools to tax foreign imports.
But what if all, or even some, of these duties were repealed? According to the Urban-Brookings Tax Policy Center (TPC), nixing all three forms of duties currently in play would reduce the average family’s costs by an average of more than $94 per month.
The think tank analyzed four scenarios in which tariffs could be reduced, finding high variability in impact between the options.
Firstly, repealing the Section 338 tariffs alone would barely move the needle on prices, TPC analysts wrote. The duties, while high, apply to a relatively small portion—about 5 percent, or $20 billion—of Canadian imports. The group’s reporting estimated that eliminating the duties would reduce the average U.S. tariff rate from 10.1 percent to 9.8 percent, and the ultimate impact on prices would be equivalent to about 0.05 percent difference (about $30 per family in 2027).
Another option—rolling back the forced labor Section 301 duties (which apply to 60 global economies) as well as the Section 338 duties—would bring the average U.S. tariff rate down by 2.4 percentage points to 7.4 percent. In such a case, TPC analysts wrote that prices across goods and categories would fall by 0.3 percent, saving families an average of $420.
The group also analyzed a hypothetical scenario wherein the Section 338 duties, the forced labor Section 301 tariffs and other Section 301 duties applied to China, Brazil and Nicaragua individually were eliminated.
China faces longstanding Section 301 duties of variable rates on a range of goods that date back to President Donald Trump’s first term in office, while Brazil was hit in July with a 25 percent Section 30 tariff for a number of different political reasons (stacking on top of its 12.5 percent forced labor rate). Nicaragua also faces the same forced labor duty rate, and will see another 10 percent Section 301 tariff take effect on Jan. 1, 2027.
Pulling all these duties combined would result in a 0.4 percent across-the-board price reduction, saving household an average of $540, the research showed. Apparel and leather products would be among the categories most impacted by a total wipeout of the Section 301 duties and the Section 338 import taxes, with analysts writing that prices on such goods would fall 9.4 percent.
The fourth and final analysis included a repeal of the Section 338 tariffs, all Section 301 tariffs and the Section 232 duties.
According to TPC, the Section 232 tariffs, which range in rate from 25-50 percent, cover the most U.S. imports of any of the statutes—about 21 percent of everything brought into the country. Imposed in 2025, they apply to metals, cars, semiconductors, lumber, pharmaceuticals and more.
Slashing these duties alongside the others would yield by far the greatest impact, cutting the average U.S. tariff rate by an additional 5 percentage points to 1.2 percent. Such an action, analysts wrote, would reduce prices on products by 0.7 percent, and the impact would be most acutely felt for purchasers of furniture, electronics, machinery, vehicles, car parts and computers.
Ultimately, the price contraction would translate to a $1,130 reduction in prices for families.
