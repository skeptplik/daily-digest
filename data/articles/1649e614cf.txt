The European Commission's publication of its draft Digital Fairness Act (DFA) last week prompted uproar within the games industry, with many notable voices candidly expressing their concerns. Opinions in the wider industry were more measured, possibly because some of the practices targeted by the DFA – such as loot boxes or pay-to-win – are wildly successful economically, but not uniformly popular amongst players.
The impact of the DFA as proposed would go far beyond less-cherished free-to-play mechanisms, though, with significant impact on game design. Some voices are warning that the European industry would be hit particularly hard. An analysis of the draft shows several requirements that could prove challenging for European developers, and ample potential for significant financial penalties.
Key provisions
Let's start with the provisions on paid-for virtual currencies (Art. 14). It is important to note that this term does not only cover virtual-currencies directly purchased against real world currency, but also what is only indirectly acquired - which is to say, secondary currencies within the game. Where virtual items or other products can be acquired using paid-for virtual currencies, companies will be required to display their corresponding monetary value in real currency.
That's not a new demand, but it can be tricky to fulfil. When paid-for virtual currency is initially purchased, it's pretty evident that the publisher must display what the user has to pay in real currency. The DFA goes beyond that, and requires that the "value" in real currency is also displayed when the virtual currency is spent.
So, what's the problem with that? First: How should the calculation be made when the virtual currency can be purchased, but also earned through gameplay? Second: If the virtual currency is spent within the core gameplay, it's anything but immersive to display anything in real-world currency: Imagine a medieval fantasy MMOG where a player uses virtual gold to buy a virtual sword, and the NPC shopkeeper must state the item's price in Euros.

Third: games companies assume that virtual currency (once acquired) does not have "value" once purchased, as it generally stays within the games ecosystem and cannot be "cashed out" into real money. If one were to accept that virtual currency does have a real-world value, this could have far-reaching consequences, for example when a player is banned or when a game is sunset. Also, if one player gives virtual currency to another player in the game world, we might have a taxable transaction in the real world under the assumption that virtual currency has a "value". Nothing of that is explicitly said in the DFA, but it is or at least could be the consequence of attributing a monetary value to paid-for virtual currencies. It's a slippery slope at best.
"Pay to win" for core players is invariably as appealing as a steakhouse is to vegans, or an SUV to environmental activists – so many might welcome its removal. The DFA outright prohibits "pay to win" for children, and the regulator will be hoping that game companies will be forced to drop this practice entirely as a game might not be popular if some players can buy strong items and others cannot. Only time will tell if that comes to pass.
It is possible that the definition of pay-to-win outlined in the DFA might be too narrow to have this effect. ‘Pay-to-progress and pay-to-win mechanisms’ means in-game or in-app purchases that are necessary (or are presented as necessary) for in-game or in-app progress or success, that are acquired through payment transactions or in exchange for paid-for virtual currencies. A set of super-strong armour available only for payment might be really helpful in the game, but it only falls under the definition if it is "necessary" for success or presented as such – which is arguably not the case if you can still succeed without it, even if that success would require days or weeks of grinding. What the lawmaker probably wants to regulate is a game offering items that provide an advantage in the game in exchange for money. Ultimately, this would mean that adults would be able to buy stronger items, where kids can't.
Under the law, "pay to progress" could cover anything from unlocking the full game after a free trial, to paid DLC.

There are also other provisions relevant for games companies which are less central for game design.
Technical issues
You may have understood by now that laws, just like games, can have technical issues that mean they just do not work as intended. The DFA draft looks as if that will be the case. In general, it suffers from the same problems as other recent legal acts of the European Union. It is over-engineered and under-workable, as it goes into too many details instead of setting out principles. Ironically, laws which are as detailed are exceedingly complex to apply – and often easier to circumvent than principle-based laws.
The DFA also has large overlaps with other EU legal acts – the European Commission itself has identified six of them in Article 4. In the event of any contradictions, it is not the DFA, but the other law which governs. That might sound good at first, but it means that anyone wanting to understand if something which is regulated by the DFA must first read six other legal acts to know if that practice is actually allowed or not. Big publishers with large legal teams and budgets for external lawyers might be able to do this – but certainly not indie developers. Over-complexity is also the natural enemy of effective enforcement.

The fact that the DFA is so difficult to apply is particularly problematic, as fines can be up to 6% of an infringer's global yearly turnover – much higher, by the way, than the most serious cyber incidents under the EU's Cyber Resilience Act (CRA).
Why the European games industry is particularly affected
Some representatives of publishers, such as Supercell's CEO, as well as industry associations have warned that the DFA might hit European publishers especially hard. This may sound overblown, as the DFA also applies to non-European publishers when they are doing business in Europe. Still, smaller companies tend to focus on their home market first, at least until their user base is large enough to scale up and go international. Also, most companies tend to treat compliance with the laws of their home country as a priority – more than the laws on the other side of the globe.
Practically, enforcement tends to hit domestic companies more often than companies abroad – unless the latter are really huge. This means that an indie publisher based in Bordeaux, Milan or Berlin is more likely to face scrutiny from European regulators than an app publisher no one has ever heard of with a registered address somewhere in Asia – even though arguably, the latter are no less aggressive in their monetisation. Conversely, the European indie may get away with practices counter to Chinese law longer than its Chinese competitor.
What next
The European Commission has invited feedback on the DFA proposal, and people in the games industry have until January 14th to provide it; it may be that changes are made in response. Regardless, there will be some time until the DFA will be finally adopted – but it won't go away. Like it or not, in-game monetisation through virtual currencies will not go away either, as indie games and traditional retail games – which is to say, games sold for a one-off payment of, say 80 Euros with no in-game monetisation – are facing competition from vibe-coded games. The challenge for games companies will therefore be to design their games in compliance with the DFA. Doing so will require an in-depth analysis of the game monetisation and the law – which also is a "pay to progress" exercise.
