In an era defined by speed and sameness, embracing nuance proves invaluable.
Such was the underlying message at Beauty Inc’s 2026 The Catalysts conference, delivered by Jeff Lee, cofounder and chief executive officer of Dibs Beauty; Chelsea Riggs, founder and CEO of Amika, and Bee Shapiro, founder and CEO of Ellis Brooklyn, in conversation with Robin Burns-McNeill, cofounder and chairman of Batallure Beauty.
The founders — each with their own unique background — talked about balancing ambition with adaptability and creativity with commercial rigor in pursuit of legacy brand status.
Riggs kicked off the conversation with a brief on Amika’s 20-year history and the brand’s commitment to scaling at its own pace after it expanded internationally too soon. “I know everyone gets shiny object syndrome about going global, but it’s very challenging,” she said. “When we think about how we continue to create this legacy brand of the future, it’s taking our time and putting one foot in front of the other, and making sure that growth is sustainable.”
Shapiro, who founded her fragrance line after 13 years at The New York Times, shares a similar mindset. “We have expanded globally,” Shapiro said, calling out her international retail partners, Mecca and Space NK. “Fragrance is a much more global category, and it is important for a fragrance brand to go further abroad, but we’re not necessarily going to.
“It’s hard to launch in a new country,” she continued. “They don’t know you. It’s like starting from ground zero.”
Indeed, scaling at any level is a gamble, and in the case of Dibs, its national distribution plan proved equally risky. “We built the brand on the premise that there was a woman, not in New York or Los Angeles, who didn’t feel seen by makeup, but we didn’t have any data or any proof,” Lee said. “For someone like me to go to the backcountry of Mississippi, and to find that customer to meet her where she was, that’s been the biggest risk.”
That said, partnering with Ulta Beauty enabled the brand to achieve its goals. “What we look for, and what we have in our partnership with Ulta Beauty, is a retailer that knows how to look around the corner,” Lee said. “[CEO] Kecia Steelman and her team have certainly done that, and it’s about finding that partnership with the retailer that knows where the ball is going for both of you in the next two to 20 years.”
Lee, Riggs and Shapiro agreed that taking chances and establishing a point of difference today is not only beneficial but necessary. “You need to make sure you’re optimizing for how you’re creating demand for your brand. How are you making sure your company is resonating and has not only a clear point of difference, but a clear customer and a clear consumer need,” Riggs said.
Honoring hero products is also essential. “There’s this obsession with newness,” Lee said. “Color cosmetics is probably the most obsessed with trend and newness, but how we combat that is a return to our heroes. [We’re] doubling down on what made us great in the span of five years, trying to drive mindshare toward that is the single best strategy because we’re not going to win in the newness hamster wheel.”
In fragrance, Shapiro said brands need to “keep the inspiration alive” to capture and engage the consumer.
“It’s a dream category, and it’s an aspiration category,” Shapiro said. “Gen Z and Gen Alpha, who absolutely love fragrance, are willing to cosign. They’re willing to jump in on that journey with you, which is I want to spray this on, and I want to feel something. It doesn’t need to have a before and after; it just needs to have the feeling.”
That emotion, though, is not something AI can replicate, Lee added. “There is no substitute for the human mind, the human ingenuity, the human creativity, the emotion,” Lee said. “All of that is so integral to building a forever brand. We’re all companies and brands in not only different stages but at different stages in our life cycles. As a five-year brand, we have enough agility to implement AI and to test it out, but there are certain things that we cannot substitute for.”
AI has, however, helped “bridge the capability gap” at Dibs, freeing employees from mundane, everyday tasks and allowing more creativity. The same is true at Amika and Ellis Brooklyn.
“I know of many examples across the organization, mostly operationally, where tasks that were taking people 45 minutes to complete are now cut down to five minutes [with the help of AI],” Riggs said. “Now, we can scale with the same amount of people because I’m freeing up my best people to do their best work and not bogging them down with tasks that, frankly, nobody wants to do.”
Where Shapiro is concerned is the effect AI will continue to have on managers and lower-level positions. Ellis Brooklyn no longer employs in-house copywriters, as they now use an AI plug-in trained to write in the brand’s style and voice. “I worry because I have kids and I’m like, ‘What’s going to happen to this junior-level person who doesn’t get to make mistakes and come through and grow?'” she said. “AI has been amazing for directors and top leaders because it gets rid of the monotony, but what’s going to happen in the future if you have less coordinators and less lower-level managers come through?”
The conversation concluded by circling back to the earlier topic of distribution and knowing when and where to scale. Speaking to the fellow entrepreneurs and founders in the room, Riggs reemphasized the power of patience. “Take your time because I believe that if you have created the demand and you continue to do that, it’s not going to go away, and the opportunity [to expand] won’t go away either,” she said.
