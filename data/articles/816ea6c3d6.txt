Jaime Ray Newman is keeping her cowgirl hat and camo gear in tow as she prepares for “The Hunting Wives” Season Two, arriving on Netflix on Thursday.
The book-to screen adaptation of May Cobbs’ bestselling novel follows a clique of Texan housewives and their secret society filled with erotic and extralegal affairs.
“Well, listen, I’m a Jewish girl from Detroit, so my life could not be more different than Callie’s, but I was named Jaime Ray,” the actress says of her character. “So my whole life I have been cast as Southern gals.” 
	“People have sort of always mistaken me as someone from the South. For better or for worse, I guess — it’s helped me careerwise maybe,” she adds. “But when I was auditioning for [Callie], I listened to a lot of ‘Real Housewives of Dallas.’” 
It’s the first time the 48-year-old is returning to a character for a second season and she’s excited to continue developing Callie’s identity.
	“When I’m in the thick of preparing for a role or shooting something, I just love taking long walks, having the person that I’m channeling on a podcast in my ear or on a YouTube channel where I can just hear them over and over and over again. And I did that a lot with Callie, preparing for her,” Newman says. 
Season Two finds Callie and her husband, Sheriff Jonny, starring in a reality show about their lives called “Married Justice.”
	“She’s always a little dangerous to be around because you don’t quite know what she’s going to do from moment to moment because she is a woman who feels deeply. She has conviction. And especially Season Two with ‘Married Justice,’ it’s such a perfect title. Like, that would be her autobiography,” Newman says. “She just always goes for what she believes is right. But with the juxtaposition of how in her personal life, she’s very secretive. She has a lot going on that she could never let public.” 
The show’s first season received rave reviews and quickly developed a devoted fandom eager to see the storyline progress.
	“I think the second season, we really doubled down. I feel that with the success of the first season, we could have become a little more ‘demure’, a little more, you know, shy and coy, and I think that we all agreed that that was not the way to go,” Newman says.
“The wigs got bigger… a lot of makeup… a lot of tight leather for Season Two. We just really wanted to go bigger, bolder and badder,” she adds.
The show is led by a team of all-women directors — Julie Anne Robinson, Jennifer Getzinger, Cheryl Dunye, Melanie Mayron and Maggie Kiley — which is a first in Newman’s career.
	“Rebecca Cutter, our showrunner, takes a lot of pride in that. We only get four directors a season, and they’ve all been women, and queer women. I feel very safe on those sets,” says Newman.
“I’ve probably only worked with, like, two or three female directors in 25 years of working. It’s such sensitive material and I feel safer in their hands.”
	One such sensitive plot point is her character Callie’s marriage and burgeoning queer relationship with her friend Margo Banks, played by Malin Akerman. In the first season, we see Callie navigating the complicated social choreography of preserving the public image of her marriage while exploring a side of herself she has to keep concealed.
“My heart breaks for Callie. I wish that she would have been born in a time and place that accepted her for who she was, or who she is, but we’re all victims of where we’re born, and the time in which we’re born in, you know?” Newman says.
The show depicts the complicated circumstances women across backgrounds are forced to contend with. They’re expected to present themselves to the outside world as “perfect,” keeping it together no matter what is happening in their personal life, but that daily performance is much deeper than just how you do your makeup or wear your hair.
“They’re girlfriends; their female friendships are ride or die. In a way, they’re married to each other. The husbands are a little bit sidelined. It’s female friendship front and center,” Newman says. “And if you look at ‘The Real Housewives,’ they’ve built an empire on female friendship. Now, there’s catfights and the dramas that keep people tuning into the next episode, but that’s power.”
Amid all the drama and suspense, at the core of the show is an unapologetic exploration of relationships between women, no matter how they take form.
“It’s not about men! You have your own power, you know?” Newman says. “You don’t need to ask permission from someone else to feel powerful.”
