A woman says her blocked nose left her with a decade-long addiction to decongestant nasal sprays that cost her thousands.
Louise Gough, 30, failed to read the fine print on her over-the-counter Sudafed which warns it is for short-term use only, and ended up addicted.
She came so reliant on the medication she was spraying up to 15 times a day and felt like a "prisoner in her own body".
Sudafed said the safe use of its products was its top priority.
But a consultant surgeon who treats this dependency said the warning should be more prominent on packaging.
Gough, from Caerphilly, estimates she spent about Â£2,000, secretly buying bottles that cost about Â£7 because her partner was frustrated by her spending.
"I used it [when I had a cold] and instantly within 10 seconds my nose was unblocked," she said.
"The relief of that was phenomenal.
"It's partly my fault. I didn't read the box and I didn't realise that you could only use it for a certain amount in a time period.
"No one ever warned me when I bought it. It just turned into a really vicious cycle."
Prolonged use can make congestion worse, so even when Gough did not have a cold, her nose would be blocked and she would use the spray.
She had developed rhinitis medicamentosa, a medication-induced nasal congestion also known as rebound congestion.
"I found myself feeling really anxious if I didn't have a bottle on me," she said.
"This was particularly bad if I was out with my partner as I'd ask him to get me one and he'd be annoyed.
"I laugh about it now but it wasn't funny at the time.
"I felt like a prisoner in my own body and I couldn't get away from it."
Ear, nose and throat (ENT) consultant Vik Veer said decongestant sprays work by temporarily restricting blood flow to the nasal lining.
Prolonged use can trigger a cycle in which the nose becomes increasingly congested as the medication wears off.
"The thing you were doing to try to make you better actually makes you even worse," he said.
"Every ENT surgeon will know about [rebound congestion] and would see this problem almost in every clinic."
Official recommendations are that nasal decongestant sprays, external containing xylometazoline and oxymetazoline should not be used for more than five consecutive days.
But Veer said he believes rebound congestion can begin much sooner.
"I think the evidence points towards you having problems within three days of using these sprays," he said.
When Gough started taking decongestants, the recommendation was seven days, with the current five-day warning added to packaging in 2026.
When Gough used two bottles in 11 days on her family holiday to Greece she said she knew she had to quit, and stopped taking them three years ago.
"It's like someone has shoved something so far up your nose you physically can't breathe at all," she said.
The first few days off the medication made her feel "claustrophobic in her own body" as she struggled to breathe naturally, she said. But after a month she was finally able to breathe through her nose unmedicated.
But Gough has not regained her sense of taste and smell as strongly as before.
A Sudafed spokesperson said its products followed strict regulatory and industry rules, with usage information provided on packaging, patient information leaflets and its website.
It encouraged anyone with questions about using its products to speak to a GP or pharmacist.
