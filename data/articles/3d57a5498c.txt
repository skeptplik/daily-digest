In truth, it took many human scientists to develop the molecules that are the mini-company’s star products: two antibodies that are still in the early stages of development and were designed to treat a rare form of muscular dystrophy. The company, Mithril Medicine in Cambridge, Massachusetts, also hired part-time consultants to advise on the project, and paid at least five contract research organizations to conduct experiments. Many more researchers will be needed to carry the drug further, especially if it eventually enters clinical trials. “It takes a village to develop a drug,” says Jeb Keiper, a partner at Third Rock Ventures, a venture-capital firm in Boston, Massachusetts.
But the news sparked a wave of discussions about whether the one-person biotech is the drug-discovery business model of the future. “We will see more and more of these small teams that are enabled by AI,” says James Zou, a computer scientist who works on biomedical applications of AI at Stanford University in California. “They will be very powerful for identifying good opportunities and moving quickly.”
Pint-sized powerhouses
Itsy-bitsy biotechs are not a new concept. Entrepreneur Matt Davidson started his first biotech company in 2013, nearly a decade before ChatGPT’s public debut. Davidson kept the roster trim for years, with only two full-time employees until the drug he was developing was ready to enter late-stage clinical testing. The company never had a physical laboratory, Davidson says. All the lab work was contracted out.
His current company, Azora Therapeutics in Los Angeles, California, has a whopping five employees. He uses AI more for summarizing reports than for scientific discovery and says that AI tools have made tiny biotechs more efficient. “We use consultants a little bit less than we used to,” he says.
Mithril Medicine leaned more heavily on AI than Azora does, and tasked Anthropic’s AI assistant Claude with helping to analyse data and suggesting next steps, as well as taming the piles of emails and paperwork that come from working with multiple contract research organizations. “What began as an exercise in staying lean evolved into the thesis for an entirely new operating model, which we now call Pipeline-in-a-Person,” wrote Cody Tranbarger, Mithril’s former chief executive and lone employee, on LinkedIn. (Tranbarger, who works for Anthropic since Mithril was absorbed into it, declined to speak to Nature, as did Anthropic.)
