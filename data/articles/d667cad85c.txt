UN climate talks in Turkey are closing in on a final deal with the announcement of a new package that COP31 presidents Australia and Turkey hope will achieve consensus on how to advance the fight against global warming.
The new draft of the deal was released Sunday, two days after the conference in Antalya was scheduled to end.
If approved, it would acknowledge for the first time that global warming is on course to exceed the 1.5-degree Celsius (2.7-degree Fahrenheit) threshold that countries had sought to preserve, while calling for faster development of carbon-removal technologies to eventually reverse the overshoot.
The draft omits mention of the fossil fuels that scientists say are driving climate change, an issue that has been at the centre of frictions at the talks. Instead, it includes language referring to a previous COP at which governments had agreed to transition away from oil, gas, and coal.
“We believe that we have put forward a text which all the parties can agree upon,” COP31 President Murat Kurum told a plenary session early on Sunday.
Kurum, who is also Turkiye’s environment minister, said the conference would reconvene later on Sunday morning to approve the deal.
It was unclear if negotiators had received the wording overnight, which would give nations only a few hours to submit objections and changes.
The presidency gave delegations several hours to raise any objections to the deal, with a session scheduled at 07:00 GMT to begin approving it.
An earlier draft had drawn strong condemnation for failing to mention fossil fuels, the largest contributors to climate change.
Environmental group 350.org said it was disappointed by the continued omission in the new draft, as well as its encouragement of carbon-removal technologies.
“Carbon capture is one of the biggest scams of the overshoot era, and our leaders are on the verge of falling for it,” said Sara Shaw, the group’s campaigns and networks director, referring to the practice of trapping carbon dioxide before it reaches the atmosphere.
Observers to the closed-door talks said Saudi Arabia and other big oil-producer nations had pushed hard for keeping fossil fuels out of the text, and carbon removal in.
A representative for the Saudi delegation declined to comment.
Kurum said he believed concerns around fossil fuels had been addressed, as had those around helping developing countries finance their adaptations to climate change.
The new text followed intensive consultations between Australia, which is leading the talks; Turkiye, which is hosting the meeting; and nearly 200 delegations from many of the world’s richest, poorest, largest and smallest nations.
The summit’s agreement is meant to address how the world will deal with climate change, which drives heatwaves, floods and other disasters.
An opening draft from Australia on Monday called for a faster phase-out of fossil fuels. A slimmer version on Thursday removed every mention of them after Saudi Arabia and other major oil and gas producers objected.
Key issues that slowed down the agreement included how much effort countries should make to wean themselves off fossil fuels, such as coal, oil and gas.
Other issues included whether to encourage industrial carbon capture and storage of pollutants, as well as whether ramping up electricity should only be done with renewables, such as wind and solar, or also involve other sources like natural gas and coal.
To avert the most catastrophic effects of warming, experts said the world needs to urgently and drastically cut fossil fuel emissions.
The two-week talks in the Turkish city of Antalya were due to end on Friday.
In a letter sent on Saturday to this year’s cohosts, Turkiye and Australia, 100 civil society groups warned that they were “on course to preside over one of the most disappointing COPs” the UN has ever held.
The letter stated there were only hours left “to save this COP from becoming a low point of three decades of climate negotiations”.
In the lead-up to these talks, the United Nations said the world would likely blow past the 2015 Paris Agreement goal of limiting warming to 1.5 degrees Celsius (2.7 degrees Fahrenheit) above pre-industrial temperatures.
