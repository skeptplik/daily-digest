Around the holidays, many people like to get cosy with a cup of cocoa and a heartwarming festive movie. Personally, though, I nestle into my blanket pile and turn on the 1984 fantasy classic The NeverEnding Story. It’s a tale about a magical book, a fuzzy dragon and two heroic boys trying to save the world from a mysterious force called The Nothing that’s devouring humanity’s imagination. To fight off the fear this instills in the young protagonist, Bastian, he proclaims: “It’s not real, it’s only a story!” Well, sorry, Bastian, but I’m here to tell you that The Nothing is real, and it’s out there. Well, it may be.
See, we think there is no place in the universe that truly has nothing in it. There are various quantum fields pervading absolutely everything, and along with them come energy and strange “virtual” particles that randomly pop in and out of existence. So, when we talk about a vacuum, we mean an area of space with the minimum possible energy, not an area with no energy at all. The vacuum of deep space, as far as we know, has the minimum possible energy.
But that’s only as far as we know. There is also a chance that it is possible to have an area of lower energy – a true vacuum to our universe’s possibly false one. Were that the case, the cosmos that we know and love would be at what is called the local minimum energy, rather than the global minimum energy. To picture the difference, imagine holding an egg in a bowl. The bottom of the bowl is the local minimum – in this case, the unit is height – so it is the lowest height the egg can settle into without having energy added to overcome the barriers of the bowl’s sides. If you add that energy, say by shaking the bowl, the egg will then find its way to the global minimum height: the ground.
For the universe, going from a false vacuum to a true vacuum – a process called vacuum decay – would have similar consequences as the fall would for the egg. And overcoming the walls of the metaphorical bowl that safely cradles our cosmos in its false vacuum could be surprisingly, unsettlingly easy.
It would begin with a bubble. Those fields that fill all of space-time obey the laws of quantum mechanics, which means that they occasionally behave in ways that are extremely unintuitive and nearly impossible to explain without a litany of equations and years of graduate school. One of those unruly behaviours is quantum tunnelling, where an extremely tiny particle can spontaneously go through a barrier, rather than over it. This would be like the egg passing straight through the bowl for no obvious reason. The likelihood of this happening to any individual particle is extraordinarily low, which is why eggs (made of many particles) never pass ghost-like through bowls. The universe has a whole lot of particles, though, so tunnelling does occasionally happen.
So, say a tiny particle tunnels into a true vacuum. That would create a tiny bubble of space-time that is, at the very least, closer to truly empty than the cosmos we’re used to, or at the very most, a bubble with absolutely nothing in it, not even the quantum fields that enforce what we think of as the laws of physics. What it would be like inside that bubble and what could happen next depend on a whole lot of properties that we don’t know, such as the energy difference between the true and false vacuum and the surface tension of the membrane separating the bubble from regular space-time around it. Depending on that energy difference, the bubble could be only subtly different from the rest of the cosmos or it could be incompatible with the fundamental forces and particles that make up the universe as we know it.
That is an important distinction because, depending on the size of the bubble and its surface tension, what happens next could be catastrophic. If the bubble is very small or has very high surface tension, it will simply shrink away and disappear just as easily as it appeared. If not, the bubble will behave just like The NeverEnding Story’s Nothing, but much, much faster (and probably without the help of a terrifying evil wolf called Gmork). It would expand outwards at the speed of light, engulfing everything it encounters. Simulations of what would happen inside the bubble universe vary in their details, but they mostly agree that the in-bubble universe would eventually collapse entirely.
Obviously, a bubble of nothing spreading throughout the universe and destroying everything in its path would be cataclysmic. The fact that we don’t even know if this is possible isn’t great, either. So, how do we tell if this could happen? It’s shockingly difficult, which is why it hasn’t been done yet. If vacuum decay is possible, the most likely mechanism for us to find out is if it were to actually happen, and because the bubble expands at the speed of light, we wouldn’t know until the moment it absorbed us. It isn’t particularly comforting news.
Nevertheless, there are hints we can look for. The precise values of various constants, from the mass of the Higgs boson to the strength of the strong force, can tell us a great deal about whether each of those particles and forces are stable. If they’re all stable, great! If any of them aren’t, those are potentially liable to decay and cause a bubble of true vacuum – not great! Those measurements need to be almost unimaginably precise to know for sure, and we haven’t reached sufficient precision yet. Right now, our best measurements seem to say our universe is right on the edge of stability, and increased precision in those numbers could push us over to either side.
The other hint would be to look for those very tiny bubbles of true vacuum that wouldn’t spread and devour the universe. In recent years, researchers have suggested that the area between two colliding black holes might be the perfect spot for these bubbles to pop up. That tracks with the universe not yet having been enveloped by nothingness, because the bubbles would probably immediately fall into the black holes and be safely contained before they could spread. But if there’s enough time between true vacuum foaming into existence and being swallowed up, various bubbles could merge together and form tiny black holes, which we might be able to detect as they evaporate away.
My two cents is that the possibility of finding any proof of a true vacuum lies so far in the future that it’s probably best to cosy up with a nice movie and try to forget about the potential looming, unpredictable destruction of the entire universe. Can I recommend The NeverEnding Story?
