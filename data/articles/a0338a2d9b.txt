Once again, game companies are putting more adverts into PC and console titles.
In June, Electronic Arts announced a new EA Advertising platform that would let companies market "directly into gameplay", something that the firm insisted was "designed to enhance, not disrupt, the player experience". (We asked Electronic Arts to contribute to this feature, but they declined to take part.)
The pitch for EA Advertising promises unintrusive ads, such as billboards within football stadiums in EA Sports FC, something that the company admittedly already does.
Unsurprisingly, users aren't overly happy about the notion of having more adverts in their games. This isn't the first time that EA has tried this; back in 2020, the company was forced to remove ads from UFC 4 after a player backlash, although the implementation was much, much more intrusive on that occasion, with adverts appearing in the middle of action replays.
Louise Wooldridge, senior research manager at Ampere Analysis, suspects that EA is looking to cut its advertising overheads with the rollout of this new division. She argues that the EA Advertising platform is an alternative to services such as Anzu and Bidstack, which tend to account for "more than 35% of ad spend"; this money will now go back to EA.
"In this sense, EA is effectively testing the viability of bringing the ad network model in-house," Wooldridge says, adding that the model has "significant potential".
"Ampere's title data shows that EA Sports FC games alone on PlayStation, Xbox, and Steam were played for almost 5 billion hours in 2025, with average monthly active users of 23 million," she continues.
"EA is sitting on a pool of ad inventory that could potentially be worth hundreds of millions of dollars annually across its sports franchises. It is an opportunity that other major publishers will increasingly need to consider, particularly as development costs continue to rise and publishers look for new revenue streams. That said, EA is unusually well positioned to capitalise on intrinsic in-game advertising given the strength and breadth of its sports portfolio; the opportunity is unlikely to be as readily transferable to publishers whose games are less naturally suited to advertising within the game world."
Gold mine
Gamers might not like the idea of more ads in their games, but, as always, cash is king. Per Virginie Chesnais, chief marketing officer at adtech firm Happydemics, "there's a lot of money to be made", pointing to the ad-supported tiers of streaming services like Disney+ and Netflix as being "extremely lucrative".
"EA is one of the biggest game developers in the world, and they're probably very confident in being able to pull in new audiences while retaining its existing ones," she says. "When you own titles that cover sports such as football, American football, UFC, and Formula 1, you can use that to tell advertisers that you have these highly engaged audiences which fit into who they're looking to target. Plus, with the data that EA no doubt has on its players, brands would jump at the chance to get in front of them."
Itai Cohen, chief strategy officer at mobile user acquisition and monetisation firm DT, agrees, saying video games are a natural extension of other forms of entertainment supported by advertising.
"It's mainstream, it commands enormous amounts of consumer attention, and the industry has made real progress in changing outdated perceptions of who gamers are," he says. "EA has the added advantage of premium IP and environments that can be very attractive to advertisers."
There's also an element of EA needing to grow revenue at a time when, as Aldora founder Joost van Dreunen points out, the company has "hit the ceiling" on its addressable audience. There's also the small matter of the firm having to pay back huge levels of interest on the loan used for its acquisition; servicing this debt is reported to cost the company $1.8 billion per year.
"For years, EA has talked about its 'player network' on earnings calls, gradually adopting the vernacular of media companies," van Dreunen explains. "Given its history of aggressive monetisation, EA is chasing revenue growth rather than audience expansion, and advertising is a viable avenue across its major franchises."
"EA is chasing revenue growth rather than audience expansion, and advertising is a viable avenue"
It's not just EA expanding advertising in its console and PC titles. In July, Microsoft announced that it was testing adverts to its streaming offering. This is different to EA's proposition, which is an integrated approach: Xbox's solution has ads appearing before sessions, rather than interrupting gameplay.
Speaking to GamesIndustry.biz, an Xbox spokesperson says that this is to make games "more accessible to more players".
"We're learning whether giving players the option to stream games they own for free with ads can create additional ways to access and enjoy gaming across supported devices," they explain. "The test is designed to help us better understand how players engage with this model and what experiences resonate with them."
Untapped potential
Whether players like them or not, the market for in-game ads is enormous; Ampere Analysis estimates that this sector on mobile alone was worth $28 billion in 2025 and is set to rise to $44 billion by 2031.
"By that point, its size would rival that of today's entire console games and services market," Wooldridge explains.
Overall, van Dreunen describes the broader in-game ad market as "nascent relative to its potential", but it is growing as overheads increase across the industry.
"After a series of false starts, an indirect revenue model, similar to what we see across mainstream entertainment, has finally begun to materialise in response to rising development costs and a growing segment of consumers unwilling or unable to keep paying premium prices for interactive entertainment," he says.
Happydemic's Chesnais agrees that in-game advertising falls short of its potential, but adds that it is usually hard to see how well this kind of marketing actually works.
"The market is still relatively under-allocated when compared to the attention gaming commands," she says. "Part of the reason is that it has traditionally been hard to measure the outcomes and effects of in-game advertising. But recent updates are helping make in-game advertising more comparable with other media channels. This includes brand metrics, allowing advertisers to assess measures such as awareness, recall, and attribution consistently across channels and better understand the role in-game advertising can play within the wider media mix."
DT's Cohen adds: "There's been a lot of interest and experimentation in the space, and the model clearly has potential, but it hasn't yet scaled to the point where it's a standard part of the monetisation strategy for most developers."
A different ball game
One factor that has held back the market for in-game ads is that console and PC players have historically been incredibly resistant to the business model, unlike mobile players. Cohen argues that mobile phones are already "multipurpose" devices, so users are more accepting of ads. But the model has been implemented very differently on this hardware.
"Advertising can feel like an intrusion into something they've already bought"
"Mobile set the stage for advertising early," he says. "As gaming shifted toward free-to-play, ads became part of the value exchange: players got access to the game for free, and formats like rewarded video gave them currency, content, or progress in return for their attention. Console and PC developed around a very different expectation. Players paid for the game, so advertising can feel like an intrusion into something they've already bought."
Wooldridge adds: "PC and console gamers are typically paying more upfront, so advertising feels like an extra layer of monetisation on top of something they've already purchased. Big screen games also typically involve deeper immersion, which can be spoiled by a not-so-subtle or ill-placed ad. The underlying economics are just different, and this means that advertising on those platforms is less culturally established, and the audiences have different expectations."
Happydemic's Chesnais also argues that part of the resistance to in-game ads on console and PC is that these experiences tend to be wildly different to those found on mobile.
"Mobile gamers are usually casual players who might be looking for something to do during the daily commute or second-screening while something plays on the TV," she says.
"Console gaming, on the other hand, is the most immersive experience in terms of media channels. You have to be highly engaged in what you're doing, and it demands a player's attention.
"This is partly why people have been reluctant to get on board with console advertising. Whereas on mobile you can do something else while the ad is playing, console gamers see ads as too obstructive and getting in the way of them playing."
Chesnais does say that adverts can be successful in PC titles so long as they are done in a way that doesn't ruin the overall experience.
"There are some really good examples of in-game advertising that blend in well within the gaming environment," she says. "Sports Interactive did exactly this in the latest instalment of Football Manager by implementing contextual adverts as part of the advertisement hoardings seen during gameplay – as you would see if watching a football match from the stands. By blending the ads into the experience and replicating something you'd see in the real world, it actually adds to the game rather than detracting from it."
"We are not interrupting gameplay with advertisements"
Speaking about the company's ad-supported streaming tier, an Xbox representative said that one of its tenets in this new business is that gameplay will not be interrupted by advertising.
"Ads are shown before a streaming session begins, and the core gameplay experience remains unchanged. We are not interrupting gameplay with advertisements. This is also an optional experience, and players can choose other ways to play," the Xbox rep says.
"We've outlined five principles that guide our approach: we start with the player, ads should create value, we do not interrupt the flow of gameplay, we hold ads to the same quality bar as our content, we're clear about what's advertising, and we make sure ads fit what players expect on each platform. We're using this test to learn directly from player feedback and ensure these experiences remain aligned with those principles."
Backlash risk
The historic distaste of in-game advertising on console and PC could have an impact on what EA and Microsoft appear to be doing, according to the experts we spoke to. Aldora's van Dreunen says that while advertising is a "critical revenue model" for most other forms of entertainment, the presence of marketing has "always profoundly affected cultural expression".
"Beyond off-screen curation, where brands dictate what they will and won't be associated with, television writing itself changed to accommodate a growing ad load," he says. "The same will happen here. You can't simply bolt ads onto existing games. The format will have to evolve to make room for them, and that transition is currently a clear source of friction."
Wooldridge says that EA faces a risk of consumer backlash, although notes that this risk is relatively small, because the company's approach appears to be less intrusive. Microsoft, she argues, is a bigger target.
"For EA, the risk is quite low – specifically if the ads remain intrinsic and are not disruptive to gameplay," she says. "Of course, if EA starts introducing video ads into premium games, it will be met with significant backlash. The risk of consumer resistance is arguably greater for Microsoft. But the value exchange should be quite digestible for consumers: watch an ad, get free access to a game, rather than pay $70 for a game and then see ads within it. So on the surface, neither should necessarily attract significant resistance."
"If it means lowering the price of subscription services and the games themselves, then I think most gamers would take that exchange"
At the end of the day, Happydemic's Chesnais says that both companies are going to have to "let the ads do the talking".
"Gamers will no doubt be hitting the Reddit forums already discussing why it's a bad thing for the industry," she says. "But if it means lowering the price of subscription services and the games themselves, then I think most gamers would take that exchange. According to a Deloitte study, one in five players would play a title more frequently if in-game ads were of high quality."
The same report also showed that a fifth of players quit a game due to "low-quality ad experiences", while playable ads with "disruptive features" double the odds a user will quit.
Chesnais concludes: "Gamers don't hate ads; they hate bad ads. If EA and Microsoft can display ones that don't diminish the playing experience, then it could actually be a great thing for the industry."
