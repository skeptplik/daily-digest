Middle-aged women in England are the least likely to successfully give up smoking, according to research.
Smoking is the leading cause of preventable death and illness in the UK. While overall smoking rates in England have declined in recent decades, rates in some regions have increased. About 6.75 million people smoke in England, of whom around 14% are women aged 45-60.
Academics at University College London (UCL) examined data from just under 8,000 women who had made at least one serious attempt to stop smoking in the past year in England between 2013 and 2026. The study found that only 18% of those aged 45-60 successfully managed to give up.
Overall, around 22% of women under 45, and just under 21% of those over 60, managed to quit.
The findings suggested that women in mid-life were nearly 19% less likely to quit successfully than younger women, 12% less likely than older women and more than 10% less likely to quit compared with mid-life men, the authors noted.
The study, published in the journal BMC Medicine, also found that middle-aged women from more deprived backgrounds were more than 25% less likely to quit than women from affluent backgrounds. Only 15.8% of participants from deprived backgrounds managed to stop smoking, whereas 21.2% of more affluent middle-aged women succeeded in giving up.
Dr Sarah Jackson, lead author of the paper and professorial research fellow at UCL’s Institute of Epidemiology and Health Care, said the findings showed that “simply encouraging more women to seek help may not be enough”.
“Women in mid-life often face a combination of pressures that can make quitting smoking particularly challenging,” she said. “Work and financial worries can create considerable stress, and for those with caring responsibilities, finding the time to look after their own health can be difficult.”
Menopause-related changes, such as disrupted sleep, changes in mood and increased stress, could also be a factor, she added, while those from more disadvantaged backgrounds were also more likely to have higher levels of nicotine dependence, which could make quitting harder.
“We need to better understand women’s experiences to ensure that stop smoking services are tailored to meet their specific needs,” she added.
Michelle Mitchell, chief executive officer at Cancer Research UK, which funded the research, said: “Growing up, my mum and aunts smoked heavily, so I have seen first-hand how difficult it can be to break free from tobacco addiction in middle age.
“Everyone should be able to access the support they need to stop smoking, regardless of age, where they live or the circumstances they face.
“The UK Government must continue to fund stop smoking services and should target additional support in communities where smoking rates are highest.”
Caroline Cerny, deputy chief executive at Action on Smoking and Health, said it was “particularly concerning” that, despite being among the most likely to use proven quitting aids, disadvantaged women in midlife are least likely to successfully give up smoking.
She said: “We need to understand what is driving this inequality and ensure that our efforts to reduce smoking respond to the realities of women’s lives.
“As we work towards a smoke-free UK and ending the harm from tobacco for good, we must ensure that those facing the greatest barriers to quitting are not left behind.”
