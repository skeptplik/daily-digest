The loneliest peptide party in New York
The peptide curious are looking for answers, and finding dubious products instead.
On a rainy fall evening, an experimental art venue in Chelsea Market had a small line out the door. There are always lines in New York City. Viral food trends, gallery exhibitions, pop-ups, and parties. The folks in line looked like your average joe, ready for a Friday night on the town. You wouldn’t look twice — except, perhaps, for a man with a wizard beard and a laser sword strapped to his back.
Inside, the man with a laser sword stood in the middle of the cavernous bottom floor of a two-story warehouse strewn with large bean bags. On the wall were psychedelic projections of jellyfish, shrimp, and other sea creatures. A bizarre choice, considering peptides won’t induce any trippy vision quests, and not a single person was getting groovy. The industrial beams had posters with QR codes that you could scan to get samples of Afterglow, a new type of peptide product. According to seemingly discarded pamphlets around the venue, Afterglow isn’t a standard injectable or oral peptide. The company makes strips that dissolve under the tongue. The “Afterglow Peptide Field Guide” also described what each sample does, dosing recommendations, and potential side effects. At the upstairs bar, bartenders mixed custom cocktails called Afterburn — complete with bioluminescent veggie nectar — and Margatrutide. (For the record, a bioluminescent veggie nectar cocktail tastes as awful as you think.)
Welcome to a peptide launch party.
Peptide parties are old news in San Francisco, but they’re somewhat more novel in New York City. Milling around the edges of the venue were various reporters and photographers. We were all trying to get a sense of why anyone had bothered to come out. Not that many did. Despite the venue’s size, the main floor had plenty of empty space.
One person I spoke to was a fencer with sore joints. They’d heard that one of the peptides might help with recovery. Another had a father in the wellness industry and had heard vague things about how effective peptides could be. One woman, staring at a projected jellyfish, merely shrugged. Her friend was more into “wellness stuff” and she had no plans, so, why not check out a party? A man, staring into his bag of Afterglow samples, was mostly interested in weight loss but scared about the safety of gray market products. A few feet away, another man was furiously reading the Afterglow pamphlet. “I like to know what I put in my body,” he said.
The only problem was he didn’t remember which sample he had tried.
A common refrain: Well, this beats doing research online. This, at least, was a social event where you could theoretically connect with other people. And there were clusters of strangers speaking to each other. Mostly, near the bar. Everywhere else, people stared awkwardly on their phones, their eyes occasionally roaming the room without meeting anyone else’s gaze for more than a second or two. After a while, they’d disappear into the back room and reappear with a goody bag full of peptide samples.
After 90 minutes, I decided to leave. For the umpteenth time that evening, I’d walked up to someone, asked about peptides, and got a noncommittal “I really don’t know anything — I just came to learn more.” (When asked if they had, they invariably shrugged and pointed to the goody bag.) It was clear that the only peptide evangelists here were likely associated with the launch. The whole thing, to me, seemed as if someone had tried to draw a cool party from memory. The last thing I saw was a person draped in a ghost sheet sitting alone in the corner.
As it turned out, the man with the laser sword was a performance artist who’d been invited to attend. Out of journalistic integrity, I asked if he had ever tried peptides. He told me he was more interested in the unifying energy of the universe. When I got up to leave, he waved and said, “Giggles.”
If I were peptide curious, the Afterglow launch party was a pretty awful way to learn anything about these substances. To be fair, Afterglow’s Partiful invitation mostly described this event as “an environment great for aura farming” and the “hard launch of [its] first of many experiments in culture.” Nevertheless, as part of the press, I did walk away with a smattering of Afterglow’s products. In total, I got packets of what Afterglow calls Charge (NAD+), Rejuvenate (glutathione), Wolverine Blend (BPC-157 / TB-500), Strength Blend (CJC-1295 / Ipamorelin), Glow Blend (GHK-Cu / BPC-157 / TB-500), Connect (PT-141), GLP-2 (tirzepatide), and GLP-3 (retatrutide). Or at least that’s what the packaging says. There’s no way to truly verify gray market peptides for safety or efficacy because they’re unregulated products.
First of all, last I checked, GLP-1 medications do not upgrade like iPhone models. Afterglow’s pamphlet correctly lists that your body has “three hormone switches”: GLP-1, GIP, and glucagon. The accompanying descriptors of what each does are broadly correct, though lacking in nuance. For example, there is a hormone called glucagon-like peptide 2, but it’s not tirzepatide. The “real” GLP-2 is a hormone related to intestinal health. Tirzepatide is still considered a GLP-1 medication that’s a dual agonist because it works on two hormones (GLP-1 and GIP.) The GLP-2 moniker is a misnomer popularized by ill-informed influencers. The same goes for retatrutide, which, as of this writing, is still an unapproved GLP-1 medication that works on three hormones (GLP-1, GIP, and glucagon) and is therefore a triple agonist. There is no such thing as GLP-3.
For all the samples, there were no instructions regarding dosing on the packaging — just another QR code leading to Afterglow’s product page. Those pages happen to contain instructions for how to use the strips, and the amount of product within the strips. There are disclaimers about how little studied the compounds are, but nothing about interactions, side effects, or what to expect while taking. Just a throwaway line to talk to your doctor.
All that information, you’d have to find on the pamphlet given at the party. (Ironically, some “important cautions” on the pamphlet state to avoid taking samples with alcohol, at a party where the booze was flowing.) Provided you didn’t throw it out. One would hope that information is on a box if you were to buy directly from the seller. But for all intents and purposes, what I just described is what a clueless partygoer would have to go on.
If everything in that pamphlet were 100 percent factual, perhaps a conscientious partygoer could feel reassured. On that front, I have bad news. The pamphlet calls PT-141 an “FDA-approved peptide that boosts sexual desire for both men and women.”
PT-141, also known as bremelanotide or Vyleesi, is FDA approved. But only for premenopausal women suffering from hypoactive sexual desire disorder. It is not approved for post-menopausal women, men, or for general sexual performance enhancement.
Another concern: How does one evaluate a sublingual peptide strip? Specifically, is this delivery method even scientifically proven effective in humans? Spoiler: It’s not! A collapsible FAQ menu on Afterglow’s site states, “We don’t know yet. Nobody has published how much of most peptides reaches the blood from a strip compared with an injection. We’re funding studies to measure exactly that, and we’ll share what we find.”
As for why you’d want to even try a sublingual strip? Afterglow’s website suggests it’s more convenient than injections or pills. No refrigeration, no needles, and no need for learning how to reconstitute a drug. Just pop a strip under the tongue and go.
And good luck trying to find reputable sources of information on your own. Afterglow says it’s funding its own research, but its site has no reviewable documentation just yet. A Google search brings up a questionable AI overview, a handful of links from dubious med spas, a couple of dead links, and one PubMed study in mice. Most of the links also primarily discuss the difference between oral peptides in pill form and injectables — so not terribly relevant here.
I’d consider it fair game if I were confident that anyone at the party I went to did their due diligence. As it stands, I talked with over a dozen people who were mostly there for vibes, booze, and freebies.
Not all peptide parties are created equal. Some happen on WWII cargo ships, complete with panel discussions about peptide supply chains. Another “peptide 101” talk I attended in New York happened at the back of a bitcoin bar. There, most attendees also knew very little about peptides, but at least they got treated to a PowerPoint presentation about the basics. There was a dubious demonstration of how to inject oneself that would’ve probably made any pharmacist or doctor scream. But at the very least, peptides were defined and attendees could ask questions. (The answers to those questions were on the right track with regard to the dangers of the gray market. They were less reliable about scientific rigor and why “anecdotal evidence” is not the same as clinical evidence of safety.)
But a striking, recurring theme is just how much is unknown. These substances aren’t well-studied in humans, so there’s not much evidence as to long-term benefits or side effects. The peptide curious know shocking little about the substances they’re supposedly interested in. Likewise, it’s incredibly difficult to get reliable, unbiased data on gray market interest or usage, though there have been attempts to quantify this surging demand in recent months. Case in point, telehealth provider Hims & Hers just conducted a consumer survey of roughly 2,000 American adults. Of those surveyed, 68 percent said they had little to no knowledge about peptides. Less than 18 percent were even able to identify that peptides are chains of amino acids. Even so, 40 percent said they were interested or very interested in trying injectable prescription treatments — including peptides — if offered by a telehealth service.
Even so, peptides are everywhere. The word appears in copious ads for skincare creams, weight loss drugs, and supplements. Looksmaxxers, influencers, and celebrities like Gwyneth Paltrow and Joe Rogan talk about them in podcasts, documentaries, and social media as if they’re miracle shortcuts. Hims & Hers has even announced it’s launching a service called Peptide Plus — nevermind that the vast majority of what it’ll offer are not actual peptides.
In this kind of environment, of course more people will become peptide curious. Which makes it even more concerning that the most readily available information sources are not medical or scientific experts well-versed on the topic at hand. There are dubious links online summarized into less-than-critical AI overviews. There are pamphlets handed out at weird parties, where there’s more booze and schmooze than education. Influencers branding themselves as experts as they flash crudely summarized studies in a bar back room. Industry panels that are inaccessible to the average consumer, full of jargon and assumed knowledge of the science at hand.
It’s clear something needs to be done until general knowledge improves. Until then, the parties will continue.
