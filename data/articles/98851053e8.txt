It takes a rare genius to write a satire that is still read 300 years after its publication. Most satire dwells on the peccadilloes of soon-to-be forgotten politicians. And it takes a unique genius to write a satire that is more relevant today than it was in the days of powdered wigs and knee breeches. That is exactly what Jonathan Swift achieved when he published “Gulliver’s Travels Into Remote Regions of the World” on Oct. 28, 1726.
The third part of Swift’s masterpiece focused on “remote regions of the world” under the sway of scientists, technologists and “projectors.” When Swift wrote, these technicians were an isolated species. The Royal Society, the object of his particular wrath, was only founded in 1660 and was powerless compared with the Anglican Church, which provided Swift with his living. But today these scientists, technologists and “projectors” rule the world and control the future, in effect transforming a satire of a slice of 18th-century English society into a dissection of the AI-dominated world that we are busy creating.
The first place Gulliver visits, in his third voyage, is a flying island, Laputa, inhabited by people who would be described, in today’s jargon, as “on the spectrum,” philosophers who are obsessed with abstract thought, particularly about mathematics, astronomy and music, but can’t cope with the practical world. These philosophers are so devoted to abstract reflection that they employ “flappers” to shake them out of their philosophical reveries by slapping them with a blown bladder full of tiny peas or pebbles. Devoted to intellectual purity, they are incapable of dealing with the mundane practicalities of life.
