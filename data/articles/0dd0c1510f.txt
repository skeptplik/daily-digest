Pesticide exposure may affect people’s fertility, but more conclusive research is needed.
Pesticide exposure may harm fertility, experts warned as the European Union weighs simpler rules on how pesticides are approved.
About one in six people worldwide experiences infertility, according to the World Health Organization (WHO). Lifestyle factors such as smoking, heavy drinking and obesity can raise the risk of infertility, while exposure to pesticides and other environmental pollutants has been linked to poorer sperm and egg quality.
Pesticide exposure may affect people’s fertility by interfering with their hormones and reproductive cells, a pair of Italian endocrinologists warned at a briefing organised by the Brussels nonprofit Health and Environment Alliance (HEAL).
In men, infertility is commonly caused by low sperm counts, abnormal shape or movement of the sperm, or issues with semen ejaculation, according to the WHO. Chemical exposures can disrupt testicular function and sperm maturation through oxidative stress, endocrine disruption and direct damage to reproductive cells, which may affect fertility.
In 2023, researchers found that men with more exposure to certain insecticides, mostly through work, tended to have lower sperm concentrations.
The results of other studies are mixed, but the findings are enough to worry experts.
“There is a close relationship between male fertility and sperm quality,” said Dr. Aldo Calogero, an endocrinology professor at the University of Catania.
In women, infertility is typically caused by problems with the ovaries, uterus, fallopian tubes or endocrine system, according to the WHO.
Because women are born with all of the eggs they will ever produce, researchers suspect that pesticide exposure before birth may matter more than in adulthood. Pesticide exposure during pregnancy has also been linked to poorer brain development in their developing foetuses.
Pregnancy is “a very fundamental timing of our life when we can effectively reduce the exposure to several chemicals in order to protect the health of the offspring, even the reproductive health,” said Dr. Rossella Cannarella, an endocrinology specialist at the same university.
EU policymakers are now considering new legislation to simplify food and feed safety rules, including the approval of pesticides. The proposal aims to cut bureaucratic red tape for farmers and national authorities, according to the European Commission, the EU’s executive arm.
But health groups and lobbying watchdogs say the plan is too industry-friendly and that policymakers are pushing the rules through without thoroughly studying their potential impact.
HEAL has called on the Commission to withdraw the proposal and reduce the EU’s dependency on pesticides. The EU had aimed to cut its chemical pesticide use in half by 2030 under its Green Deal, but withdrew a proposal that would have banned them in sensitive areas after protests by farmers in 2024.
The risk that pesticide exposure could harm fertility “stresses the importance of trying to keep achieving these objectives,” said Sandra Jen, who leads HEAL’s health and chemicals programme.
Long-term exposure to certain pesticides has also been linked to some cancers, Parkinson’s disease, respiratory issues and other health problems.
The WHO recently said that hormone-disrupting chemicals found in everyday products pose a risk to human health. These chemicals are present in many plastic items, pharmaceuticals, toys, household goods, personal care products, electronics and pesticides.
