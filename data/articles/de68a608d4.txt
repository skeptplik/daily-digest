Botox loyalists claim something is amiss with their favorite injectable. The neurotoxin, the go-to treatment to reduce the appearance of fine lines, is supposed to last three to four months. But some are claiming online that their Botox isn’t working as well—at least not as well as it used to.
From accusations that providers are diluting solutions to encourage more frequent treatments to claims that people have become immune to Botox altogether, the theories can get downright conspiratorial. But before anyone freaks out and cancels their next appointment, read what the experts have to say on what’s really going on with Botox.
Is Botox Actually Becoming Less Effective?
The good news is that Botox itself hasn’t suddenly stopped working. As New York–based board-certified dermatologist Hadley King, MD, explains, Botox arrives at medical practices as a freeze-dried powder that must be reconstituted with sterile saline before injection. Although the FDA-approved labeling specifies a recommended concentration, some injectors use different dilution volumes depending on their technique and treatment goals.
King says it’s more important to pay attention to the number of units you’re receiving than the saline concentration, since dosage is a major factor in how long results last. “Twenty units is still 20 units whether it’s delivered in a smaller or larger volume,” she says.
Why Your Botox Might Not Be Lasting
In reality, there are many reasons your Botox isn’t lasting as long as you’d like. Some common ones include:
King says underdosing is one of the biggest reasons. “Many people are requesting a softer, more natural look (sometimes called baby Botox), which intentionally uses fewer units, [and] less product generally means a shorter duration of effect,” she explains.
Brian J. Abittan, MD, director of skin and hair rejuvenation and hair transplantation at the Kimberly and Eric J. Waldman Department of Dermatology at Mount Sinai, adds that patients want more movement in their faces and less of a “frozen” look, which can lead to lighter doses that wear off faster.
King explains that people with stronger facial muscles or particularly expressive faces may need higher doses to achieve their desired results. If the dose is insufficient for the muscles being treated, the effects may appear to wear off sooner.
How someone responds to Botox varies from person to person. King says some patients may experience Botox lasting three to four months, while others may get only two to three months despite the same dose and treatment.
“Precise placement matters,” says King. “Even with the correct dose, injections that don’t optimally target the muscle can reduce the duration of effect.” Poorly placed injections may also make the treatment seem less effective from the outset, rather than causing otherwise successful results to wear off sooner.
The type of practice where you get your Botox done also matters. Choosing an appropriately trained, licensed medical professional, such as a board-certified dermatologist or plastic surgeon, substantially reduces the risk of counterfeit products and improper injection techniques. “Those situations are uncommon in reputable medical practices,” she says. “But [they] highlight why it’s important to seek treatment from qualified providers.”
King says some patients wonder whether frequent intense exercise, high muscle activity, or a rapid metabolism might explain why their Botox seems to wear off sooner. However, there is little scientific evidence to suggest that these factors meaningfully shorten its effects.
Generally, Botox Doesn’t Stop Working the More You Get It
For most patients, King says they don’t become tolerant to Botox. In fact, she says many long-term patients may find they can maintain results with similar (or even slightly fewer) units because repeatedly relaxing the muscle may reduce its strength over time. She also points out that first-time patients might notice movement returning earlier than patients who have been getting treatment consistently over time.
She says a small subset of patients develops neutralizing antibodies against Botox, which can make treatments less effective. Although higher doses and frequent injections have been suggested as potential risk factors, true resistance is rare, particularly among cosmetic patients. “An experienced injector will first rule out these more common explanations,” she says.
How Botox Compares With Other Treatments
Many people are already experiencing Botox and filler fatigue and are looking to other procedures (XERF, facelifts, etc.) to address other signs of aging. Abittan says it’s important to pay attention to what each treatment actually does, since each addresses different issues. Lasers, for example, can help with skin tightening, improve pigmentation or texture, and address wrinkles or lines at rest. Neuromodulators like Botox, by contrast, work on movement-related wrinkles, he says.
“These treatments are complementary rather than competitive,” adds King. “The modern approach to facial rejuvenation is increasingly individualized and multimodal. Rather than replacing Botox, newer technologies give physicians more tools to address the specific cause of each patient’s aging concerns.”
If you’re still unhappy with how long your Botox lasts, consider discussing a different neuromodulator with your provider. Dysport, Xeomin, Jeuveau, and Letybo are alternatives that generally offer a similar duration of effect, while Daxxify has demonstrated longer-lasting results in clinical trials, with effects lasting a median of approximately six months. As always, consult a qualified medical professional before trying a new injectable.
