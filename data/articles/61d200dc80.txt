What will it take for the world's superpowers to agree on AI regulations in war?
Diplomats to discuss autonomous weapons at a UN meeting this week, but experts say binding rules are unlikely
As the UN prepares to discuss autonomous weapons this week, insiders say hopes of meaningful, binding regulations around AI in warfare are slim — and time may be running out.
Diplomats will discuss lethal autonomous weapons systems, including a possible framework for regulations, at a Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons meeting running Monday to Friday in Geneva, Switzerland. More than 100 countries that are party to the convention will be represented, including Canada, the U.S., Russia and China.
UN Secretary-General António Guterres warned in August that the world was nearing a "moral red line" where autonomous weapons like AI-powered drones could kill without human involvement. He's said he wants a legally binding treaty on autonomous weapons by the end of 2026 as AI increasingly becomes a factor in deadly strikes.
Meanwhile, some experts say the use of AI to determine where and when to strike in the first place is an even more pressing concern than the weapons themselves.
But so far, the world's biggest military superpowers — including the U.S., Russia and China — have shot down any serious attempts at binding rules.

Leaders are afraid of losing their competitive advantage because of the idea that "whoever wins the AI race will dominate the world," said Paul Lushenko, a senior fellow at Cornell University Tech Policy Institute and a retiring U.S. military officer.
He says everyone he speaks to in the military recognizes the moral implications of AI. At the same time, the prevailing consensus among the most powerful militaries is that even if they follow a set of rules, they can't trust their competitors to do the same, creating a Cold War-esque stalemate.
"That's a really challenging position to be in," he said.
Paul Lushenko, senior fellow at Cornell University Tech Policy Institute, says the world's military superpowers worry that if they follow regulations around AI, their competitors won't do the same. (Lisa Maco/Cornell University)
Lushenko said he does not expect any kind of "bona fide international policy" or regulation to come out of this week's meetings.
About one-third of the countries that attended a February military AI summit in Spain, including Canada, agreed to a declaration on how to govern use of the technology in warfare, but the U.S. and China were among those that opted out.
UN diplomats were negotiating a treaty in September to regulate the use of lethal autonomous weapons when U.S. and Russian diplomats cut out key safety provisions on the final day.
According to reporting by the Washington Post, those provisions included language that would have required systems to operate in a predictable and reliable manner, mandated ethical considerations and required that humans review military targets picked by AI before striking.
AI in action
Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy, addressing the UN in September, warned there is a "real possibility" that AI will soon begin to decide what happens on the battlefield.
"We need peace before we reach that point," he said.
Ukraine and Russia are both increasingly using AI to gain advantages in battle as the war between the two countries approaches its fifth year. In August, the New York Times reported that a Russian drone operated entirely by AI killed three Ukrainian civilians.

In one of the most prominent examples of AI-assisted targeting, Israeli intelligence officers described to Israeli-Palestinian publication +972 Magazine and the Hebrew-language outlet Local Call how the military's Lavender AI system allegedly identified 37,000 targets in Gaza, with one saying soldiers had about 20 seconds to review each target before they carried out a strike.
The U.S. has since made a major push to ramp up AI use, with the Pentagon declaring the country's military an "AI-first fighting force."
The U.S. used Anthropic's Claude chatbot to help identify nearly 1,000 targets in the first 24 hours of its war with Iran in February. After a U.S. missile strike on a girls' school in Minab, American senators raised concerns over the use of AI tools, including Palantir's Maven Smart System, in target selection.
Those concerns did not deter the military. In June, Cameron Stanley, chief digital and artificial intelligence officer for the department of defence, said Elon Musk's Grok chatbot had been used to launch thousands of missiles at Iran.
The frequency of AI-assisted targeting in Gaza and Iran has raised concerns from experts who worry that even though a human technically may have the final say before strikes are made, that human could effectively serve as a rubber stamp.
'Dystopian future'
Craig Martin, a former Canadian navy officer and an expert in armed conflict laws who teaches at Washburn University in Topeka, Kan, also worries about automation bias, whereby humans tend to become overly confident in AI's accuracy and validity.
"This may have been the case in the ... use of Lavender in Gaza, similarly the incident of the strike on the school in Minab by the United States," he told CBC News.
Craig Martin, a former Canadian navy officer and an expert in armed conflict laws, would like to see a ban on fully autonomous weapons. (Andrew Lee/CBC)
Martin is calling for changes to international law that would ban fully autonomous weapons and restrict semi-autonomous weapons.
He says he has hope for some form of agreement on AI, but it "would be a mistake to say that one is extremely optimistic," given that international law is in "a bit of a crisis right now."
"My greatest fear would be that we miss this window of opportunity and we increasingly develop weapons that are governed by AI over which we have little control and often little understanding and no legal regime that's governing those weapon systems," he said.
"It could lead to some kind of dystopian future."

Lushenko says his concerns extend to nuclear weapons.
While noting that AI offers the possibility of built-in deterrents and has potential to reduce human error and civilian casualties, he says war games conducted in research programs have found AI chatbots tend to rapidly escalate to suggesting the use of nukes in evolving crises between powerful countries.
Fears of escalation
The fear among major powers of being left behind is driving a "race to the bottom," said Branka Marijan, senior fellow at the Centre for International Governance Innovation and senior researcher with anti-war organization Project Ploughshares.
"Without those guardrails we really, I think, are in a very dangerous moment," she said.
Branka Marijan, senior fellow at the Centre for International Governance Innovation and senior researcher with Project Ploughshares, says the world is in a 'dangerous moment' without guardrails around AI in war. (Submitted by Branka Marijan)
Marijan says what keeps her up at night is not necessarily the sheer destruction that could be caused by autonomous weapons or by AI selecting targets in the midst of an ongoing war, but mistakes that could cause an international incident to escalate into a full-blown crisis.
CNN reported in September that the U.S. military almost boarded a Chinese vessel in the Middle East because an AI chatbot mistakenly said the ship was carrying nuclear materials.
Marijan says incidents like that "are likely to keep occurring" and next time, the military might not put the brakes on soon enough.
"You could have a system that completely influences a state actor to act in a way that might be based on faulty information ... that results in some catastrophic mistake."
