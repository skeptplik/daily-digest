The European Central Bank compared the AI boom with the dotcom bubble in its biannual Financial Stability Review on Wednesday, warning that a surge in stock market listings may point to late-cycle exuberance while tech giants borrow heavily, leaving markets vulnerable to a sudden sell-off.
Risks to the eurozone's financial stability "remain elevated, given persistent geoeconomic uncertainty and growing exposure to AI," said ECB Vice-President Boris Vujčić, who had sounded a similar warning on Monday.
The report comes a day after the European Commission said the EU economy had weathered the Iran war's energy shock better than expected. The ECB agrees, but warns that the AI optimism helping the economy has itself become a risk.
Echoes of the dotcom era
Share prices rest on "extraordinarily strong earnings expectations" for AI firms that may prove hard to meet, the report said, warning that cheaper AI models could undercut today's market leaders.
With AI spending now outstripping the cash their businesses generate, US tech giants are borrowing at a record pace, increasingly in euros, leaving European investors more exposed.
The ECB estimates that big tech firms had more than $4 trillion (€3.63tn) in off-balance-sheet commitments by the end of the second quarter of 2026, debt-like obligations that are not recorded as liabilities, while chipmakers often finance the customers buying their chips.
The central bank stopped short of calling the AI boom a bubble, but said such a rapid shift to debt invites comparisons with both the dotcom and subprime mortgage bubbles of the early 2000s.
Meanwhile, the amount raised by US IPOs is on track to beat their dotcom-era high relative to the size of the economy, and similar booms have tended to come near market peaks, the ECB said.
ECB staff estimate they will raise around $212 billion (€192bn) this year, up from about $44bn (€40bn) in 2025, according to the report.
SpaceX's record IPO in June led the way. Anthropic, the maker of the Claude AI models, is expected to set its IPO price on Wednesday and begin trading on the Nasdaq on Thursday.
Big listings can shift "unquantified risk" to less informed buyers, including retail investors, the ECB warned.
Government debt under strain
Shaky public finances in big advanced economies could also trigger a global repricing of government debt, the ECB warned. Long-term borrowing costs have hit their highest levels in more than two decades in several countries, and many eurozone governments are paying the most to borrow since the 2012 debt crisis.
Eurozone governments are issuing the most debt since the pandemic, driven mainly by Germany's defence and infrastructure spending.
With the ECB no longer buying government bonds under its stimulus programmes, more of it is going to foreign investors and hedge funds, which tend to be flightier in a crisis and could amplify any sell-off.
France stands out, with investors demanding more to cover political risk before the 2027 elections. Its 10-year borrowing costs topped 5% in October for the first time since 2002 and now stand at 4.85%.
On Tuesday, the Commission forecast EU public debt rising from 82.6% of GDP last year to 85.6% in 2028, with interest payments swallowing 2.4% of economic output by then.
"Fiscal consolidation is overdue," the ECB stated.
On the other hand, banks are in good shape to absorb shocks, the report said, with profitability at its highest in a decade and non-performing loans at a record low of 2.2% of the total in September.
Vulnerabilities are greater at some non-banks, such as investment funds and hedge funds, where safeguards are less developed. US tech and AI-related firms now make up between 16% and 26% of eurozone non-banks' equity investments.
The ECB pointed to the near-collapse in July of Situational Awareness, a US hedge fund whose assets under management shrank from around $45bn (€40.9bn) to $10bn (€9bn) after its leveraged AI bets went wrong.
Such stress could spread to banks, which get almost a fifth of their funding from non-banks. The ECB called for stronger safeguards against leverage and liquidity risks in the sector, while urging authorities to keep banks' capital buffers in place.
Higher energy prices could also leave households and companies struggling with repayments, and bad loans can take years to build up in the aftermath of an energy shock, the ECB warned.
The race to succeed Christine Lagarde
The report also lands as governments close in on a deal over the ECB's leadership.
Germany's finance ministry said on Tuesday it was seeking the chief economist job for Princeton University professor Markus Brunnermeier, with Berlin reportedly backing former Bank of Spain governor Pablo Hernández de Cos to replace Christine Lagarde as president in exchange.
The developments further indicate that France and Germany are nearing a deal to back the Spanish candidate, as Euronews reported last Friday.
