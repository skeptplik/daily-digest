UN talks on AI in warfare end with no commitment to binding rules
Most countries supported working towards treaty, but some major military powers resisted
UN-backed talks this week on regulating AI in warfare ended Saturday with nations agreeing to further discussions, but without a consensus on developing firm rules on how to rein in deadly autonomous weapons.
Proponents for regulations including UN Secretary General António Guterres had called for a legally binding treaty within a short time frame on AI weapons like drones that could identify and kill without human involvement.
The discussions ran Monday to Friday at a Convention on Certain Conventional Weapons meeting in Geneva. More than 100 countries that are party to the convention, including Canada, were expected to take part.
Though most countries supported working towards the treaty, some military powers like the U.S. and Russia resisted committing to binding rules that would limit them in developing such high-tech weapons. That resulted in a softened agreement to "elaborate" a possible accord on such lethal autonomous weapons systems by 2028.
Izumi Nakamitsu, the UN high representative for disarmament affairs, hailed a "significant achievement" but acknowledged a "glass half full or half empty" scenario, citing Guterres's hopes for a legally binding deal this year.
However, some advocacy groups bemoaned the blocking of stronger language at a time when AI is increasingly becoming a factor in deadly strikes.
"States have agreed it is sufficient to just talk about the issue, for the 14th year, while these weapons enable atrocities," said Max Tegmark, a machine learning specialist at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology and founder of the Future of Life Institute.
Superpowers resist binding rules
The outcome is disappointing but expected, said Branka Marijan, senior fellow at the Centre for International Governance Innovation and senior researcher with anti-war organization Project Ploughshares.
Marijan says military superpowers like the U.S. and Russia have resisted legally binding rules.
"There is a real issue here of political will. There's just no political will among the major militaries to have legally binding instruments and hard laws," she said.
AI is being used increasingly in conflicts around the world to gain advantage in battle.
Both Russia and Ukraine are using AI to track targets and plan attacks in the war entering its fifth year. In Ukraine, there are reports that autonomous drones operated by Russia have killed civilians, while Ukraine has also reportedly tested such weapons.
The U.S. has made a major push to increase its use of AI in combat, and is testing advanced fighter drones that fly themselves.
The need for regulation has become more urgent as the world sees this "incredibly dangerous move" towards the development and deployment of increasingly capable autonomous weapons systems — without any guardrails, Marijan said.
"I think there's extreme scenarios, but there are very immediate risks and concerns if you think about the fact that each country could deploy these systems now and there are no real constraints," she said.
CBC News reached out to Global Affairs Canada and the Department of National Defence for comment.

In a statement sent to CBC News earlier this month, the Department of National Defence said the Canadian Armed Forces supports the development of autonomous functionality on the condition that it would be "operated within a responsible chain of human command and control and commits to uphold the reliable and lawful use of such weapon systems."
Canada was among the countries that agreed to a declaration on how to govern use of artificial intelligence in warfare at a military AI summit in Spain in February.
In 2025, Canada also supported a UN resolution calling on countries to lay the groundwork for possible negotiations on a treaty governing the use of lethal autonomous weapons systems.
The UN's Nakamitsu called for countries to begin work towards a deal as early as Monday and "start thinking about how to get as strong as possible [an] instrument to prohibit and regulate lethal autonomous weapon systems."
