Home Office says more offenders to be tagged before they leave prison
Thousands more offenders will be tagged before leaving prison in a bid to tighten supervision of those released, the Press Association reports. PA says:
The move expands on a pilot scheme to fit eligible offenders with GPS, alcohol and curfew tags before they leave the prison gates, rather than sending them off with a tagging order in place.
It comes as further waves of offenders are set to be released early over the next year to free up prison spaces in overcrowded jails in England and Wales.
Six prisons started fitting tags before departure last year under the “tagging at source” scheme, and more will follow suit to bring the number to 83 by the end of March.
Criminals and defendants should be under no illusion that leaving prison or court means escaping the watchful eye of the authorities.
Tagging is just one of the many tools we use to monitor offenders, but the evidence shows it works.
By fitting tags before they even walk through the prison gates or out of court, we’re tightening the net around those who break the law and making sure there is nowhere to hide from the consequences of their actions.

Burnham urged to step in to halt energy bill hikes in January, as Ofgem says price cap to rise 26%
Millions of British households will face annual energy bills of more than £2,000 a year for the first time since Russia’s invasion of Ukraine, as the cap on energy bills climbs for a third straight quarter, Jillian Ambrose reports.
Commenting on the increase for the Conservatives, Andrew Bowie, the shadow energy secretary, said:
It is an outrage that, as bills soar, Labour are not only piling green levies and taxes on to bills but signing us up to an EU scheme that risks pushing them even higher.
Labour are utterly out of touch and could fix this tomorrow if they had the backbone.
And Ed Davey, the Lib Dem leader, said:
Right now, millions of families feel completely powerless in the face of reckless wars driving up energy prices, and they are being left to fend for themselves while bills spiral out of control.
The meagre and poorly targeted support the government has promised will be wiped out by this rise, and many of those who most desperately need help won’t get a penny. The government needs to wake up to the scale of this crisis.
The prime minister must step in and stop such a massive hike going ahead in January. The Treasury and the energy network companies are both raking in huge windfalls because of this crisis - that cash should be used to keep bills down for struggling households, not left sitting in Treasury coffers and shareholders’ pockets.
Burnham’s devolution plans risk ‘significant disparities’ for people in England, MPs say
Good morning. Kemi Badenoch will be choosing what to raise at PMQs this morning and she’s spoilt for choice.
It would be very surprising if she did not raise the most important story of the morning – the news that gas and electricity prices will rise by 26% from January under Ofgem’s latest price cap.
And, as someone who is herself over-focused on social media, it is likely that Badenoch will also want to raise the row about TikTok influencers in Downing Street. It is a story about preposterous media figures with no respect for the political establishment; but that’s enough about tabloid editors for now.
One thing unlikely to come up is the public accounts committee report out today on devolving power in England. So let’s give it some coverage anyway. Burnham has many political ambitions, but the one idea that seems to matter to him more than any other, and that marks him out from his predecessors, is his desire to devolve power out of Westminster, to let mayors and other local leaders have more clout and – via this devolution – to promote growth “in every postcode”.
But, given the way the policy is being implemented, this may not happen, says the committee, which has a Labour majority. It says:
There is a risk of significant disparities in opportunities and outcomes for people living in different parts of England, depending on what type of strategic authority they have.
The government has consciously adopted a differentiated approach to devolution. Areas that choose an elected mayor to lead their strategic authority (‘mayoral strategic authorities’) can access a broader range of devolved powers and funding flexibilities to improve local outcomes than those without a mayor.
However, even among areas with a mayor, powers and arrangements differ. Only seven of England’s 18 mayoral strategic authorities receive an integrated settlement – a single funding pot for each area, collectively worth around £15.9bn up to 2029–30, that provides greater funding flexibilities and multi-year funding certainty.
London also differs from other mayoral areas. Its constituent boroughs have no formal role in the Greater London Authority’s decisions or vote on its budget. This risks decisions being taken without formal input from the boroughs that are responsible for delivering local services, and may make lines of accountability less clear for local people.
Fiscal devolution could further deepen differences between areas. The government’s October 2026 Fiscal Devolution Roadmap confirms that only mayoral strategic authorities will be able to retain a share of local income tax. It proposes ways to limit disparities between different mayoral strategic authorities, but important details are still to be determined, including through the next spending review, and it therefore remains uncertain how effective these arrangements will be.
