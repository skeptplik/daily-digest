Paul Hamilton is the founder of Halo Service Solutions, a UK software company that has built its workforce around graduates, degree apprentices and other early-career talent. Here, he argues why businesses need to stop competing for the same pool of experienced candidates and start investing in developing the next generation.
I didn’t start growing the business with a theory about early-career talent. Fifteen years ago, working from my mother-in-law’s spare bedroom, I needed to make my first hire and could not compete with established companies for someone with a long track record. Recruiting a graduate was initially a practical decision, but it changed my view of what good hiring looks like. I saw that ability and experience were not the same, and people could progress much faster when an employer gave them real work and clear expectations.
Businesses: more a sports team than a family
I’m a huge fan of team sports, which probably explains why I think of a business more as a sports team than a family. A strong team gives people a sense of belonging without lowering its standards or losing its competitive edge. Experienced players help prepare the next generation because they know the whole team benefits when new talent comes through, and I think the same should be true in business.
Leaders need to know when to step in, but doing something faster themselves is not always a good reason to take over. Sometimes the better investment is letting someone else learn, despite mistakes having to be accounted for as part of individual growth.
Sports clubs understand a truth that many businesses seem to have forgotten: a strong first team cannot rely solely on buying established players. You need an academy that spots potential early, gives people time to learn and creates a clear route into senior roles.
Viewing the development of talents as part of running the business
Yet businesses across Europe continue to complain about skills shortages while competing for the same small pool of experienced candidates. They pay a premium to move proven people from one company to another, attach years of experience to supposedly entry-level jobs and then wonder why the supply of talent never grows.
This is the thinking behind Cantera, the name I use for our graduate, scholarship, degree-apprenticeship and college-leaver programmes. In Spanish football, the cantera is the system through which a club finds, develops and promotes young players. The word literally means “quarry”: a place where something valuable is found and carefully developed.
Athletic Bilbao is a prime example of a club that puts its academy at the heart of how it competes. Its commitment to fielding players from the Basque region means it must maintain high standards throughout its youth system.
Players who come through the academy learn the club’s standards, methods and identity long before they reach the first team. For employers, the lesson is not to copy a football club’s recruitment rules. It is to treat developing talent as part of running the business, rather than something separate from it.
Younger team members need work they can grow into
Gianmarco Rubino’s career shows that this route exists. He joined our Graduate Scheme in 2020, moved from Software Consultant to Product Manager in 2022 and became a director in 2023. His experience of the product, the industry and working directly with customers was part of that journey, alongside high levels of aptitude and the right attitude.
For new starters, seeing someone take that route successfully makes the opportunity and their own growth path tangible. It shows that responsibility is not reserved for people who arrive with a senior title or a decade of experience.
Too often, young employees must spend years proving themselves before doors are opened to them. Customers and colleagues may instinctively place more trust in an older voice, including in the software industry. Employers cannot remove every external assumption, but they can choose not to reinforce it.
Younger team members need a clear remit, visible backing from senior leaders and opportunities to lead meetings, answer difficult questions and own the outcome. That does not mean leaving them to sink or swim. It means giving them work they can grow into, with someone ready to help when needed and develop their confidence as well as their understanding. We see trust grow when newer employees are given meaningful responsibility early.
Funding can support recruitment; it cannot replace development.
The October 2026 UK Budget gives employers more help to build younger workforces, but it does not remove that responsibility. It confirmed £100 million (Editor’s note: approximately 120 million Euro) to extend mayoral apprenticeship brokerage across England, along with a £2,000 (Editor’s note: approximately 2,400 Euro) payment for eligible young apprentices hired by non-levy-paying employers and a £3,000 (Editor’s note: approximately 3,600 Euro) Youth Jobs Grant for businesses taking on eligible unemployed young people.
These measures should make it easier, especially for smaller firms, to open the door. What happens after someone walks through it still depends on the employer: the quality of the coaching, the usefulness of the work and whether there is a real route to progression. Funding can support recruitment; it cannot replace development.
AI changes the work through which new starters learn
The same responsibility applies as AI changes the work through which new starters learn. The answer is not to preserve every repetitive task for its own sake. It is to redesign early-career learning so that AI becomes part of the academy rather than a substitute for it. New starters should use AI, as ignoring the productivity increases it provides would be naive, but they must also learn to question its output and explain why an answer is right or wrong. Used well, AI can speed up learning, but used carelessly, it can create false confidence without depth.
A credible academy model has to account for hires that do not work out. It also needs to give experienced colleagues time to coach and check whether people are progressing. The test is not simply how many young people are recruited, but whether they become capable of taking on greater responsibility.
Our retention continues to sit well above 90% for employees who have been with us for over two years, which we are proud of, while recognising that this approach will not work in every case. The alternative, however, carries costs of its own: repeated recruitment fees, rising salaries for scarce experience, longer vacancies and a business that remains dependent on the external market.
Start deciding how you will help build the talent you need
By the end of 2030, we will have a team of over 1,000 in the UK, all recruited as graduates, degree apprentices or through other youth schemes. People often tell me how unusual that is. I am proud of the opportunities we can offer, but I do not want this approach to remain unusual. I want other businesses to see a model they can adapt, not an exception they can admire from a distance. This is how we intend to keep building the business as we grow.
We do not have all the answers. Developing people takes patience and a willingness to let people learn, and I understand that time can be one of the commodities businesses value most. But competing for the same experienced candidates will not give Europe a larger pool of talent.
More employers must help create it and be rewarded by retaining a large percentage of the best talent from the next generation. The question is not whether to value experience, it is whether we are willing to give people the chance to gain it. Stop asking only where you can buy the talent you need. Start deciding how you will help build it.
Paul Hamilton is the founder of UK-based software company Halo. Paul has built a successful technology business without external investment, taking an unconventional approach to recruitment and talent development. His team has an average age of 24.5, with 93% of employees joining without any previous experience in the software industry.
Halo is specialising in service management software, helping organisations streamline operations, manage IT services and improve customer support. Halo works with major organisations across a range of sectors, as well as professional football clubs including Ipswich Town, where it is the headline partner and shirt sponsor.
