No plans to reinstate CEO Jensen Huang, Nvidia chair tells investors
Huang had said he'd like to return to the tech giant after resigning over affair with Chinese businesswoman
Nvidia board chair Harvey Jones opened an investor conference call on Tuesday by saying the AI chip company would not rehire co-founder Jensen Huang as CEO and would not take questions on the matter.
Jones's start to a call about quarterly results was a forceful response to a movement to bring back Huang, who stepped down last week. With Huang gone, investors are eager for clues about Nvidia's future and strategy, from its commitments of billions of dollars to cloud providers, suppliers and AI startups to who will ultimately lead the world's most valuable chipmaker.
Shares of the company rose 4.5 per cent in extended trading, after Nvidia forecast sales ahead of Wall Street targets. They were down about 17 per cent for the year through Tuesday's close, valuing the company at roughly $3.8 trillion US.
"It's no surprise that they're doing very well, and there'll be people who will breathe a sigh of relief about it," said Rick Meckler, partner at Cherry Lane Investments. "But the story here is very complicated, and it's hard not to factor that in as an additional risk for people buying stock."
Jones said the board had hired executive search firm Heidrick & Struggles to help find a new CEO.
Interim CEO Colette Kress, who is also finance chief, defended her team's work since Huang's departure. "Our leadership has stood up so strongly over the last week," she said. "We've got this."
Leadership from Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Co, which makes chips for Nvidia, was in the office and "fully engaged in helping us," she said. In prepared remarks she added that the company's latest flagship AI chip, called Vera Rubin, was off to an explosive start.
Huang suggested he'd like to return to Nvidia
Huang, the AI boom's most prolific salesman, resigned last Thursday after reports of extramarital affairs, including one with Chinese businesswoman Alice Huang.
U.S. intelligence agencies are trying to determine the extent of Alice Huang's ties to China's Ministry of State Security and what information she may have passed to Beijing, according to two people familiar with the matter.
Jensen Huang's exit ended three decades of founder-led control over Nvidia's chip roadmap, customer deals and Washington ties, making execution a bigger risk than near-term AI demand, analysts say.
The company expects fourth-quarter revenue of $130 billion US, plus or minus three per cent, above analysts' average estimate of $125.25 billion US, according to LSEG data. Nvidia said the outlook assumes no data-centre chip sales to China.

Anshel Sag, principal analyst at Moor Insights & Strategy, said Nvidia's outlook was "quite positive, albeit not as ambitious as in previous quarters, which many have already anticipated."
"I don't think anything near term will be affected by Jensen's departure," he said. "His executive leadership team is capable and has followed his leadership and executed effectively for years, reaching this point."
The board is considering internal and external candidates to replace Huang as CEO. But a growing "Stand With Jensen" campaign has complicated the search, and Huang said on Tuesday he would be "honoured" to return if the board deemed it best.
Amazon executive chair Jeff Bezos, speaking at the Semafor conference in Silicon Valley on Tuesday, said the Nvidia board should keep its focus within the company. "If you don't bring Jensen back, I would not hire an outsider. Nvidia is too complicated," he said.

National security scrutiny
Earlier on Tuesday, Nvidia's board created a committee on national security and export controls, its first major governance move since Huang's departure, as scrutiny grows over the chipmaker's national security role.
Washington has also tightened licensing rules for chip shipments to Southeast Asia and overseas data centres serving Chinese firms, while lawmakers prepare to question Huang, who had pushed for greater chip exports to China.
Investors were already uneasy about AI spending after Democrats' midterm sweep fuelled fears of tougher scrutiny and slower data-centre expansion. They also want to know whether Nvidia's next CEO will continue financing customers, a practice that critics have called circular.
Tuesday's results offer a first read on Vera Rubin, Nvidia's newest generation of AI processors and successor to Grace Blackwell. Nvidia said in August that Vera Rubin would account for about a fifth of data-center revenue in the October quarter and forecast 70 per cent revenue growth in the next fiscal year.
