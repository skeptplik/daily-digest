Good morning. Leo Cendrowicz here, bringing you the Friday newsletter. And on today’s menu: UK PM Andy Burnham is in Brussels for an EU-UK summit, while we digest the Coalition of the Willing meeting and the Xi-Trump-Putin summit.
Britain's back. Well, at least for one day. Andy Burnham is in Brussels today for his first visit since becoming UK Prime Minister in July, bidding to reset relations more than a decade after the Brexit referendum.
And what a difference a few months make. Burnham has declared that Brexit hasn't worked, floated the possibility of Britain rejoining the EU and promised a national conversation about the country's European future. Words that would have been political heresy under his predecessor, Keir Starmer.
But first, the small print. Today's second EU-UK summit brings Burnham together with European Commission President Ursula von der Leyen and European Council President António Costa, with a package of agreements designed to repair some of Brexit's economic damage.
The headline deliverables have been trailed for months: a deal to slash paperwork and border checks on food and agricultural products, another to reconnect the two sides' carbon emissions trading systems, and a youth mobility scheme allowing up to 100,000 young people from each side to live, work and study across the Channel.
There's also closer cooperation on migration, including a proposed Channel action plan, and promises to work together on security, technology and energy.
Sounds straightforward? This is Brexit we're talking about. Negotiations went down to the wire yesterday, with EU ambassadors called into a special meeting to settle disputes that threatened to overshadow today's diplomatic love-in.
One particularly thorny question was whether British officials could help shape EU regulations that London would subsequently have to follow. France initially dug in against granting Britain special treatment, before a late compromise appeared to give UK officials a consultative role, but no vote.
Then came university tuition fees. Brussels wanted EU students to pay the same as British students, while London resisted. An emerging compromise involves discounted fees, with the details to be settled over the coming months.
And the elephant in the room? Britain's demand to be included in the EU's forthcoming Made in Europe industrial programme, protecting manufacturers from foreign competition. Brussels is broadly sympathetic, but wants London to align with EU trade policies, notably towards Chinese electric vehicles. No definitive deal on that today.
The bigger picture: Burnham sees today's agreements as a first step towards something much more ambitious. He has suggested that Britain could eventually rejoin the EU, although he has postponed setting out the options until after further discussions with Brussels.
And Brussels? It welcomes the warmer language, but isn't exactly throwing itself into Britain's arms. Memories of Brexit remain fresh, and EU governments want to know whether London is serious about a lasting rapprochement.
So today's summit is less a remarriage than a cautious second date. The chemistry may be improving, but nobody is moving back in just yet.
On Europe Today later this morning, we'll be speaking to Nigel Farage, leader of Reform UK and still the loudest anti-EU voice in British politics, as well as Peter Wilding, founder of British Influence and the man who coined the term Brexit.
Global Europe
Three men in a room, and not much to show for it. After all the fanfare, the historic meeting between Donald Trump, Vladimir Putin and Xi Jinping in Shenzhen produced plenty of photographs, a few warm words and precious little substance.
The three leaders emerged with a grandly titled "peace declaration", pledging cooperation on nuclear risks, UN reform and oil supplies. Conspicuously absent were the two wars dominating international diplomacy: Ukraine and Iran.
So who won? Trump got his moment on the world stage after a bruising midterm election defeat. Putin got to reinforce his claim to great-power status. But the real winner looks like Xi, who played host and presented China as the indispensable convenor of global diplomacy.
As for Europe, it wasn't invited. The spectacle revived uncomfortable memories of the 1945 Yalta Conference between Roosevelt, Stalin and Churchill, and fears that the big powers might carve up the world over smaller countries' heads.
But there was no grand bargain, no breakthrough on Ukraine and little sign the three agree on much beyond the value of a good photo op.
Forget Yalta: European leaders were having none of it. Just hours after the Shenzhen summit, they rejected any attempt to carve up the continent into spheres of influence at a Coalition of the Willing meeting in Berlin, Jorge Liboreiro writes.
"A Yalta-style format will not bring peace," German Chancellor Friedrich Merz declared. "At Yalta, Europe was divided up. It was parcelled up. And we won't allow anything like that to happen again."
To drive the point home, Merz gifted the more than 40 delegations pieces of the Berlin Wall, a reminder of what Europe's division meant.
French President Emmanuel Macron dismissed comparisons with Yalta, insisting that Europeans had decided to determine their own fate. He also urged NATO to reopen the question of Ukraine's membership to counter Russia's demands to redraw Europe's security architecture.
Wait… what about Russia? Ukraine's NATO membership would be a major step towards the security guarantees Kyiv wants. There's just one rather important catch: Moscow.
Putin reiterated his opposition to military cooperation between Ukraine and Europe, despite US envoy Jared Kushner's suggestion that Russia might accept NATO-style security guarantees for Kyiv.
Instead, Putin invited European representatives to Moscow for talks, just as he had previously invited Volodymyr Zelenskyy. The message, it seems, remains the same: dialogue is welcome, provided it happens on Moscow's terms.
In other news
Red carpet, Russian drones: While Putin was meeting Trump and Xi, Russian drones struck Kyiv, damaging the Dutch and Austrian embassies. EU foreign policy chief Kaja Kallas summoned Russia's envoy and pledged greater pressure on Moscow.
Hungary's change of heart: Budapest is expected to lift its veto on Ukraine's final two EU accession clusters today, following Péter Magyar's election victory and an agreement on ethnic Hungarian minority rights. Moldova also stands to benefit, Luca Bertuzzi and Sandor Zsiros report.
Cyberattack on Commission: A major cyberattack disrupted European Commission systems handling salaries, leave and medical reimbursements. Investigators are examining whether sensitive staff data was stolen. The perpetrators remain unidentified.
Methane rules delayed: The EU has agreed to postpone key methane emissions rules by two years, easing pressure on oil and gas importers. Environmental campaigners say the move undermines Europe's climate commitments.
Montenegro's 10-year probation: EU ambassadors have broadly backed a 10-year probation period for new members, starting with Montenegro, allowing voting rights to be suspended for serious rule-of-law breaches or obstruction of EU decisions.
Sánchez contra Feijóo: Spain's election rivals, Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez and conservative Alberto Núñez Feijóo, clashed over corruption, migration and the economy in a bruising televised debate. Polls suggest the November 29 election could bring the far right into government.
Historic genocide ruling: The UN's highest court has found Myanmar guilty of genocide against the Rohingya, a landmark first for the International Court of Justice. The verdict could have implications for South Africa's genocide case against Israel.
Ashes to ashes: EU Health Commissioner Olivér Várhelyi had ashes thrown over him by cancer activists protesting against plans to relax pesticide rules. Four people were detained, while Ursula von der Leyen condemned the stunt as "unacceptable".
EU, Hollywood, opera: Commission Executive Vice-President Teresa Ribera appeared at the European Business Summit gala dinner alongside actress Cate Blanchett and soprano Barbara Hendricks, both UNHCR Goodwill Ambassadors.
