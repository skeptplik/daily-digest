Employers across Europe increasingly value skills while formal qualifications are still significant. Euronews Business takes a close look at which countries are most ready for a skills-first approach with experts explaining the gaps.
The labour market is constantly changing. New jobs are emerging and new skills are required. Degrees still matter in Europe’s job market, but employers are paying more attention to what people can actually do, experts say.
“The currency of the labour market is shifting from traditional credentials alone towards a stronger focus on skills,” OECD’s ‘A Skills‑First Labour Market’ report says.
This is known as a skills-first approach.
“In practice, skills-first hiring is not about replacing degrees altogether, but about giving greater weight to individuals’ demonstrated skills, wherever they were acquired, to improve the matching of talent to jobs,” Glenda Quintini, senior economist at OECD, told Euronews Business.
Dr Soon-Joo Gog, senior director at the Centre for Skills-First Practices at the Singapore University of Social Sciences, emphasised that skills-first does not mean skills only. It is an operating principle. Individuals and employers use demonstrated skills, alongside qualifications.
The OECD’s Skills-First Readiness and Adoption Index measures how ready each country is for this approach.
How does the index work?
The index gives each country a score between 0 and 1. A higher score means a country is better prepared for skills-first practices and uses them more widely.
The index looks at how well countries identify, develop, recognise and reward skills. This includes adult learning, the use of skills data, public support for training and how far employers value what workers can show they are able to do.
Sweden and France lead the way
Sweden has the highest score in Europe at 0.70, followed by France (0.69), the UK (0.65) and Belgium (0.64).
“Countries such as Sweden and France perform strongly overall, although there is no single pathway to building a skills-first labour market,” Quintini said.
“Sweden, for example, stands out for its strong enabling framework, including the use of skills intelligence data and public support for skills development.” she added.
Finland (0.63), Czechia (0.61), Norway (0.61) and Latvia (0.61) also score above the European average.
The average across 23 European countries is 0.58. Spain (0.59), Denmark (0.58), Portugal (0.58), Italy (0.58), Lithuania (0.58), Poland (0.58) and Austria (0.58) are close to this average.
Germany (0.57), Switzerland (0.57) and the Netherlands (0.56) are slightly below it.
Hungary (0.45) and Greece (0.46) have the lowest scores. Turkey (0.52), Slovakia (0.54) and Slovenia (0.54) also score low.
Quintini noted that countries at the lower end of the index often face greater barriers related to adult learning participation and the development of broader learning ecosystems.
“The gap is therefore not simply about education systems, but it reflects how effectively countries identify, develop, recognise and reward skills in the labour market,” she said.
The US scores slightly above the European average at 0.60. Canada (0.55), Japan (0.51) and South Korea (0.48) are below it.
Gog pointed out that most European countries have laid the foundations but the harder step is to make these practices routine.
“That takes time and requires coordinated effort across the system,” she said.
Degrees still matter, but they are not the only signal
Experts agree that degrees remain important. But employers increasingly want to see skills as well.
“Degrees continue to matter in OECD labour markets, particularly because they remain a widely recognised signal of knowledge and capabilities,” Quintini said.
“However, employers are increasingly looking beyond formal qualifications and paying greater attention to the actual skills that workers possess and can demonstrate.”
She said skill shortages are driving this shift. Firms also need to find talent from a wider range of backgrounds, and skill needs are often changing faster than education systems can adapt.
As of 2025, more than 70% of employers across OECD countries report difficulties filling vacancies because of a lack of skilled talent. At the same time, around a third of workers say they are either over-skilled or under-skilled for their current job.
Significance of degrees varies by occupation
“Degrees still matter but their importance varies considerably by occupation and the real requirements are not always made transparent to jobseekers,” Pawel Adrjan, director of economic research at Indeed, told Euronews Business.
Indeed research found that only 14% of UK job postings explicitly mention formal education requirements, compared to 50% in the US.
He underlined that degrees remain important in specialist and highly regulated professions in practice.
“The key distinction is that a degree remains a valuable signal of knowledge and capability but it is not necessarily the only way candidates can demonstrate the skills required to do a job,” he added.
Skills need to evolve rapidly
Anna Toni, senior specialist at the Ministry of Economic Affairs and Employment in Finland, emphasized that stakeholders increasingly recognise that valuable skills are acquired not only through formal education, but also through work experience, volunteering, hobbies and other life experiences. New tools and methods are being developed to identify and validate these skills.
“Labour markets are changing faster than before, driven by factors such as digitalisation, the green transition and AI. Skills need to evolve rapidly, and education and employment policies must adapt accordingly,” she told Euronews Business.
Skills or degrees: Which pays more?
The OECD found that on average, extra education raises wages by about 16%, while better numeracy skills raise them by about 9%, when comparing gaps of similar size.
This finding shows that employers still place substantial value on educational credentials as signals of productivity, especially when information on workers’ skills is limited according to Quintini.
In the OECD index, below 1 means education pays more than skills. France is the only European country above 1 at 1.33. This means a numeracy gap pays about a third more than an education gap of the same typical size.
“This [in France] may reflect a labour market and policy environment that is more conducive to recognising and valuing demonstrated skills,” she said.
Gog warned that the test covers numeracy only, so it misses the technical, social and job-specific skills that many jobs need.
