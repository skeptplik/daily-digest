One can imagine high-fives all around Nvidia’s boardroom after the board reinstated Jensen Huang to the CEO suite. The market gave the move a fat thumbs-up, boosting Nvidia’s share price by about a third. Shareholders, whose interests the board is meant to represent, are surely happy.
But like Fifa’s controversial decision to defer the one-match suspension of US forward Folarin Balogun after receiving a red card for a foul on Bosnia and Herzegovina’s Tarik Muharemović during the World Cup over the summer, Huang’s absolution might backfire.
Like Balogun, it seems Huang was forgiven following a phone call from someone in the White House, maybe even Donald Trump himself, who apparently told Nvidia’s board there was nothing to worry about in the stories about his sleeping around with a potential Chinese spy, as well as a company employee.
But like the US soccer team, which limped on from Trump’s call to Fifa boss Gianni Infantino demanding Balogun be reinstated only to be thumped by Belgium under a cloud of allegations about misuse of presidential power, Huang’s return is unlikely to build Nvidia’s reputation as a paragon of corporate governance.
It’s hard to know what’s what at this stage. There is perhaps a non-zero possibility that Alice Huang was not sleeping with Jensen. Or maybe the relationship was not, in fact, a honey trap to entangle the middle-aged executive in the service of Xi Jinping’s project to secure Nvidia chips.
Maybe, as Trump attested, there is no national security risk to worry about. “One of the things the board wanted to know is: ‘Is there a national security problem?’” he said. “We said: ‘No, it’s not a problem.’”
Unrealistic though this may seem, consider the alternative: the leader of the most important company of the artificial intelligence era was bamboozled by desire into handing over valuable assets to the nation’s main geopolitical rival. And then, the president of the United States – who happens to own a non-trivial number of Nvidia shares – got him off the hook. It’s funny, once you get past terrifying.
And what’s perhaps most terrifying is what Huang’s peccadillos say about power in the AI age. The observant have noted the parallel with the case of Sam Altman – who was fired by the board of OpenAI only to be reinstated within a couple of weeks following a revolt by investors. This has been attributed to Silicon Valley’s fetishistic embrace of company founders as untouchable geniuses who should be left alone to do their will, in the service of profit and progress.
What’s scariest is that this clutch of self-anointed titans – the Huangs and Altmans, the Musks, Amodeis and Zuckerbergs – truly believe that non-tech rubes should get out of the way as they bring about their revolution, one which they admittedly only vaguely understand but which is likely to bring about large-scale unemployment and other massive dislocations to the planet’s 8 billion-plus humans.
It’s hard to find an appropriate parallel. But perhaps the closest precedent came during the Gilded Age, in the late 19th century. The frontier network technology of the time was the railway. The dominant industrial input was steel. The names were also different: Vanderbilt, Carnegie, Rockefeller, Morgan. But the era also suffered from wanton corporate power.
The oligarchs of the time were also untouchable. They not only held absolute sway over their companies, but bought political power as a matter of course, through lobbying, campaign financing and outright bribes. And they benefited handsomely from government largesse. The massive federal subsidies doled out to Elon Musk’s companies have common lineage with the land grants of millions of acres for railroad companies to grow.
Corruption ran rampant. As President Rutherford B Hayes wrote in his diary, echoing Abraham Lincoln’s Gettysburg address: “This is a government of the people, by the people, and for the people no longer. It is a government of corporations, by corporations, and for corporations.” Inequality soared. In 1913, the richest 1% of taxpayers drew almost 18% of the nation’s income and held nearly 40% of the nation’s wealth – similar shares to what they reap and hold today.
The Gilded Age provides us with one reason to believe that the masters of Silicon Valley may be brought to heel: after all, it ended. The Progressive Era that followed in the early 20th century brought about the first federal income tax, antitrust law and a raft of government regulations. Election reforms curbed the power of political machines. A salaried bureaucracy – of the type that Musk’s Doge tried to eviscerate – emerged to reduce bribery. By the late 1970s, the richest 1% hoarded somewhat less than a quarter of the nation’s wealth.
Democrats’ sweep in the midterm elections offers a promise that the rampant corruption of the Trump era may soon end. Huang is already set to testify before Congress over the scandal. Maybe companies and their executives will be held accountable for their actions. Maybe the Trump-era notion that the leaders of exceptional companies like Nvidia should be left to do their will is replaced by a focus on their corporate responsibility.
One thing is certain. Trump, thankfully, is soon to lose the ability to change the world with a phone call.
