AI companies are sitting on “bombshell” revelations that could disrupt a cornerstone of security for banks and governments in as soon as a few months, leading experts believe.
Scott Aaronson, a pre-eminent theoretical computer scientist, told the Guardian there were “well-attested rumours” that powerful artificial intelligence models had broken ground on decades-old maths problems related to how the world encodes secret information.
Depending on what has been discovered, it is possible that hackers – and many other people – could in short order have the power to more easily decrypt the world’s secret communications: banking transactions, diplomatic cables and private emails, for example.
If this happened, then spy agencies, governments, banks and private companies could be forced to very suddenly update the foundations of how they communicate, or risk extremely sensitive information being widely exposed.
Aaronson’s and others’ concerns come from a series of mathematical advances that frontier AI companies have announced in the past weeks.
Most of these are abstract breakthroughs. But to experts in the field, they indicate that AI is powerful enough to crack problems that most of them thought would be unsolved for the foreseeable future.
Earlier this week, Aaronson wrote that he was “virtually certain, from multiple reliable sources, that the AI labs are now sitting on one or more bombshells [in cryptography]”.
“The people who thought AI couldn’t do any interesting maths are definitely wrong at this stage, and so it’s no longer clear where the limit is,” said Steven Murdoch, a professor of computer science at University College London.
Speaking to BBC Radio 4’s Today programme on Friday, the mathematician Marcus du Sautoy said “we don’t know” what the broader ramifications of many of these discoveries were, and what this meant for encryption.
But, he added, “no one has been talking about the problem which is at the heart of protecting all our electronic secrets. Why is nobody talking about that problem? Some of us are thinking: maybe they’ve already solved it, and of course, it would have such massive implications that everyone has just kept it quiet.”
Governments, banks and spy agencies have been preparing for the possibility of powerful decryption for the past few years. This is because quantum computers, when developed, will be able to break the decades-old algorithms that underpin many forms of encryption.
But the timeline for quantum computers to be able to do this is still years away, not weeks or months. Murdoch said many governments and technology companies were working towards a higher standard of encryption – one that would not be vulnerable to quantum computers – but slowly. “The target was something like 2032, and that was probably going to be missed.”
Now he, Aaronson and others think there may be sudden advances in what the world understands about cryptography, and these could come soon.
What these revelations could be is less certain. What Aaronson and others think could have happened is that either AI has come up with a new way to break encryption or, conversely, it has proved that such a shortcut does not exist.
This would come from AI models picking apart the maths that underlies encryption, and that up until this point has not been fully understood. If AI has done this, it could find a way to decrypt, and therefore expose, massive amounts of secret data.
It could also prove this information is more secure than previously thought.
The world uses multiple forms of encryption – based on different underlying algorithms – and there are different guesses as to what may have been found, and therefore what systems could be affected.
No concrete announcements have come from AI companies regarding revelations about encryption, said Aaronson, but “anyone informed is assuming” that they are there.
Alan Woodward, a professor of computer science at the University of Surrey, said a mathematical breakthrough related to cryptography would not necessarily mean widespread chaos tomorrow. What has changed is the speed at which AI is solving problems that have been shrouded in mystery for decades – with potentially serious consequences.
“What [Aaronson] is basically saying is that people need to be ready for something to suddenly spring out, because there’s definitely a sort of a trend.”
Earlier this week, Andrew Bailey, the governor of the Bank of England, addressed fears that companies such as OpenAI and Anthropic had achieved breakthroughs in cryptography, saying these companies should be cautious about releasing new models to the public.
In the past weeks, AI companies have released proofs of mathematical results that closely relate to this question. One of these involved a foundational question about how to categorise computationally “hard” problems.
Aaronson said these breakthroughs had been seismic for experts in the fields of maths and computer science, some of whom have spent their careers working on these problems, and others who assumed that these problems would not be solved in their lifetimes.
“We’ve never seen a period like this,” he said. “I’m trying to face the reality of what is happening.”
He emphasised that these breakthroughs still needed to be understood, however – which means human mathematicians and computer scientists need to go through and verify the proofs that AI companies say they have produced.
Woodward said it was unlikely that any discoveries would “undo encryption by the end of the week”. Even if such a finding exists, there are hurdles to its widespread deployment.
But big banks and governments that rely on encryption “need to be agile”, said Woodward. “The ones that are agile and can change quickly will be safe. The ones that are the laggards could get caught out.”
Anthropic and OpenAI were approached for comment.
