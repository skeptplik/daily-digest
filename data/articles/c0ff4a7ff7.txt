UK’s long-awaited National Cyber Action Plan delayed until December
Britain has again delayed the launch of its National Cyber Action Plan, the government’s long-awaited strategy for defending the wider economy against state-backed and criminal hackers, according to multiple people familiar with the matter.
The document, originally conceived as a national cyber strategy, was first due to be published by the end of 2025. After initial delays it was rebranded as an action plan and was close to publication in July this year before being postponed amid Keir Starmer’s resignation.
Officials, who had planned to publish the document in mid-November, now aim to unveil it on December 9 according to several sources, speaking to Recorded Future News on condition of anonymity.
The reasons for the latest delay are unclear. A government spokesperson declined to comment on the timing but said ministers remained committed to publishing the plan.
The latest delay follows an influential cross-party House of Lords committee warning that the government “appears complacent” about “potentially catastrophic risks,” including cyberattacks from Russia, China and Iran.
In a report published last week, the National Resilience Committee found Britain was dangerously underprepared for crises which could cascade across the economy, with disruption spilling over multiple sectors and disrupting the everyday lives of millions of people.
Baroness Northover, a Liberal Democrat member of the committee, told Recorded Future News that the country could not afford more uncertainty or drift. “This situation is unacceptable at a time of significant and escalating risk,” she said. “We must act urgently. The cost of inaction is enormous.”
“Further absence of an overarching National Cyber Action Plan, aimed at defending the economy against state-backed and criminal hacking, is not just frustrating, it is serious,” she said.
“The UK has got to keep pace with threats. Delay and avoidance is simply not an option and underlines the Committee’s point that the UK remains seriously exposed and underprepared for very significant events, and our vulnerability is current, active and acute on many fronts.”
No dither but delay
Successive delays to cybersecurity legislation and policy announcements have contributed to a perception that the issue is a low political priority, despite its growing importance to Britain’s economic and national security.
The Cyber Security and Resilience Bill, an update to the country's critical-infrastructure cyber laws, took more than four years to reach Parliament and is now not expected to be enforced until 2028 — a decade on from the NIS Regulations it was written to replace.
The core provisions of the CSRB had already been completed back in 2022 under Rishi Sunak, whose government incorrectly described the laws as “updated” before failing to include them in that year's King's Speech setting out the government’s parliamentary agenda and leaving the draft bill ultimately unintroduced to parliament.
When Starmer's government first moved to bring the bill forward in September 2025, it was then delayed again amid a cabinet reshuffle.
Separately, a set of ransomware proposals — mandatory reporting for all victims, a licensing regime for extortion payments and a ban on ransoms for critical infrastructure operators — was due to go out to consultation in mid-2024, before being scuppered when Sunak called a general election.
Officials argue that the delays belie the substantial amount of work underway in Westminster.
The government is expected to bring forward a new National Security Bill next year to reform the Computer Misuse Act — although some fear the reforms as proposed would fail to protect security researchers — as well as finally set the ransomware proposals on the statute books.
In last month’s budget, the government committed £120 million ($159 million) over four years to the AI Security Institute and a further £40 million ($53 million) to establish a National Agentic Security Unit to address the threats posed by AI agents acting autonomously.
Another £70 million ($93 million) was earmarked for an AI-powered vulnerability hunting center at the National Cyber Security Centre (NCSC), with £60 million ($79 million) allocated to the first phase of Cyber Shield, a national defense system that would use AI agents to find and fix security weaknesses across government and critical infrastructure networks.
Potentially more consequential for the technology industry was a commitment, buried among the budget announcements, that the government would consult next year on “where liability for cyber breaches should sit.”
Ministers have also launched a voluntary Cyber Resilience Pledge, seeking commitments from British businesses to put cybersecurity on the boardroom agenda and better manage their supply-chain risks. The government describes the pledge as a central pillar of the National Cyber Action Plan.
National Cyber Action Plan
The plan’s contents have not been officially disclosed. Recorded Future News understands it will include three pillars focusing on Threat, Growth and Resilience.
The clearest public indication of the government’s approach came in a lecture to the Royal United Services Institute in June by the NCSC’s chief executive Richard Horne.
Horne called for a full court press across what he termed the “near, mid and far spaces” of cyberspace — a framing people familiar with the plan expect to shape the areas it focuses on.
He defined the near space as the defense of individual organizations, the far space as offensive action against adversaries and the “mid space” as the shared “cloud, technology and telecommunications infrastructure,” most of which he said was “in private hands.”
In that space, he said, the government would partner with providers to “harden the mid space and disrupt attacker activity.”
The idea of an action plan is to specifically set out how and when specific activities will be undertaken, rather than simply describing the overall strategy. Sources who spoke to Recorded Future News says the critical details would be in the plan’s implementation schedule.
