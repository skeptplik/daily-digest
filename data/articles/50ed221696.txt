Self-publishing is now firmly established as a viable route to market for indie developers, with many only looking to publishers for help with specific issues – chiefly marketing, based on conversations with GamesIndustry.biz this year. Audrey Leprince, co-founder of Cairn developer The Game Bakers, argues that while working with third parties is essential, self-publishing is ultimately the only route to sustainability for indie developers.
Leprince was speaking at Paris Game Biz in October, where she gave a presentation called "Self-Publishing: Build Your Own Distribution". In addition to co-founding the Montpellier-based studio behind the indie hits Squids, Furi, Haven, and this year's Cairn, she is the co-founder of Wings, which funds games from studios with women or gender-marginalised developers in leadership roles.
Leprince talked about her experience of self-publishing with The Game Bakers, emphasising the importance of building a community around your studio, the benefits of not being financially beholden to a publisher, and the importance of continually marketing your games. Below is a summary of the key insights from her presentation.
Community connection
"The first reason I think self-publishing is fantastic is because you have this direct connection with your community," Leprince told the audience at Paris Game Biz.
"Players find their games through their communities, their friends, their family, their favourite streamer, their YouTuber – that's how they find their games. So if you are going to give your game [to a] publisher, they're going to reach them through their social media, through their outreach, through their brand, through their social media manager and community managers. And you're not going to gain access to that community [...] for the next game if you don't have the same publisher."
That direct connection builds over time. Leprince noted that around 3.8 million people played Furi and another two million played Haven, "so now we're talking to a few million people every time we launch a game."
She emphasised just how important communities are for a game's success. "They're actually the number one marketing tool," she said. "Of course, there's also the platforms, Steam, PlayStation, and Nintendo Switch, but those ones you can't really influence anymore." As an indie developer, she said, "long gone is the time where you could actually negotiate to be placed on those stores," adding that the recent move away from manual curation on Steam means your game will only be shown to specific audiences that Steam decides on. "So the community is really the number one."
Money
Leprince said that the second reason for choosing self-publishing – as well as self-funding – is that as a developer, you get to keep all the money.
She emphasised that a funding deal that sees a studio giving up 50% of their game's revenue might enable the studio to put out its first game, but even if it's a fairly big success, there may only be just enough left to make the second game – and probably not enough to do a more ambitious project. This can lock the developer into a cycle of reliance.
"The moment you choose to work with a publisher, you set your step to work with the publisher again and again and again, until you have massive success. You never have enough money to take all the risks you need if you keep giving 70% or 50% of your revenue to the publisher."
Self-funding allows a studio to retain its creative freedom and set the studio's direction, she said. "Unless you know how to find the right publisher to support the risk for your projects, you might reach a stage of your studio life where you want to make a game that no one wants to buy – that's going to be a problem. Then you're going to start making a game that people want to buy, which is not the game you wanted to make. And maybe you will be less good at making it.
"It's why I think our games are successful and quite different: It's because no one is telling us what to do."
She gave the example of how people dismissed The Game Bakers' vision for Cairn. "I swear when I said it's just about climbing, people were laughing. They thought I was joking. They were like, yeah, there's going to be boss fights or something. No, it's just climbing the mountain. They didn't believe it."
Marketing
Leprince emphasised the importance of marketing as being the key to success in self-publishing, even if it feels like an onerous task.
"We keep hearing, 'Oh yeah, but I don't have time to do marketing and publishing, because I have to work on the game'. Fair enough. But my take is that you are actually doing the marketing when you work on the game."
She explained that in making a game, the studio should have already thought deeply about things like the game's style, its audience, and what makes it different – all key marketing points.
"If you don't know what's marketable, don't make a game," she said. "You have to know who you're talking to, and what you're making, and who's your competition. It's impossible otherwise. There's no world in which you make a game and someone else is going to make it a success. It's not going to happen. You have to make it a success from the beginning, from the decisions you take, from the art director you hire, from the writer you hire, from the gameplay decisions you make from the very beginning. You are the creative director of the game. You are the first marketer of the game."
"There's no world in which you make a game and someone else is going to make it a success"
Leprince emphasised the need for developers to keep up to date on industry trends through newsletters like GameDiscoverCo, as well as engaging with the community. "There's no secret," she said. "The secret that publishers have and that you don't is that they own communities."
At the start, Leprince took on all the marketing for The Game Bakers, but now the studio has a full-time marketing manager as well as a community manager who works two days a week, creating content for sites like TikTok and Instagram. In addition, the studio works with marketing agencies.
Leprince emphasised that although it's necessary to allocate budget to marketing, there are ways to keep costs down, such as the payments to influencers. "We start organic, but what we do is we target the influencers that the big influencers watch," she said. "So we get the small ones that influence the big ones, and the big ones, when five of their friends have been playing a game, they're considering it. So that's how we got huge names in influence to cover Cairn for free."
Always on
Some may have a perception that marketing only starts near a game's launch and ends not long after, but Leprince emphasised that it remains a constant, even for games that have been out for years. "Our games have an incredible long tail. A lot of our sales come from year two, year three, year four, year five, year ten," she said.
The marketing never really stops – and the team's marketing manager is working constantly, even if the studio's next big game is still a long way from launch or even reveal. In the meantime, there are patches, DLC, and console ports to market, as well as things like awards to enter. "You have to keep pushing and talking to the community, creating events."
Leprince gave the example of a recent deep discount on Furi and Haven on Steam, which brought "massive income" to the studio, and which resulted from a discussion with Steam at GDC. "So you have a lot of ways to keep your current game selling," she said, listing things like updates to years-old titles. "All of that is marketing work. It's not production driven anymore. Then there's the art books, and merchandise, and events, all of that to keep talking about the game all the time. So it's a lot of work actually."
She said that certain events will involve big marketing pushes, like a new game mode, launch date announcement, or DLC launch, but for others, it's fine to reuse assets, or perhaps do interviews with the design or writing team. "You just have to find ways to create content as cheap as you can," she said. "Reuse everything you do. And actually they don't really mind. For instance, Nintendo doesn't mind that the trailer we put in Nintendo Direct is the same as the launch trailer."
The right partner
Even though The Game Bakers is firmly behind self-publishing and self-funding, Leprince stressed the importance of finding partners. "For Furi, PlayStation was our partner. They brought $1 million to make the game. It was against one of those deals [for] PlayStation Plus at the time."
PlayStation was also a partner for Cairn, but The Game Bakers decided they didn't want to do a subscription deal this time around. "So instead we had what we call a co-publishing deal. So they brought a substantial amount of money for marketing. They bought some placement on the store, they bought some placement in social media."
"I would say that anyway, you are still taking 100% of the risk, even if you have a publisher, because if everything fails, it's going to be on you."
Platform partners are great for providing visibility, she said. "A show like Summer Game Fest is 50 million people watching. A PlayStation showcase can go to 80 million people watching. This is not direct conversion to wishlists or sales, but it's huge awareness."
Having a partner is half of Game Bakers' marketing strategy, she said. "Without PlayStation and without Xbox, we wouldn't be where we're at. We have massive powerhouses that carry our IP much further than we could. So I advise you to always work with a more powerful partner, a platform in particular, and choose the right one."
"Actually it's easy to work with them," she continued. "If you are big, you have an account manager. If they don't know you yet, they have people who are there to talk to you at shows. So you have to have a friend of yours make an introduction to one of those persons."
Judging the workload
Finally, Leprince emphasised that developers have to evaluate whether they have the capacity to take on the burden of self-publishing and marketing. "If you are already overwhelmed making the game, don't even try doing this on top," she said.
"You need to find a person that you can work with that will help you do this marketing outreach, help you coordinate with a community manager if you don't want to do it yourself, or a content creator, and do the hard work of finding the right influencers for your games. It's time consuming."
"At Wings, when we have solo devs, we don't let them self-publish any more, because they just burn out. So we help them self-publish [...] in the hope that they will be able to grow their team a little bit for the next game."
Importantly, she stressed the need to seek help if you need it. "This is not a solo adventure. You should go with at least one person you can trust. This is the key to having a sustainable studio."
