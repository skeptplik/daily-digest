In this podcast, Michael Stiefel spoke to Zhou Yu about using dynamic simulations to evaluate AI agents. One particular focus was conversational agents where actions depend on prior responses, and the non-deterministic nation of LLMs means you cannot rely on static, reference-based benchmarks.You need to test them using dynamic simulations that incorporate different personas, goals, and corner cases. You can simulate how conversations shift based on moods, preferences, accents, or environmental conditions such as factory background noise. Rather than relying on simply binary pass or fail, the rule based task verifiers can measure whether the task was completed as well as threshold metrics for cost, efficiency, safety, and compliance.
Simulations can be used during the entire software lifecycle. Product managers can use them for prototyping and design. Engineers can use them for testing in CI/CD pipelines. They can also be used to test production for model drift or failure of third party tools. They can also be used to test compliance with the laws in different regions. Simulations can also be used with synthetic data to fine-tune models.
Key Takeaways
- To evaluate AI agents you cannot rely on static, reference-based benchmarks because the systems are not deterministic, and often the results depend on prior responses. Testing requires using dynamic simulations that can incorporate personas, goals, and corner cases.
- Using simulations for testing applies to the entire software lifecycle. Product managers can use it for designing. Engineers can use it for unit and regression tests. Before deployment you can do stress testing. You can use it to test production for model drift or failure of third party tools.
- Reliability Engineering for AI systems requires the combination of skills from machine learning, systems reliability, and traditional software engineering. Human subject matter experts refine simulation scenarios based on production data, design documents, or even social media.
- Simulation of conversational agents can evaluate multiple parts of the human - software interaction. For example, it can consider the varying interaction possibilities stemming from prior responses, and the way they might change based on human moods, as well as varying goals and preferences. Another example is simulation of corner cases. It can also take into consideration environmental considerations such as factory noise. It can verify whether a task was completed alongside metrics for cost, efficiency, safety, and compliance. Legal compliance in different regions can also be verified.
- Detailed logging of agent statements and tool calls also provides an audit trail for regulatory compliance and failure analysis.
