Consider a famous scene from the heat of a Test cricket battle. The South African team manager calls the opposition chairman of selectors into a room and says: "You'd better have a look at this." The pair sit down to view some sinister television footage.
The chairman of selectors is aghast at the replays: something comes out of one of his players' trousers and is rubbed against the ball. The ball-tampering player and his own team manager are promptly dispatched to the match referee. What the player has to say for himself, the chairman of selectors later admits, isn't "completely honest".
The player is duly excoriated and his punishment doubled on account of his lying. The press goes into overdrive. Career-ending sackings are called for but don't quite eventuate. And then, eventually, everyone moves on.
The player is not Cameron Bancroft, in case you haven't already guessed. It is Michael Atherton, about whom most of us probably hold far more positive views: a brave opening batter, a thoughtful and perceptive TV and newspaper analyst and something close to a conscience figure in the game, beyond reproach despite that regrettable interlude at Lord's in 1994.
This raises questions about recent events that were not adequately answered in 2018, when Australia's tampering crisis exploded. Firstly, in these tumultuous, adversarial times, can we no longer hold multiple ideas in our heads at once?
Are we really not mature and reasonable enough to say that in the unending and sometimes bitter pursuit of the success Australians demand of them, our cricketers occasionally take things too far, just as Atherton did?
Are we really to put aside the plainly obvious truth that Sandpapergate was, in and of itself, a trifling matter for which a select few got stung with the compound interest on decades of accumulated grievances against entitled, poorly behaved Australian cricketers?
And are we really going to keep shooting the messenger? We might think Usman Khawaja naive, but in our heart of hearts, have we ever really believed that three batters were the only ones who knew about a minor cheating scheme that was of no direct personal benefit to them?
It is possible this entire affair now says far more about the Australian cricket public than it does our cricketers. It certainly says more about their bosses.
As was the case in 2018, the public expressions of dismay, both informed and not, speak of one positive: Australians care deeply about Test cricket and take it personally when they feel its honour is being sullied.
Equally, the defensiveness, the reflexive and faintly sinister demolition of Khawaja's character, and the "leave it in the past" mentality show that an unfortunately large cohort are in favour of administrators burying any form of dishonesty or wrongdoing by their own team, no matter the strength of the evidence in front of their eyes.
It is precisely this dogma that got Australia in this mess in the first place: there is a line, but we decide where it is and whether or not we've crossed it. And when we do, we will bully into submission anyone who talks about it more honestly.
So many people have made this issue about themselves; perhaps you can permit me two personal anecdotes that illustrate broader points that have not been taken seriously enough.
In the first, I am, from memory, nine years old, down at the local cricket club, standing with other boys in a semicircle around our coach. He has in his hand a two-piece Red King cricket ball that has been battered so often against our cyclone fencing practice nets that it looks like roadkill. The coach is showing us what to do when a ball has degenerated beyond spit and polish treatment: pick the seam, gouge one side, hope for the best.
In the second, I'm in my mid-30s, working as a publisher but engaged in the unliterary task of searching the abrasives aisle of the local hardware store for the perfect piece of yellow sandpaper. It will be torn into small pieces and photographed to feature on the cover of Gideon Haigh's Sandpapergate book, Crossing the Line: How Australian Cricket Lost Its Way.
The first scenario was a point made with possibly unnecessary force this week by Stuart MacGill: everybody does it and always has (although perhaps not on the wall of the SCG dugout). Any club cricketer who says they are unfamiliar with at least some form of ball alteration is either an extremely disengaged teammate or a member of Pinocchio's Invitational XI.
As for the second, and putting aside the claim that Australian batsmen were using sandpaper on their bats — not beyond the realms of possibility, but behaviour that would be "rare" in both senses that cricketers use that word — it prompted a feeling that has never left me: "Someone in the Australian cricket team made this exact trip to Bunnings warehouse."
There were, in other words, both subconscious cultural factors and very obvious acts of premeditation at play in 2018 that always made the denials appear ludicrous.
Frankly, it is time that a lot of people grew up and acknowledged that a messy pile of accumulated facts are occasionally going to contradict each other, and that the truth is not as neat as a cricket scorecard. Some key points we should always bear in mind:
One: it is implausible that only Steve Smith, David Warner and Cameron Bancroft were aware the ball was being altered.
Two: many lies were, and since have been, told in service of Cricket Australia's process of "investigation", which put public relations priorities well ahead of the truth.
Three: having committed to a PR version of punishment, acting not in response to a case of ball tampering but to a public relations disaster, Cricket Australia was bound to hand out disproportionate penalties that have warped our rational sense of wrongdoing.
Four: the actual truth — or at least a version of it that did not insult our intelligence — was always bound to come out eventually. That it has occurred when some of the extremely well-remunerated players are still main characters should not mean it is buried again.
Five: Test cricketers are human beings, as prone to white lies and interpersonal dramas as the rest of us, with the added burden of intense scrutiny of their every move. The idea that any one of them would experience, process and recall certain events the same way as another is preposterous.
Six: It is equally preposterous to think every Australian player gets on with his teammates all the time, or that inevitable frictions are as dramatic as they have been made out to be. Australian Test stars of previous generations have, in books and post-career media work, detailed threatened (and actual) lawsuits against each other, exposure of infidelities, even death threats. Most of them have hugged it out in the end.
What many fans are left with, probably, is the similarly vague sense of anger and resentment — "This again? Really?" — that followed the original events. Cricket is a game for soul-searchers and its history is pockmarked with these moral eruptions.
Anger, in this case as in others before it, needs a target. The easiest one is Khawaja, because it is less practical and cathartic to direct it at faceless administrators. But make no mistake: back then, they absolutely bottled a situation that demanded level heads, and now they claim there is nothing else to see here.

What would a level-headed reaction have looked like in 2018? That was the question faced by English chairman of selectors Ray Illingworth when he dealt with the Atherton affair.
To go with the media's lashings, Atherton was fined £2,000 — a proactive measure to ward off the suspension that was the preference of the Australian match referee, Peter Burge.
Years later, Illingworth pondered what a suspension would have meant for Atherton. "It could have finished him off as captain," he wrote in 2006. "[But] I don't think he would have been happy no matter the outcome."
"We were the first country to step in on the matter. Richie Benaud came up to me afterwards and said: 'Well done, you've handled it brilliantly.'"
It is something nobody would say of Cricket Australia.
