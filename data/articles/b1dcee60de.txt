France aims to build reserves of critical minerals and urges Europe to invest more. Researcher Frédéric Jeannin says these stocks should protect France from market shocks and prepare its industry for a possible armed conflict.
A late-term announcement, at a time when France and Europe are facing tensions that are disrupting global trade and supply chains. Emmanuel Macron is launching the creation of reserves of rare earths and critical metals to reduce the country's dependence and anticipate shortages. Three key sectors are targeted: defence, the automotive industry and aerospace.
The plan, seen by Euronews, was unveiled at the start of the week during a meeting at the Élysée called "Protect France", which brought together around 15 industry bosses.
Implementation of this mechanism of strategic reserves and "joint purchases" has been entrusted to the Caisse des dépôts, the state's financial arm.
Companies will be required to play their part by diversifying their supply sources, or risk seeing their public support cut. Mechanisms for "price guarantees" will also be offered "to give producers visibility".
But many questions remain open. At this stage, the challenge is to define precisely the storage scenarios, the stakeholders involved and the minerals for which needs are most urgent.
Frédéric Jeannin, a researcher at the IRIS think tank and co-head of the Energy and Raw Materials programme, stresses the importance of this preparatory work: "The state needs to draw up a list of what is strategic, which companies it wants to supply," he told Euronews in an interview.
Physical reserves or virtual ones based on purchase commitments? In the expert's view, "we can have both". The point of this "insurance", he explains, is to allow the country to cope with two types of threat: "a security shock or a market shock".
"We wonder whether China has a better picture of our industry than we do"
According to Frédéric Jeannin, these reserves must first serve to "strengthen our resilience" to international raw materials markets, where access to resources is "less and less certain, at least at an economically acceptable price".
China currently dominates the sector, notably thanks to its vast refining capacity, the energy geopolitics researcher points out.
While the Élysée describes dependence on supplies as a possible "tool of coercion and threat", Frédéric Jeannin sees these reserves as a "safety buffer". They would make it possible to cushion price swings that could be triggered by decisions taken in Beijing.
China has a legal arsenal that allows it to block the export of its critical raw materials depending on their end use and final recipient, whenever Beijing considers that these exports run counter to its interests.
Export licences thus enable it to screen sales on a case by case basis, without having to impose a blanket embargo that would hurt its own mining companies.
The researcher also highlights the level of transparency demanded of buyers regarding the processing and value chain of materials imported from China, "to the point of demanding information that amounts to industrial secrets".
"We wonder whether China has a better picture of our industry than we do", observes Frédéric Jeannin.
Reserves to hold out in the event of war
Frédéric Jeannin identifies a second factor: the prospect of an open conflict with Russia.
The aim is first of all very concrete: "We need to guarantee that raw materials will be available on our soil, ideally through physical stockpiles, to supply the defence industry".
"But there is also a psychological dimension", he adds: it is a way of telling a potential "hostile actor" that France is able to withstand a crisis, "with stocks available, with components available, with raw materials available that would allow us to hold out for a certain period".
How long that period would be remains to be seen. Although the scheme is designed to be long term, the Élysée says it has already "generated" nearly 7 billion euros of investment in France.
Even though French and American industry are not comparable in size, the United States provides a benchmark. Last February, Washington announced the creation of its strategic reserve, "Project Vault" ("projet coffre-fort"), worth 12 billion dollars. It is intended to cover the equivalent of two months of national consumption.
"Seven billion is a good start. We are talking about amounts that will run into billions of euros", notes Frédéric Jeannin. But, he emphasises, it is still necessary to know who these investments will benefit and according to which criteria.
"What companies need to keep in mind, and indeed, I would say, what citizens need to keep in mind, is that this resilience is necessary today and that it has a cost", the expert stresses.
"Our industries have to factor in a cost of resilience to make sure we have what we need on our territory. It will be more expensive, but it is necessary to protect us in the event of a crisis", he concludes.
"Europe is making a strategic mistake"
Warnings about the need to build up strategic reserves are not new, including at European level.
The European Union has identified 34 critical raw materials, 17 of them considered "strategic", for which demand is expected to outstrip supply in the years ahead.
For these strategic materials, dependence on third countries is high. China supplies the EU with 85% of its samarium, which when combined with cobalt forms magnets that are vital for electric motors; Turkey provides 99% of its boron, used in particular in ballistic protection; and South Africa delivers 41% of its manganese, essential for steel production, according to a map (source in French) from the European Commission.
In early November, Members of the European Parliament backed (source in French) the creation of a Competitiveness Fund, currently under negotiation as part of the EU budget for 2028 to 2034. France wants to earmark 25 billion euros out of the roughly 300 billion euro envelope, in order to stabilise prices and build up European reserves.
But asserting Paris's priorities within the European budget is proving difficult. Thursday's extraordinary European summit even descended into a full-blown drama, laying bare divisions among the 27 member states. The flashpoint was a proposal by the President of the European Council, Antonio Costa, to cut around 200 billion euros from the initial budget draft put forward by the Commission.
Emmanuel Macron denounced a "strategic mistake" in response to the cuts, which were supported notably by German Chancellor Friedrich Merz.
"We are about to formalise a gap in European public investment compared with China and the United States", the French president said at a press conference in Brussels.
"When it comes to having a budget that matches our ambitions, there is no one to follow us", he lamented.
Emmanuel Macron threatened to pull out of the budget negotiations if no agreement emerges by the end of the year. He refuses to let the talks drag on and encroach on the French presidential election, whose first round is scheduled for 18 April 2027.
The fate of France's strategic reserves will also depend on who succeeds him at the Élysée.
