President Vladimir Putin has ordered the Russian government to approve a program by the end of 2026 to build data centers in the coal-producing regions of Yakutia and Kemerovo, following a wave of Ukrainian strikes on computing facilities last month.
The order comes as Russian officials weigh how to protect the country’s digital infrastructure from drone attacks. The Digital Development Ministry has urged companies to build data centers underground in Russia, while some businesses have begun reserving server capacity abroad.
Kemerovo region Governor Ilya Seredyuk has proposed building data centers in disused coal mines. He told state media that coal companies already spend heavily on reclaiming and maintaining former mine sites and argued that putting servers underground could cut cooling costs.
Analysts say adapting old mines could drive up construction and operating costs. Eldar Murtazin, an analyst at Mobile Research Group, estimated that a data center built in a former mine could cost two to five times as much as a conventional facility. He pointed to the expense of running power lines and fiber-optic cables underground, pumping out groundwater and staffing remote sites in rotations.
One early estimate put the cost of building underground data centers at 30% to 40% above that of comparable facilities above ground.
Distance from customers could also pose a problem. Pyotr Istomin, a manager at electrical equipment supplier CHINT in Russia, said most demand would remain concentrated around Moscow and that data transmission delays between the capital and the Kemerovo region could reach 70 milliseconds, enough to affect some financial transactions and other services that rely on near-instant responses.
Ukrainian strikes in October hit facilities used by Yandex, VK, Sberbank and Russia’s Federal Tax Service. Yandex facilities in the Kaluga and Vladimir regions were among those hit.
Russian officials have also explored setting up data centers for artificial intelligence in countries Moscow considers friendly. Demand for space in foreign data centers rose among Russian companies after the strikes.
