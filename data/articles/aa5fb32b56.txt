Israeli opposition leader Eisenkot begins the hard work of forging a coalition government
Israel's ex-military chief Gadi Eisenkot was tapped by President Isaac Herzog to try to form a government Sunday evening. But his lack of a clear majority in parliament will make trying to forge a coalition alongside the country's divided minor parties an uphill struggle.

Israeli President Isaac Herzog formally gave opposition politician Gadi Eisenkot the first chance to form the next government Sunday evening.
Complex coalition negotiations still lie ahead, but it marked a significant step in the former military chief's effort to oust Israel's longest-serving prime minister, Benjamin Netanyahu, after neither bloc emerged from the October 27 parliamentary elections with a majority.
“I hope with all my heart that whatever government is formed will lead to unity, connection and healing among all segments of our people – for our very existence depends on it, and we have only one country,” Herzog said at the ceremony at the presidential residence in Jerusalem.
The announcement doesn't guarantee that Eisenkot, a former army chief, will become the next prime minister. He has up to six weeks to form a coalition government by securing alliances.
Herzog said that in consultations with the parties, a majority of members of parliament, known as the Knesset, supported giving Eisenkot this task.
At Sunday's ceremony, Eisenkot promised to give the country leadership that people can trust, that will act in the interests of the public and that represents all parts of society.
“An absolute majority of Knesset members representing the Israeli public support change,” he said. “I intend to navigate the situation with patience, humility, composure and a deep commitment to a Jewish, equal, liberal, and secure Israel.”
Among the possible kingmakers – and most politically fraught potential allies – are the Arab-led parties. They made historic gains in the election thanks to high voter turnout among Israel's Arab citizens.
Only one Arab party, Ra’am, has ever joined a governing coalition, in 2021. Ra’am has said it will support Eisenkot, but the party does not have enough seats on its own to give Eisenkot a parliamentary majority.
Breaking with decades of precedent, the second Arab-led party, the Joint List, has offered to support Eisenkot from outside the coalition to end Netanyahu’s grip on power.
But that cooperation is fraught for both sides. For the Joint List, it would mean backing a government unlikely to end Israel’s wars or curb the creeping annexation of the occupied West Bank through illegal settlements. For Eisenkot and other opposition parties, it would mean aligning with parties that not only criticise Israel’s policies but reject its identity as a Jewish state.
One of Eisenkot’s partners, the more conservative Yisrael Beytenu party, already has said it will not join a coalition with Arab-led parties.
Eisenkot has laid out four conditions for parties that want to join him. They must recognise Israel as a Jewish and democratic state, accept its declaration of independence, support military, national or civil service for all citizens, and declare the Hamas militant group a terrorist group. It remains unclear whether his would-be Arab partners are ready to accept these conditions.
Furthermore, Netanyahu has repeatedly asserted unsubstantiated claims that the election was “stolen” by Arab parties. In a pressure tactic on Eisenkot, he says any alliance with these parties would be an act of betrayal.
Israel’s Shin Bet security agency has launched an investigation into possible foreign interference in the vote, but the country’s Central Elections Committee – and Herzog – say the vote was valid and even if wrongdoing is found, it did not have a significant impact on the electoral results.
Eisenkot has vowed to form a government as quickly as possible. But each likely scenario appears to entail compromises or broken promises.
Aside from seeking the support of the Arab-led parties, Eisenkot has two options: a power-sharing agreement with Netanyahu or an agreement with the ultra-Orthodox parties, which demand military exemptions for their community.
Eisenkot and his partners made equal military service a central part of their campaign. Specifically, they are seeking an end to the long-standing draft exemptions.
The religious parties, which were part of Netanyahu’s outgoing coalition, want to preserve them, calling them essential to their lifestyle of religious study. But the exemptions have grown especially controversial among the broader public during the past three years of war.
Anticipating its position as a swing vote, the ultra-Orthodox party United Torah Judaism has left the door open to cooperating with Eisenkot.
The former military chief also appealed to Netanyahu's right-wing Likud Party as well as the far-right party of Finance Minister Bezalel Smotrich to join a government led by himself as prime minister.
"My door is open and my hand is extended, wholeheartedly and willingly, to anyone who understands the gravity of this moment," he said.
Eisenkot has 28 days to form a coalition, with a possible two-week extension from the president. If he is unable to form a government, Netanyahu could then be given a chance to put together a coalition. If Netanyahu is also unable to do so, the country would return to elections early next spring.
Israel’s new members of Knesset will be sworn in on Tuesday evening. Netanyahu and his cabinet will remain in office in a caretaker capacity, with limited powers, until a coalition is formed.
Despite tenuous ceasefires, Israel's military continues to fight Hamas militants in the rubble of Gaza and Iran-aligned Hezbollah militants in Lebanon, where Israel has occupied swathes of the south.
The the war the country launched alongside the US against Iran also shows no sign of ending.
