The global wellness industry has reached a fever pitch—and shows no signs of slowing down. Overstimulated, under-rested, and on the brink of burnout, we’re desperately seeking antidotes to the ills of the digital era. But what if the answers to our modern-day woes lie in ancient Native American wisdom, not in a supposed cure-all gadget, supplement, or fitness routine?
For millennia, Indigenous communities have prioritized connection to nature, practiced reciprocity with community, and upheld sacred cultural traditions. We can all embrace these holistic principles in our daily lives, says Lakota/Anishinaabe wellness advocate Chelsey Luger. That’s the premise behind her new book, Native Home: The Ancestral Art of Creating Healing Spaces. In it, she offers tangible ways to incorporate these concepts into our living spaces to promote mental, physical, spiritual, and emotional balance.
“Everybody in North America should understand Native American home teachings because we’re all living on this land,” says Luger, who is an enrolled member of the Turtle Mountain Band of Chippewa and a descendant of the Standing Rock Sioux Tribe. “There’s a movement in the home industry right now centered around ensuring our surroundings support our well-being. This might be an emerging conversation in the wellness space, but Indigenous communities have been living this way for centuries.”
Luger and her husband, Onk Akimel O’odham fitness educator Thosh Collins, co-founded the grassroots initiative Well For Culture back in 2013, with the aim of revitalizing Indigenous wellness practices. Since then, they’ve been traveling to tribal nations across the country to both teach and learn from these communities. Native Home is the culmination of those lessons.
She underscores that these principles are applicable to everyone, no matter their heritage, with this caveat: The difference between appreciation and appropriation is all in the approach. “I’m not encouraging you to do exactly what Indigenous communities do,” she says. “I’m encouraging you to apply these teachings to your own home in a way that reflects your own culture.”
For example, in a Native worldview, every home should tell a story through its design and decor, Luger explains. Her ancestors painted their tepees with images representing their familial experiences. Similarly, the artwork adorning our walls should feel curated and specific to our identity.
With this in mind, Luger encourages people to stay intimately involved in their home’s design and decorating process, as she’s doing with her family’s in-the-works house on Arizona’s Salt River Reservation (where Collins is from). But you needn’t be building a new abode to reap the benefits of Indigenous wellness. There are easy, everyday ways to embrace these principles—and they don’t cost a thing.
Starting small is as simple as decluttering one room to reduce sensory overload, letting in more natural sunlight to balance circadian rhythms, opening up your windows for cross-drafting airflow, or carving out an area for movement—even if it’s only a designated stretching space. Next-level applications encompass philosophies like Indigenous minimalism and Native geomancy (auspicious arrangement, akin to feng shui), which are all explained in the book.
Like most Indigenous wisdom, Luger’s advice is pretty intuitive—and yet, it isn’t how we lead our lives today. “We might say that we don’t want to overconsume, but Americans tend to think the bigger the house, the better,” she says. “Our generation is facing a housing crisis, and we should all embrace smaller living. Not only is it easier to maintain, but it keeps us better connected. That’s what this is ultimately about: interconnection.”
Here, Luger offers five tips for incorporating Indigenous wellness in your home.
Be One With Nature
“We’ve always had a respect for the elements—air, fire, water, and earth. Air in particular is considered sacred by most Indigenous communities. Here in the United States, we use air conditioning more than most other countries in the world. It’s a wonderful luxury, but our indoor environments should better reflect the outdoors. Try keeping your windows open for part of the day, no matter the season.”
Adorn the Doorway
“Doorways are considered portals, which are places of high energy. Growing up, my dad would hang a hand drum or a medicine wheel above our door. It’s a way to bring good energy both for those arriving and for those leaving. Honor the doorway by hanging something that represents the presence of your ancestors and offers both protection and peace.”
Embrace the Open Concept
“It’s a very European idea to have a separate room for everything. In contrast, Indigenous homes historically tended to be more fluid, with communal rooms that foster togetherness. That interconnection is part of our values as Indigenous people. Let at least one space in your home feel more multi-use, where family members can share space.”
Round It Off
“Many traditional Native structures are round, which represents women, Mother Earth, and our egalitarian values. Indigenous architect Douglas Cardinal (Métis/Blackfoot), who designed the National Museum of the American Indian in Washington, D.C., often expresses this with curvilinear forms. But you don’t have to build a round house or room. Choose a circular dining table, rugs, and pillows rather than square or rectangular ones.”
Above All, Honor Your Heritage
“As Native people, we’ve never lost that closeness to our ancestors, even through colonization and the boarding school era. That spiritual and practical connection to who we are as people is just part of our culture. Whoever you are, wherever you come from—everybody has ancestors they can and should feel connected to. Take time to learn about your culture, and then make your home a reflection of that.”
