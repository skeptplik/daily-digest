Samaritans old and new will, I’m sure, have been just as shocked as I was by your article (‘Is anyone up for a fight?’: The battle for the soul of Samaritans, 10 November). Many, though, will not be surprised. I was a volunteer for 26 years in three branches. The work was demanding and life-changing; the experience is so transformative that it gives every part of society people who are strong enough to bear others’ burdens and able to share their pain. We don’t stop being listeners when we stop being volunteers.
During my time, I found that the people at the top were detached from what went on at branch level. It is not news to me that they cannot understand how central to the work each local branch is and how important every human contact is to those who need to talk.
One phrase in the article struck an especially harsh note – the strategy will apparently enable the organisation to attract more “call handlers”. This is not a customer-service business. It is one flawed human being giving up their time to really listen to other human beings whose lives have fallen apart.
As it is so hard to do, it is not easy to find volunteers. When I became the director of my branch, recruitment was first on my to-do list. Difficult as it was to do, we managed it, but branches need to have the support and resources to do it well. Providing those should be the main job of the national team. I would say to them: stop counting the beans and start appreciating that this is one service that is too important to stop being small and local. Start doing what volunteers do – listen.

Our first concern is for callers, and we share the concerns of the chief executive, Julie Bentley, that in 2025 “some callers still didn’t get through, especially in crucial overnight hours”. In the past five years, answer rates have significantly improved, and they continue to do so.
The 13 branches facing closure or merger answered about 84,000 calls in the year to June. The future-proofing plan relies on scaling up selected branches and offering remote volunteering to replace that capacity. Neither of these has got off the starting blocks, let alone recruited and trained the volunteers needed to fill the gap.
If the management shared plans on how it will improve answer rates without losing ground through these changes, volunteers would be far less anxious for the future of the service. We have repeatedly asked for these, to no avail.
I have also taken calls from ambulance crews and police officers asking us to speak to the person they are with. Calling Samaritans the “fourth emergency service” is no exaggeration. Nobody would close a fire station before the new one had a crew. Our callers deserve a plan, not a leap of faith.

Branches are sustained by loyalty and familiarity, and most callers are conscious that they are talking to someone not too far away. The idea that volunteers will, for example, travel from Worthing to Brighton for shifts is so unrealistic as to be delusional, and mega-branches offering a call-centre experience will be ineffective. Those responsible for this calamity should think again.
