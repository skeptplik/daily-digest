People could be barred from settling in the UK over minor infractions such as cycling on the pavement or failing to pay a TV licence, ministers have been warned before the introduction of new “earned settlement” rules.
Under the plans, which are to come into effect next year, securing indefinite leave to remain will become significantly harder for many people. The rules introduce a strict new threshold where any adult conviction resulting in a court disposal – including fines, community orders, conditional cautions, and absolute or conditional discharges – triggers mandatory refusal.
This toughens up the current system under which applications are automatically refused only if an applicant has received a custodial or suspended sentence of 12 months or more.
The employment justice charity Work Rights Centre (WRC) has spearheaded civil society evidence submitted to parliament against the proposals, fearing the changes mean people who moved to the UK to work – including NHS and social care staff – will face lifelong exclusion over minor infractions.
Dr Dora-Olivia Vicol, the chief executive of WRC, said the rules meant that even Andy Burnham – who was given a court-imposed fine and six penalty points for speeding on the M62 at Manchester magistrates court in 2023 – would fail his own government’s test if he were an immigrant.
She said: “That even the prime minister would be rejected under his own rules demonstrates how draconian and punitive the new earned settlement policy is,” adding that the charity was “bitterly disappointed that the government had ignored widespread concerns about the plans”.
Remaining on temporary visas forces people to pay thousands of pounds in periodic visa renewals and immigration health surcharges alongside taxes, leaves them with no recourse to public funds, and can restrict their ability to switch employers or secure mortgages.
At a hearing of the House of Lords justice and home affairs committee on Tuesday, peers and experts examined the consequences of the government’s plan to define refusal grounds by court disposals rather than offence severity.
The committee chair, Don Foster, said its understanding was that minor matters such as “cycling on a pavement … not paying a TV licence … littering [and] flyposting” were among offences that could “lead you to permanently being barred from obtaining permanent permission to remain in this country”.
Dr Madeleine Sumption, the director of the University of Oxford’s Migration Observatory, said that while standard fixed-penalty notices did not trigger refusal on their own, minor offences would escalate into mandatory bans if a notice went unpaid, was contested in court, or resulted in any court-imposed disposal.
Sumption told the committee: “I think one of the interesting features of it is that it removes the discretion that might currently apply, so it will be mandatory for caseworkers to refuse settlement.”
She said contesting a penalty – such as challenging an alleged speeding offence or littering fine – could inadvertently trigger a mandatory refusal if a court subsequently imposed a fine or disposal.
The committee confirmed the government had conducted no assessment and held no reliable data on how many applicants would be affected by the changes.
In a recent written response to questions from Lord Foster regarding the definition of a criminal record, the Home Office minister Ray Collins said: “Where a person has been convicted in the UK for an offence committed when they were aged 18 or above, their application for settlement will be refused.”
Lord Collins said this applied where an individual had received “a custodial or non-custodial sentence, a community order, a conditional caution, an absolute or conditional discharge or a fine” – with only simple cautions excluded.
The Labour peer Beverley Hughes highlighted that Collins’s written answer meant even the “non-payment of a parking fine” would invoke a mandatory refusal once dealt with by a court.
Willy Bach, another Labour peer, described the inclusion of absolute discharges – where a court rules that punishment is unnecessary – as “completely extraordinary” and “the best example of all … as to why this really shouldn’t stand”.
However, the Conservative peer Elizabeth Berridge said it was a standard “point of principle” across many international jurisdictions that citizens were treated differently from offenders who were not yet citizens.
The Home Office minister Jo White said: “Under the new requirements for settlement, where a person has been convicted in the UK for an offence committed when they were aged 18 or above, their application for settlement will be refused, irrespective of the seriousness of the offence or how long ago it was committed.”
Downing Street declined to comment.
