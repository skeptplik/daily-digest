A ding-dong has divided the town of La Garnache in western France over attempts to silence the bells of the local church, which strike all night.
Since 1942, the clock tower of Notre-Dame de l’Assomption has chimed an eighth-century hymn every 15 minutes … day and night. Between 10pm and 7am, the bells ring 37 times, adding up to more than 700 individual chimes.
The town’s traditionalists say the sound is part of France’s cultural heritage and accuse incomers of not doing their research before moving to the town. Those living within earshot say all they want is a good night’s sleep.
Now an attempt to silence the bells has failed. A vote to decide their fate resulted in only 749 of the town’s 5,515 residents turning out, of whom three-quarters voted for the bells to continue.
“The town hall has noted the result and thanks all those of La Garnache who took part,” officials said in a statement.
The six bells of the carillon – separate from the church’s three main bells – were installed in September 1942. They play a hymn composed by Saint Louis-Marie Grignion de Montfort, a Breton priest who in 1711 passed through La Garnache, south-west of Nantes.
The carillon is a complex mechanical work of springs and cogs in which the bells are tapped by hammers. It is one of only a handful believed to remain in France and is described as “fragile”.
At 15 minutes past the hour, it plays a quarter of the hymn; at half past, it plays half of it; and at 45 minutes, three-quarters. On the hour, neighbours are treated to the whole melody, plus a ring for the hour. Each hymn section lasts only seconds, but for those trying to sleep, particularly in a heatwave with the windows open, it is a nightmare.
Thierry Simonin, 63, moved to the former rectory near the church in 2014.
“Between 10pm and 7am it rings 726 times,” he told Ouest-France. “In summer, we have to choose between being hot or hearing the bells and not sleeping. It’s not at all restful.”
Simonin launched a petition and collected dozens of signatures and letters of support, all submitted to the town hall with a request to silence the bells at night.
A former town mayor, François Petit, who opposes moves to silence the bells and organised the poll, has little sympathy for the petitioners.
“All the people who are speaking out about this arrived here after the bells. Perhaps those who live next to the church didn’t do their research in advance before moving there,” Petit told journalists.
He said the carillon and its chimes were part of the town’s history. Although the clock loses time and frequently breaks down, it could not be turned on and off. “It has springs and weights that cannot be easily stopped,” he said.
Romain Menet, a La Garnache resident, told French TV reporters: “It’s good the bells ring the whole time. It’s part of French heritage.”
Jérôme Déporté agreed: “It’s never bothered me and I’ve lived here for 27 years. Perhaps in the beginning, but not at all now. It’s not a subject that preoccupies me.”
The division over La Garnache’s bells is the latest wrangle over rural “noise nuisances” that have pitted longtime country residents against their newly arrived neighbours.
In 2019, a court ruled that Maurice the noisy cockerel could keep crowing after holiday-home owners on the Ile d’Oléron, off the country’s west coast, sued in an attempt to shut him up.
Two years later, the French parliament passed a “sensory heritage” law that aimed to “define and protect the sensory heritage of the French countryside”, nicknamed the Maurice law. There have been legal cases over croaking frogs, noisy geese and farm manure smells. In Provence, tourists demanded the mayor of Beausset silence the chirping cicadas.
The law was reinforced by further legislation in 2024 which stated that if a rural activity, noise or odour was present before inhabitants had arrived to live there and had not become worse, they could not make any legal claim for noise nuisance.
