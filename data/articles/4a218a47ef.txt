Undisclosed AI use has affected more than two dozen court cases this year, prompting judges to complain about AI-generated witness statements, fictitious case law and nonexistent statutes, an investigation has revealed.
Proceedings where judges have been forced to deal with lawyers, police officers and claimants misusing the technology in 2026 include a high court dispute between tax lawyers marred by what the judge called “unacceptable” use of “generative AI hallucinations” and references to nonexistent cases.
Another involved a police officer in a grievous bodily harm case who used AI to write a witness statement so it would maximise the attacker’s sentence and “make a judge …weep”. A barrister in a $21m (£16m) high court dispute between two Nigerian oil companies made an argument based on a ruling – supposedly by a senior judge – that had been entirely invented by a hallucinating AI, leading the judge to warn of “the dangers of relying on the product of AI without verification”.
The spate of cases in which problems with AI tools have occurred in 2026 – at least 25, according to a trawl of published judgments by the Bureau of Investigative Journalism shared with the Guardian – comes despite a series of warnings from senior legal figures about the danger of the technology when used to cut corners and build cases. Brett Dixon, the president of the Law Society, told the Guardian AIs producing verbose case documents, particularly when non-lawyers represent themselves, is also taking up more of judges’ time, undermining efforts to clear court backlogs.
“You go from three pages from a lawyer … using human judgment to a rambling 60 pages from a commercial AI,” he said.
Last week, the supreme court judge Lord Sales also said the supreme court was growing “increasingly concerned” about AI and highlighted fears the technology could leave the legal profession deskilled.
“The danger is not simply that the machine gets the answer wrong,” he said. “The greater long-term danger is that human beings gradually lose the ability to know whether it has got the answer wrong.”
Cases affected by AI this year often involved people representing themselves, but also legal professionals increasingly using the technology to accelerate research, save time and increase earnings. Earlier this month the problem hit the Crown Prosecution Service when one of its lawyers cited an imaginary legal precedent.
Among the cases so far in 2026 a senior insolvency judge, Mark Mullen, discovered a junior solicitor had relied on an AI chatbot to research a point of law and took its invented quote from a fictitious statute. The judge examined the AI chat logs and discovered it had produced “nonsense” and warned in his judgment “AI has the potential to be wholly unreliable”.
In another case, the court of appeal considered whether to let a convicted criminal from Derbyshire challenge a four-year prison sentence for crimes including grievous bodily harm. The man had pleaded guilty after smashing his way through a glass patio door with a shovel to get into a house where the mother of his children had barricaded herself with a friend and her child. He threatened them with a large kitchen knife, fled and in the process broke a police officer’s shoulder.
The investigating police officer created a victim personal statement (VPS) by uploading a transcript of the mother’s interviews to Microsoft’s Copilot AI and prompting it “to achieve the highest sentence and the most tear-jerking VPS to make a judge or reader weep when reading”.
After the officer was removed from frontline duty the appeal court judges warned that “that no police officer should ever prepare a witness statement of any kind in this way”.
“We have seen how vulnerable the court system is to the introduction of AI,” said Brian Flanagan, associate professor at the school of law and criminology at Maynooth University. “It is always going to be very tempting and people will continue to produce fabricated authorities and that’s pretty serious.”
Last month, the Civil Justice Council recommended that witnesses of fact in civil cases must sign a declaration that their statements had not been prepared through the use of an AI tool that had generated or influenced its substantive content – including facts, reasoning, arguments and opinions. The CJC’s working group, led by Sir Colin Birss, the second most senior judge in England and Wales, also highlighted the urgent need for guidance on the use of AI by non-lawyers who are representing themselves in the courts – known as litigants in person.
A Ministry of Justice spokesperson said: “We’re aware of the growing use of AI in our courts and we’re taking steps to ensure any use of the technology is safe, appropriate, and properly controlled. That includes boosting the support given to courts through additional judges, legal officers, modern case management systems, and remote hearing technology.”
