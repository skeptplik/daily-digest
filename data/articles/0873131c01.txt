Constellation Energy – America’s largest operator of nuclear power plants – does not share Wall Street’s concerns that the Democrats will slow down data center development. On the company’s third-quarter earnings call last week, CEO Joe Dominguez was, for lack of a better word, unbothered as he addressed the blue wave.

“I do think we’ve seen a bit of an overreaction in the market to the election results based upon fear that Democrats won’t let data centers get built,” Dominguez said.
Many of the Democrats who seized control of the House and Senate in this month’s midterm elections ran on anti-data-center campaigns, which triggered a historic $2.5 trillion sell-off in AI-adjacent stocks that reached well beyond tech to the energy, construction, and materials sectors.
Up until a few years ago, many of Constellation’s nuclear power plants were in severe financial distress and in danger of shutting down early. But the data center development boom and its enormous demand for electricity have dramatically reversed the nuclear industry’s fate.
Now, Constellation and other companies like it are signing major deals with Big Tech companies to provide power to data centers. Microsoft, for instance, signed a deal with Constellation to fund the revival of a nuclear plant at Three Mile Island in Pennsylvania. (Three Mile Island is the site of what the Nuclear Regulatory Commission refers to as the “most serious accident in US commercial nuclear power plant operating history.”)
Constellation also signed major contracts in the third quarter to provide power to Amazon and Google data centers, Dominguez said.
Nuclear energy has had bipartisan support for years. The US Department of Energy classifies nuclear as a “clean” energy source because the act of operating a nuclear reactor does not directly produce carbon emissions. This makes nuclear an appealing investment to Big Tech companies that have to balance aggressive data center development with the ambitious climate goals they set long before the AI race took off. Still, nuclear energy has its issues. One of the biggest is nuclear waste, which the US government has not figured out how to dispose of at scale.
Big Nuclear and Big Tech also tout these deals as good for the economy and job creation.
“The market scare that people got last week was not lost on Democrats,” Dominguez said on the call. “They do not want to be the reason that the economy takes a nosedive by halting data center development.”
Of course, Constellation’s future is essentially staked on the data center buildout, and Dominguez’s job is to convince investors they have nothing to worry about.
In this instance, however, Dominguez really does seem genuinely unconcerned by the new Democratic majority in the federal government. It could be that he understands that, yes, while the Democrats won the midterms on promises of data center regulation and the blue wave included many new Democratic governors, data centers aren’t necessarily a strictly partisan issue. Some Democrats have opposed harsh measures like moratoriums — though on the flip slide, Constellation may also have to deal with challenges from the other side of the aisle.
In Virginia, some of the state lawmakers who have been the most vocal about data centers are actually Republican. Virginia Sen. Richard Stuart, a conservative Republican, has aggressively pursued data center regulation. Last month, Virginia Gov. Abigail Spanberger, a Democrat, denied Stuart’s request for a special session to address a concerning new report from the Virginia Department of Environmental Quality on the state’s groundwater supply, which has become strained amid rampant data center growth.

The fight against data centers so far has been highly fragmented and hyperlocal, but that could change soon. The Trump administration loves data centers, and the federal government is working on wresting some control over data center development away from the states. But even then, Democrats and Republicans at the federal level tend to view data centers as an inevitable part of the future that needs some guardrails rather than out-of-control corporate growth infringing on the average American’s quality of life.
Just look at the Bipartisan American Affordability and Jobs Act, which was co-sponsored by two Democrats. The Senate is expected to vote on a passage of a sweeping bill which is being marketed as a way to speed up the permitting process for new power plants and transmission lines and connect new data centers to the grid faster. New Mexico Sen. Martin Heinrich, one of the Democrats backing the bill, has touted its inclusion of provisions designed to protect utility customers from rising rates due to data centers, ensuring Big Tech pays its fair share of costs. Researchers at Columbia University SIPA’s Center on Global Energy Policy called the bill’s protections “mixed,” noting they do not account for wholesale capacity and energy prices, a “principal way” data center electricity demand can lead to higher household bills.
Senate Democrats are sharply divided on the bill, which critics say will fast-track data center projects at the expense of public input and environmental oversight and lead to a massive overbuild of energy projects that Americans will end up paying for.
On Constellation’s earnings call, Dominguez pointed out that Virginia and Illinois, states with Democratic governors already in place before the election, have seen “a great deal of data center development.”
“The bottom line is that Democrats care deeply about economic growth and they care very deeply about the many labor jobs that go with data center construction,” Dominguez said. “With labor such a key constituent for Democrats, it’s hard to imagine that we are going to halt work …There might be some differences and certainly there are going to be some local areas, I think, that are not going to want data centers.”
Of course, Dominguez is speaking to investors here, and he wants to assuage their concerns. He also has a good point. Grassroots efforts to stop local data center development have been successful and have contributed to broader opposition to data center development across the country. But data center developers can also abandon projects and go elsewhere. Until a few years ago, data centers were largely concentrated in Northern Virginia, but with the AI boom, developers have fanned out across the country in search of available power and land. A total of 38 states have welcomed them with open arms and generous tax breaks, per the National Conference of State Legislatures.

Thanks to the convoluted way the US electric grid and energy markets work, Constellation’s power plants don’t even need to be located in the same state as their data center customers — the plant supplying the power just needs to be connected to the same region from which the grid is drawing power. The purchase agreement, in that sense, is symbolic: Because the data center needs more electricity than it can draw from the local grid without causing disruptions for everyone else, the data center pays for independent power providers like Constellation to supply the grid with extra electricity.
That’s why data centers and the enormous amount of electricity Big Tech says it needs to power them have been such a boon for the nuclear industry. For years, companies like Constellation have been sitting on a bunch of old reactors teetering on the edge of extinction because they didn’t have enough customers to keep them afloat.
Nuclear lobbyists will tell you that’s because the NRC’s safety regulations are overly restrictive and lead to untenable upfront capital costs that make projects impossible to finance. (This has been an industry talking point since 1979, after the NRC seriously overhauled its safety standards following the partial meltdown at Three Mile Island. That site is now owned by Constellation, which has signed a 20-year deal with Microsoft.)
Both the Biden and Trump administrations have worked to support the development of nuclear power in the US by passing developer tax credits. The Trump administration has gone even further, pushing for a sweeping reform of the NRC that would make it much easier for new reactors to obtain operating licenses.
And the Department of Energy this month approved a $5 billion loan to Constellation, which the company will use to upgrade its existing nuclear fleet. Another nuclear energy company, Vistra, received a $4.2 billion loan.
It’s not totally unlike the Biden administration’s passage of the Civil Nuclear Credit Program in 2022, which planned to loan $6 billion to struggling nuclear companies in the US.
That’s not lost on Dominguez.
“Interestingly, in many respects the election had the Republicans saying a lot of the same things that the Democrats were saying,” Dominguez told investors on the call. “And I believe that the policy solutions are going to be somewhat similar.”
