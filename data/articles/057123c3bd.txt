Nobel Prize-winning economist Joseph Stiglitz says young people are right to be worried and that youth protests across Europe are "legitimate".
Hundreds of thousands have taken to the streets in France, Denmark, Italy and Spain in recent weeks to speak out against rising living costs, housing crisis and poor pay.
Nobel Prize-winning economist Joseph Stiglitz said he understands the youth's worries and that their concerns were "legitimate".
"Part of the problem [...] is inequality across generations," said Stiglitz.
"They can't afford housing. They're not given the education that would make them productive. AI is giving rise to new sets of anxieties," he said.
The former World Bank chief economist said that the world the next generation is inheriting is filled with uncertainties linked to rising inequality, climate change and other issues.
Protests have filled European countries' streets in recent months, fuelled by widening wealth gaps. "I think those are legitimate complaints," said Stiglitz. These pressures drive fear within younger generations that they may never match the living standards their parents enjoyed.
'I pay €460 for 5 square metres'
Last week, hundreds of thousands of protesters took to the streets in Spain for the first nationwide 24-hour strike since 2012.
Marching through pouring rain, protesters in Madrid carried signs chanting "we have no home", speaking out against rising living costs.
"People work a lot, and it isn't enough to pay the rent, to pay for a house," said Paula Diaz, a 25-year-old theatre technician who lives with her parents.
Arturo Leon, 24, is a student in Barcelona and pays €460 a month for a 5-square-metre bedroom in a shared apartment with four others. "We do not have a contract, and there are lots of things in the flat which are broken, but the landlord refuses to fix," he told Al Jazeera.

'We don't want mice in our schools'
In Italy, thousands of students called for more school funding as they marched, set up tent camps and occupied schools to make their demands heard.
"We don't want windows falling on our heads, and we don't want mice in our schools. We want clean buildings, and I want to live in a better world than this one," said Sofia Cataldi, a 16-year-old Roman student.
This call for a better life sounds like an echo of student protests that have been rumbling in France since the beginning of September.
"The state of schools, the state of hospitals, staffing levels in schools and hospitals, the way things are getting worse and worse across all public services — these are issues that really resonate with us," said Aurelane Thos, a 38-year-old nurse.
"We're stressed about our studies, but when you have a bunch of cops in front of the high school every morning, you only want to keep protesting," said Mathilde, a 17-year-old pupil in Paris.
Stiglitz backs taxing the rich
The 83-year-old American economist has been a long-time advocate of taxing the ultra-wealthy. He argues that governments would have room to respond to social demands if they were willing to raise more revenue.
"There is so much money at the top that even a small tax rate on the top generates a huge amount of money," he said.
Stiglitz backs proposals such as the suggestion by French economist Gabriel Zucman for a minimum levy of around two per cent of fortunes above €100 million.
Such tax revenues would allow more money to be spent on public services and should be levied at the European Union level. He argues the bloc's free movement of people and capital made tax avoidance easier.
But the Columbia University professor warned that tax alone won't solve Europe's challenges. European countries should rethink parts of their public spending and implement more flexible pension systems that reflect longer life expectancy, while taking into account physically demanding jobs.
"Students may not fully understand," Stiglitz said, that beyond the usual budget pressures, "this is a particularly difficult time."
