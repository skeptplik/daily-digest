In the Andromeda Galaxy, a giant star about seven times as massive as the Sun spent nearly a decade slowly swallowing its much smaller companion. The process, known as common envelope evolution, was proposed in the 1970s to explain how some of the tightest pairs of stars in the Universe form, but no one had ever caught it in the act.
In a new Nature study, astronomers led by Viraj Karambelkar, an astrophysicist at Columbia University, finally witnessed the entire spectacle from the decades before it began to the eruption that ended it in late 2025. “It lasts for a few years, maybe a few decades,” Karambelkar said. “Stars evolve on time scales of millions of years. So, it’s really like a blip on the entire cosmic time scale.”
Shared envelopes
Some pairs of stars orbit so close to each other that they could not have formed at that distance, so their orbits must have shrunk. Common envelope evolution is the leading explanation for that shrinkage. When one star in a binary grows old, it expands into a giant, and its outer layers can engulf its companion. The two then share a single, loosely bound envelope of gas. As the engulfed star drags through it, it loses orbital energy to friction and spirals inward. Depending on how much of the envelope gets lost, the pair either ends up in a much tighter orbit or merges.
Karambelkar’s team spotted a candidate system where this might be happening while searching Andromeda for stars changing in brightness, using the Wide-field Infrared Transient Explorer (WINTER) telescope at Palomar Observatory and data from NASA’s Near-Earth Object Wide-field Infrared Survey Explorer (NEOWISE) mission. One giant had been brightening for seven years. “When a star first swallows its companion, it has a very subtle effect on its brightness,” Karambelkar said. “It slowly brightens, and a lot of light comes out in the infrared.”
