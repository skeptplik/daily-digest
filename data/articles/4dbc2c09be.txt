Oddly enough, this video game weighs in on Canada's fighter jet options
Ace Combat ranks F-35 above Gripen — both being considered by Ottawa
The gaming industry might not be the first place to look for military procurement advice, but a recently released video game offers a subtle suggestion for what Canada should pick for its new fighter jet fleet.
Ace Combat 8: Wings of Theve was released in early October. The series, which produced its first game in 1995, is set in a fictional world but uses real-life jets for players to control.
As players progress through the game, they're able to unlock higher-caliber aircraft. Both the American-made F-35 and Swedish Gripen-E, which the federal government is considering purchasing, are available.
The F-35 is ranked near the top of the game's aircraft tree, ahead of the Gripen.
Andy Lee, a doctoral candidate at the University of Toronto who studies video games and military simulations, said it's "super common" for games to have a system where players unlock new equipment as they progress.
"Tech trees are viewed as one of many ways to maintain retention, so there's something to invest in so that you keep playing beyond just narrative completion," she said.
"You're making choices as you progress through the game, which gives you some sense of personalization as well."
Lee said it's likely the developers, Bandai Namco, consulted defence experts. But she added that the game is not entirely realistic either — given that the company needs to make it playable for gamers.
"Ace Combat is sort of a flight simulator, but it's an arcade-y one, right? What I mean by that is that it's not beholden to total realism. It's supposed to be fun," she said.
"But for the most part, these developers are knowledgeable and they're not simply going off of their gut. So when they balance things from a gameplay perspective, it is probably weighted in some way based around real-world, technical specifications."
The company has to get permission and licenses to use the manufacturer's product in the game and Lee said that likely includes a fee. CBC News asked Bandai Namco for an interview but didn't receive a response.

For Dave Perry, a defence analyst and president of the Canadian Global Affairs Institute, it's not surprising that the game ranked the two jets where it did.
"I think by pretty much all assessments [the F-35] aircraft's got a lot more capability than alternatives on the market, including the Gripen," Perry said.
The Canadian Global Affairs Institute gets sponsorship support from defence contractors including Lockheed Martin, manufacturer of the F-35, and Saab, which makes the Gripen.
The F-35 has distinct advantages in being more compatible with other defence products widely used in the North American defence industry and capabilities that aren't available in the Gripen, Perry said, comparing the two fighters to cellphones to make his point.
"F-35 would be the equivalent of current version flagship smartphones. The Gripens are analogous to a much earlier model," he said.
"It'll still make the calls, you can probably do some texting, some other things. But it doesn't have the full product suite and offerings that the flagship model of iPhone does right now — and it isn't the Apple product that works in the Apple ecosystem."
Data obtained by Radio-Canada earlier this year showed National Defence Department testing of both jets in 2021 put the F-35 far ahead of the Gripen.
Prime Minister Mark Carney paused plans to proceed with a planned order for 88 of F-35s in response to diplomatic and trade tensions with the U.S. President Donald Trump's administration.
Under an agreement announced in 2023, Ottawa already has a firm order for 16 F-35s, and has since started to pay for 14 additional aircraft.
Perry noted that there are likely political considerations going into the government's deliberations. But he said that the Gripen does have some advantages that the F-35 doesn't, specifically that it is easy to maintain or alter depending on mission needs.
A Swedish Gripen fighter being fitted with air-to-air missiles during a training exercise in Iceland. Canada is considering purchasing the Swedish fighter jet. (Murray Brewster/CBC)
"You can change all kinds of things on that aircraft, including an engine, with just a few people and a relatively rudimentary pack of tools," he said of the Gripen.
Sources have previously told Radio-Canada that the government is considering a larger mixed fleet of both jets. Saab has offered to build the new jets in Canada, which could mean thousands of new jobs in the country.
The Carney government announced earlier this year its intention to also purchase six of Saab's GlobalEye surveillance jets that would be used to to monitor the Arctic for missiles, drone attacks or enemy incursions. Sources told CBC News earlier this fall that the air force was concerned the GlobalEye meets only a handful of the military's requirements.
When it comes to the current choice of jet fighters, Lee said the government likely isn't going to look at Ace Combat when making its decision. But she noted that simulation technologies are used by governments for training and considering new equipment.
"The line between actual defence simulating software and commercial video games is sometimes pretty thin," she said.
Perry quipped that the government has been dragging its feet on making a final decision that it might as well consult the new game.
"This most recent review of Canada's fighter purchases has gone on so long that maybe they're actually seeing how video gamers assess this aircraft. They've certainly had enough time to look at every conceivable type of analysis, conventional or not," he said.
