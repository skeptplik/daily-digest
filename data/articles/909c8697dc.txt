There are few things in this world that are more universal than grief. And yet, each person’s own particular grief can feel as unique as the person they are grieving for. I have been in academia for decades, but have heard little about grief in that time, so I wanted to share a few tips that helped me get through the early stages of grief when my mother died last year.
My mother grew up in deep poverty in Taiwan and she worked relentlessly throughout her life to give me and my siblings many opportunities. She was the first in her family to go to college, and at 23 years old, she moved to Canada to work as a nurse. After a few years of enduring Canadian winters, she had had enough and moved to sunny California. She met my dad there, and a few years later she gave birth to my sister, then me and then my brother. She raised us while working several jobs as a nurse. She cheered the three of us on throughout our lives. That’s not to say that she ever fully understood my career: she once described my work (I am an evolutionary biologist, specializing in birds and bacteria, at the University of California, Los Angeles) as “counting birds”.
She was adventurous, but between her family and work responsibilities, she didn’t have much time to travel. So, once she retired, I took her on fieldwork and conference trips with me whenever I could — to China, the Netherlands, Italy, Mexico, Japan and throughout the United States.
When her grandchildren came along, she helped them to learn how to read, to do maths and to speak Chinese. In the last years of her life, I had a small house built for her in our back garden in Los Angeles, so she would always be close to me. When you lose someone who is extremely close to you, your world is never the same.
In the weeks and months after she died, I found myself in a fog of shock and grief. I felt the need to be moving constantly: running, walking the dog, cleaning the house. At times, I could not be certain of my ability to conduct the high-level abstract and conceptual thinking that academia often requires, so I turned to Trevor Price, my former PhD adviser at the University of California, San Diego (who is now at the University of Chicago, Illinois). I explained my brain fog and deep grief, and he read over my work and made sure that I didn’t miss any crucial scientific arguments in some of the papers I was writing. Although I graduated more than two decades ago, he did not hesitate for one second to help me.
People won’t know that you need help until you reach out. And the earlier you do it, the more time and energy you can give yourself and the easier it will be for others to help.
Here are some tips that helped me to navigate the academic world while grieving.
Find out what assistance your institution offers
Ideally, you should determine what support is available before it is needed. Institutions often have all sorts of resources and benefits for people experiencing grief. These might include time off for a bereavement, leave for family care, counselling and a break from teaching classes. I never knew, until my mother passed away, that I could have taken paid leave to take care of her while she was unwell: she had a debilitating stroke five months before she died. Make sure you understand (and ask questions) about your work contract and what your employer offers.
Talk to your colleagues and plan for worst-case scenarios
Sometimes, a family member’s illness can occur suddenly, and other times it might start off as one condition and snowball into something else. Talk to the chair of your department and to your colleagues as early as possible about getting cover for your courses and committees. Be creative and brainstorm which other people — including your colleagues, former advisers and former students and postdocs — might be able to step in and give your conference talks, guest lecture for your classes and conduct other activities that you might need help with.
I learnt this the hard way. I did not reach out to people early enough and was unrealistically optimistic about my mother’s health trajectory. While I waited for her to get better, I was so panicked about the thought of losing her that I became incapacitated. I couldn’t focus and, at times, I could barely do simple tasks such as answering e-mails, or making appointments for meetings. I told myself as soon as she was better, which would be any day now, I would catch up. And so, I didn’t have a realistic plan in place when she died.
Don’t expect perfection from yourself
You have to accept that you might not be at your best when it comes to research productivity, mentoring and teaching while you’re grieving. Ask someone to help with advising your students, and rely on colleagues or former students. You might have to reduce the frequency with which you meet your students, hold laboratory meetings and work with collaborators, and resort to advising through e-mail and video calls. And you might have to give less detailed comments on your students’ writing. I did all of the above during the first few weeks and months of grieving. I had an especially hard time giving advice to my graduate students who were doing field work. Ellie Diamant, a former PhD student of mine who is now a biologist at Bard College in Annandale-On-Hudson, New York, generously stepped in and met with many of my students. It took me a while to understand and accept that I simply wasn’t going to meet the normal standards I set for myself while going through this difficult time.
