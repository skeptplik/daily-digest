Most luxury retailers rely on international custom, but not Beymen. Now 55 years in business, Türkiye’s answer to Harrods — located across the country — is growing rapidly, boosted by local demand. And last weekend, the retailer held an opulent two-day event to celebrate the first anniversary of its flagship Tersane location, alongside the opening of a new luxury handbag exhibition, “Timeless Treasures”. As part of the program, Beymen invited guests including interior designer Harry Nuriev, as well as fashion designers Giuseppe Zanotti, Giuseppe Santoni and Antonin Tron, to a black-tie gala for a dedicated speech from Beymen Group CEO Elif Çapçı and a special performance from Diana Ross, alongside other festivities.
As the lavish party suggests, despite a recent luxury slowdown, Beymen has fared well, according to Çapçı. This year, the group is close to making €1.5 billion, growing at 15% in the first nine months. (The group — a privately owned company — declined to share further figures.)
Following investment from Mayhoola — the Qatari fund that owns Valentino and Balmain — in 2019, Beymen Group has staged a national expansion in multi-brand retail, as well as direct-to-consumer (DTC) partnerships with major European luxury brands.
What attracted Mayhoola? “The Turkish market is a very important and pivotal market between the Middle East and Europe,” says Rachid Mohamed Rachid, Mayhoola CEO and chairman for Valentino and Balmain, noting the country’s size and relatively strong luxury consumption and youth presence. “The way the country is growing economically — quite an aggressive growth in the last 20 years — has given this market a level of importance.” It’s the Sunday morning following the gala and the executive sits opposite me, beside Çapçı. The three of us are inside a plush personal shopping salon on the top floor of Beymen’s Tersane location, a bespoke flagship situated on the northern shore of Türkiye’s Golden Horn — a historic waterway dividing the old and new city.
At present, Beymen owns 290 stores in various formats across Türkiye and Egypt (a small operation): 25 multi-brand, around 60 franchise stores with leading designer brands, and three of its own brands, with stores across the region. And progress is ongoing. Current investments include a third Valentino store, as well as a Ferragamo, Pucci and Toteme store. This year, Beymen also opened a new multi-brand beauty outpost — under the Beymen Beauty Stores name — following two new additions last year. (Beauty is a fast-growing category for the business, confirms Çapçı.)
Now, the retailer is capitalizing on its national market share through enhanced clienteling, store experiences and cultural programming, while navigating the nation’s limited supply of luxury products. “Over the last [few] years with Mayhoola at the helm, we have been increasing our focus on becoming a true luxury player and raising the bar in how we offer luxury, our customer experience, the value proposition,” says Çapçı, noting the firm’s expertise and support in enhancing store renovations, brand partnerships, as well as VIC clienteling and customer management. “It’s not only how you do stores and visual merchandizing, but how you do events, how you become more associated with culture, art, and social responsibility.”
Beymen is about to celebrate the Tersane store’s first anniversary, and has mounted “Timeless Treasures” to mark the moment. The showcase features 55 global brands’ interpretations of the Anatolian envelope-shaped bag, including Ganni and Louis Vuitton, plus some carefully selected student designers. The company considers this a positioning exercise, bridging the gap between local consumers and international luxury houses, while signaling Beymen’s investment in local heritage and creative industries. “Timeless Treasures” follows another landmark exhibition rooted in heritage, “Golden Opulence” (2023). This cultural programming, Rachid tells Vogue Business, helps showcase the last 500 years of Türkiye’s history. “I think the Turkish consumers are very proud of their country,” he says.
According to Çapçı, every multi-brand store in the Beymen portfolio has been built from scratch. This localized approach reflects a local consumer base. Indeed, 85% shoppers are domestic, bolstered by a small percentage of foreign visitors from Gulf countries, Russia, and ex-Russian republics. “We are not a big believer of multi-brand luxury traveling well,” Çapçı says.
Betting on Beymen
Beymen has a strong market share in Türkiye, counting only a handful of competitors that specialize in international luxury. Communité is one, offering a brand assortment that spans Willy Chavarria and Proenza Schouler through to Dries Van Noten and Carolina Herrera. Vakko is another, stocking Comme Des Garçons Play, Oscar de la Renta and Thom Browne. Besides these, Türkiye’s fashion landscape is more typically associated with its factories and fabrics, and a strong domestic market for demi couture, dominated by DTC brands such as Özgür Masur and Amor Garibovic. Beymen’s range spans Christian Louboutin, Prada and Fendi to Canali, Kiton and Tod’s.
Rachid invested because he saw white space. “Although the country had been historically a very important supplier of fashion in terms of textiles and leather goods, [it wasn’t as] developed in the design and the luxury segment,” he says. “It was really an opportunity for us to define that, while also tapping the market’s consumption potential.”
Per Euromonitor, luxury goods in Türkiye demonstrated stable growth last year, as the sector recovered despite macroeconomic challenges. Inflation — exacerbated by the Middle East crisis — remained elevated, eating into discretionary spending among middle-income groups. As in other markets, high-net-worth individuals (HNWIs) are leading spending. And Beymen is able to harness them to weather the retail storm.
A localized operation
In-store sales represents 70% of Beymen’s revenue, with the remainder coming from e-commerce. For its Beymen brand chains, the split is 80% in-store versus 20% online. In tune with global consumer sentiment, the group’s wealthiest shoppers expect distinguished shopping experiences.
In this vein, the Tersane store has been designed as a destination retail experience. Beymen partnered with the Office for Metropolitan Architecture (OMA) — the Rem Koolhaas-founded firm behind several Prada locations — to create a refreshingly bright and airy department store, channeling sunshine from the adjacent waterway. Despite its clean, modular design spread across nine distinct sections, the Ottoman-era shipyard building has kept many of its heritage features. Throughout the 12,000-square-meter space, Beymen has placed works from its own art collection.
“We believe that success is dependent on both understanding a market and your roots in that market,” says Rachid. “I believe department stores are local businesses. I don’t believe that you can take a department store and move it from one country to another. You have an experience when you go to Beymen in Türkiye, another experience when you go to Harrods in London, and another when you go to Galeries Lafayette in France.”
Çapçı compares Beymen to big European businesses that rely on fluctuating foreign income. She doesn’t cite specific examples, but it’s widely understood that London’s West End — Harvey Nichols, Selfridges, Harrods — rely heavily on Chinese and Middle-Eastern custom. For Çapçı, it’s a risky approach, which is why she’s keen to keep expansion plans domestic. (To her point, China’s spending has slowed following a real-estate crisis, and Middle Eastern spending has dipped following ongoing conflicts.)
Building a client legacy
While VICs lead luxury spending at Beymen, the company particularly values its aspirational and accessible luxury shoppers, despite the smaller ticket sizes. Beymen’s VIC clienteling has been enhanced by Mayhoola’s investment and its savoir-faire gleaned from brand-specific experience. (Mayhoola owns 100% of Balmain, Beymen and Pal Zileri, and 70% of Valentino.)
Well before the acquisition, however, Beymen had established itself on intergenerational client relations. “This used to be a much smaller business, where you knew the clients by name. We are still keeping that,” says Çapçı, noting Türkiye’s culturally recognized heritage in hospitality. “We know the families. We go into their homes. If they like something, [we] just send it. They keep what they like. They send the rest [back]. They pay later.” Some clients have shopped with Beymen for multiple decades, adds Çapçı.
Beymen estimates its target consumer at 35-plus, however the company focuses on communicating and selling to Gen Z, perceiving this cohort as an investment for the future. The company also sees Gen Z as the opinion leaders who influence older generations. Rachid emphasizes that the Turkish population is young and dynamic, sitting on the intersection of the Middle East, Europe, Central Asia and the Mediterranean. “You can feel it,” he says, taking a sip of Turkish tea. “I think that that has a strong impact on the character of the consumption here. People are open to new ideas, they are open to diversity.”
On a granular level, Rachid adds, the Turkish consumer comes to stores with a strong understanding of workmanship and quality. The proximity to traditional craft and textile design makes the Beymen consumer discerning. “They’re not only going after flashy, big names,” he explains. “They have a very strong appreciation and knowledge. Don’t forget that one of the biggest attractions in Istanbul is the Grand Bazaar.”
Looking ahead, Rachid wants to prioritize the service each consumer receives over international and national store expansion, focusing on interactions and experience. “I think this is really the competitive advantage of Beymen,” he says.
