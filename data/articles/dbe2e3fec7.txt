Nearly 700,000 women across the UK are to be invited to join the world's largest trial of how artificial intelligence can help in breast cancer screening.
AI will be used to review mammograms to see if it can improve early cancer detection and ease the workload of clinical staff, while not over-diagnosing women unnecessarily.
Recruitment to the EDITH trial , externalhas already begun at Cambridge University Hospitals, the first of 30 sites across the UK.
Women aged 50 to 71 are invited for routine breast screening every three years in the UK, and more than two million attend annually.
At present two NHS experts look at each breast X-ray, known as a mammogram, but in the trial, one of the human readers will be replaced by AI.
Several smaller studies have already shown that AI can improve breast cancer detection rates, but researchers running this trial say they need to be sure it won't cause harm by leading to women being recalled unnecessarily for further tests.
Sian Taylor-Phillips, professor of population health at the University of Warwick, and co-chief investigator said: "AI-assisted technology could help improve early cancer detection but we want to avoid the overdiagnosis of milder disease that would never have caused symptoms."
Breast cancer is the most common cancer among women in the UK, with around 59,000 cases diagnosed each year.
About a third of these are picked up by routine mammograms, usually at an early stage when they can be more easily treated.
It's estimated the screening programme helps prevent around 1,300 deaths a year.
Prof Fiona Gilbert, department of radiology, University of Cambridge, honorary consultant at Addenbrooke's Hospital, and co-chief investigator said: "Millions of women have a mammogram each year and only a small number, less than 1%, will have cancer.
"Technology can make reviewing these images more efficient, freeing up specialist breast radiologists to spend more time caring for patients with cancer, supporting faster diagnosis and earlier treatment."
Gilbert told the BBC there was a "huge shortfall" in radiologists and that the number of imaging examinations was increasing by 8% a year.
Screening 'saved my life'
Shirley Essex, 70, from Cambridgeshire, was diagnosed with early-stage breast cancer a year ago after a routine screening appointment.
She had surgery and radiotherapy, but did not require chemotherapy.
She told the BBC that her mammogram had saved her life: "I was astonished to be told I had cancer because I did not have any symptoms."
Shirley is not part of the study but is passionate about the importance of screening.
"I feel so lucky my cancer was detected early. It was my last routine mammogram, so if I had not attended, my cancer would have kept growing," she said.
How will the trial work?
Women will be informed about the study when they receive a routine screening invitation, but will not be obliged to take part.
The trial will not change what happens during a breast screening appointment.
Instead, it will test whether AI-assisted tools could replace one of the two human specialists reviewing mammograms.
Researchers say machine-learning software can be trained on millions of previous images in order to detect patterns, and so potentially find subtle changes in breast tissue that might be missed by the human eye.
In one arm of the trial, an AI programme will grade images before they are sent to NHS experts. In another, it will review images afterwards.

It is being led by the University of Warwick, the University of Cambridge and Cambridge University Hospitals NHS Foundation Trust, working with NHS England.
'Huge potential'
On a visit to the breast screening unit at Addenbrooke's Hospital, Health Secretary Yvette Cooper told the BBC: "AI has huge potential to improve treatment, which is why we need trials like this to ensure we are using it in the most effective way with proper safeguards."
Prof Peter Johnson, national clinical director for cancer at NHS England, said NHS breast screening "saves lives" and he urged every woman invited to the trial to take part.
"Every scan will still be seen by a specialist, and if proven effective, it could ensure women get the diagnosis and treatment they need sooner," he said.
Four AI companies are involved in the trial, which is expected to last around three years.
