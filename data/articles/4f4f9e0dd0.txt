Why Holiday Security Can't Wait for Black Friday
Bot-driven fraud and holiday social engineering attacks stretch across many weeks, straining security teams and small businesses alike.
Cyber experts have long drawn connections between the accelerated volumes of holiday shopping and threat actors looking for opportunities to take advantage, but as the festive season kicks off, it's important to remember that security posture during this time period is a marathon — not a sprint.
Verizon today published survey results for its holiday trends report, tracking the insights of 600 small business owners and decision-makers in the US. Of those surveyed, 11% said they have dealt with a cyberattack or data-security incident during the holidays, and a third expect to increase cybersecurity investments this holiday season compared with last year.
Meanwhile, managed security services firm VikingCloud this week published a report surveying security and IT leaders which made even steeper claims: a full 86% of respondents said their business has faced at least one successful cyberattack in the past 12 months, and a whopping 98% said at least one came during the 2025 holiday season. And the Retail & Hospitality Information Sharing and Analysis Center (RH-ISAC) last year said the holiday season continues to be the most active fraud environment from a defender standpoint.
Some reasons behind the prominence of cyberattacks during this time period are straightforward: Many cybersecurity teams experience lighter staffing during holiday periods and weekends (leaving the door open for easier ransomware attacks). The chaos of the holiday season also means online services and retailers are inundated with activity, giving threat actors cover. And on the social engineering front, the holidays give plenty of pretext for threat actors conducting phishing campaigns (fake emails promising unbelievable Black Friday deals, charity scams, and so on).
Some teams may take this as a cue to prepare for a few select days or weeks of this last part of the year, such as Black Friday or the week leading up to Christmas. But new research around one narrow corner of the threat landscape suggests that may not be wise.
Happy Holidays! It's Your Own Box of Bots!
One of the less-discussed types of threat activity retailers face each season is an influx of malicious bots.
F5 today published a study dedicated to automated threat activity leading up to Black Friday. The report analyzed bot and automation traffic across three Black Friday shopping seasons (2023 to 2025) using data from F5 Distributed Cloud Bot Defense. It covers retailers in three retail sub-industries: fashion, e-commerce, and quick-service restaurants (QSR). The report's authors observed that overall automation rose across the three seasons, from 17.4% of transactions in 2023 to 18.5% in 2024, and then 28.6% in 2025.
The types of activity differ across industries, but F5 observed bots being used for product scraping and inventory hoarding, credential stuffing and account takeover, fake account creation (to claim new user offers), and gift card cracking (inserting gift card numbers into balance checkers to test for valid accounts).
In terms of trends overall, cyberattackers are increasingly leaning toward Web stores over mobile APIs, and a broad rise in bot sophistication over time. But one of the most notable conclusions that F5 researchers made is that there's no single best date to defend. Attackers start their bot onslaught weeks before Black Friday, and continue it well into the weeks after.
As Sean Murphy, F5's field chief information security officer (CISO) for North America, tells Dark Reading, "The data shows that automated activity can be elevated well before the shopping weekend, and the busiest day for automation varied dramatically from year to year."
Holiday Defense Requires Weeks, Not Days, of Effort
Bot traffic does not necessarily reflect all threat activity, but there may be a broader lesson here: that steeling your security hygiene for the holiday season is not a few single days or weekends to prepare for but a broader stretch of time that spans from early November to the end of the year. Murphy says security teams "should have monitoring and protections in place throughout the shopping season rather than scaling them up only for Thanksgiving week."
For those in ecommerce dealing with Web and mobile APIs, he urges ensuring protection isn't substantially weaker on one than the other.
"Web traffic had much higher automation rates among the retailers studied, but the report specifically cautions against interpreting that as evidence that APIs are inherently safer. Where API protection is weaker, attackers can shift toward that channel," he says. "And because the dataset contains both simple scripts and automation designed to imitate legitimate users, defenses need to account for a spectrum of bot sophistication rather than relying on a single detection technique."
Finally, he recommends organizations prioritize targeted employee security awareness and training, particularly for those teams handling customer accounts, payments, and other sensitive information. "Many attacks still begin with social engineering and credential theft, so employees need to know how to recognize suspicious requests and protect account access."
Chris Flood, senior vice president and CRO of Verizon Business, tells Dark Reading that small businesses can reduce their risk with four fairly simple steps: require multifactor authentication, train employees how to spot scams, keep guest Wi-Fi separate from payment systems, and keep software and devices updated.
