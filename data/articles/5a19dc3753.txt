Talks to give Britain access to the EU's electricity market are under way, but Germany, Belgium and the Netherlands are among those insisting London pays in first.
Millions of British households will pay around £443 (€527) more a year for energy from January, after UK regulator Ofgem raised its price cap by 26%, blaming the war in the Middle East.
The typical annual bill will rise to about £2,166 (€2,578) under the new cap. Ofgem sets the cap every quarter, and it limits what suppliers can charge for gas and electricity.
About 20 million households on standard variable tariffs will feel the rise. Another 10 million on fixed-rate deals won't be affected until their contracts end.
"Conflict in the Middle East continues to drive energy costs higher, and we know this price increase will be a worry for many households as winter approaches," said Neil Kenward, Ofgem's director general for markets.
The US-Iran war broke out on 28 February and has sharply reduced tanker traffic through the Strait of Hormuz, pushing up oil and gas prices. European natural gas, measured by the Dutch TTF futures contract, has climbed by around 190% since then, from about €32 to €92 per megawatt-hour.
How UK bills compare with Europe
From January, British households will pay around 70% more for electricity than the average household in the EU.
Once standing charges are included, a typical British household will pay about €48.40 per 100 kWh, based on XE's exchange rate of £1 = €1.19. The EU average was €28.30 in the first half of 2026, according to Eurostat, and the most expensive country in the bloc, Ireland, charged €38.70.
The gap is narrower for gas. British households will pay about €14.40 per 100 kWh, against an EU average of €11.00.
The UK is already in talks with Brussels to join the EU's internal electricity market, a move supporters say would boost energy security and make electricity trading easier. But five EU countries, including Germany, Belgium and the Netherlands, have demanded that Britain contribute financially to cohesion funds, which aim to narrow gaps between EU regions, in return, Euronews reported earlier this month.
Martin McCluskey, the UK's energy minister, said the UK is still working to understand the EU's proposal and that no amount has been agreed. McCluskey added that any payment must deliver "value for money."
The price rise comes despite support measures announced in October in Prime Minister Andy Burnham's annual budget. These included a one-off £100 payment (€119) towards energy bills for 20 million households and the removal of fixed fees on gas bills. Burnham's government has also extended a temporary tax cut on household electricity prices.
Many households will still end up paying more even after the subsidy.
The increase is also likely to push inflation higher next year. The UK's annual inflation rate hit a 12-month high of 3.6% in October, driven by higher motor fuel costs linked to the conflict.
Gas prices are still well below their peak during the 2022 energy crisis, which followed Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine. The biggest increase to the price cap came in October 2022, when it went up by 80%.
