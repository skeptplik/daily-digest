OpenAI just revealed new details about its ongoing partnership with AMD, in which engineers used undisclosed AI models to help optimize the hardware maker's upcoming flagship GPU and CPU designs. The leading figures are up to 14% PPA (power, performance, and area) improvement for the GPU circuitry, and unspecified single-digit PPA gains on the processor.
OpenAI says that its model spotted "further improvements by rewriting the code that describes the chip’s circuits." This work was done in SystemVerilog RTL, the language commonly used for high-end chip design. The firm claims that its models' optimization efforts are equivalent to condensing a year's worth of conventional chip design work into "under 5 months" — a time crunch it says is unprecedented in this arena.
Furthermore, OpenAI says its model was reasoning on RTL "at the level of an expert microarchitect," and that it expects to squeeze further PPA improvements in both GPU and CPU designs, up from the now-improved baseline.
This isn't AI's first time working on chips. It worked with Broadcom on designing OpenAI Jalapeño, an inference-targeted chip that was already running actual workloads with engineering samples as far back as June 2026.
The company expected to have small initial Jalapeño deployments around late 2026, and pinned a broader rollout to 2027. Test benchmarks have been out for a while, displaying purported excellent savings on power draw and overall latency versus the market-leading Nvidia Blackwell GPUs in single-token prediction workloads.
The blog post also explains how GPT's chip-design acumen evolved. The researchers tested its high-end LLMs by asking them to improve PicoRV32 and Ibex chip cores. The models were given 112,000 tokens per run and told to optimize for throughput (a combination of Instructions-per-Clock and clock cycle duration), and scored the results.
A fat zero was given if the chips didn't work or used more than 16x the previous area, and 1.0 was given for the best designs under the test constraints. The test also didn't tell the models which workloads the chips were going to be used for. As expected, GPT-6.2 Astra had by far the best showing, on both axes: both the lowest overall cost and the highest optimization score.

A second test also revealed an interesting but unsurprising fact: the models provided better results when essentially redesigning the chips according to their initial specifications, rather than optimizing existing code.
There's no word on whether the scientists used unmodified versions of GPT to perform this work. Task-specific high-end LLMs exist for those who can afford them, and OpenAI itself says it's working with Synopsys to train GPT-Synopsis, a model for using chip design tools.
