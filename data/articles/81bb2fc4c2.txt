In September, Pavel Rabtsevich sat down at his computer and asked an AI model to discover a planet. Rabtsevich directed the bot to a specific batch of telescope data and gave it a high-level plan to hunt for evidence of undetected planets. In about two weeks, the bot delivered the goods: two possible planets orbiting a star 116 light years from Earth. Professional astronomers are now checking the bot’s findings.
Rabtsevich, who works in Spain as an artificial-intelligence specialist and has no formal astronomy education, is one of the most successful of a wave of amateur astronomers who are using AI models to search for worlds outside the Solar System. Citizen scientists have been using publicly available tools from NASA and other researchers to find planets for decades. But AI’s ability to scour the internet for such tools and use them to process massive amounts of data has lowered the barrier to entry for non-specialists who want to become planet hunters.
The newfound accessibility of data-analysis tools, however, has meant that not all amateurs are doing research responsibly, says Michelle Kunimoto, an exoplanet researcher at the University of British Columbia in Vancouver, Canada. Some planet hunters don’t fully understand the algorithms being used by AI tools and thus can’t validate the models’ findings, Kunimoto says. “There’s been a huge, overwhelming surge of AI-driven papers, which tend to have [planet] candidates that are not the best quality,” they say. And as a result, “the systems we use to disseminate science are being overwhelmed”.
An AI astronomer
For his search, Rabtsevich enlisted Claude Code, an AI coding tool made by Anthropic. He asked it to sift through data from more than 120,000 stars observed by NASA’s Transiting Exoplanet Survey Satellite (TESS) in late 2025 and early 2026. He guided Claude to use a popular planet-detection algorithm to search for dips in the stars’ brightness. A regular and repeated dimming of a star can be a sign that a planet is orbiting it and blocking a little bit of light every time it passes between the star and an observer.
Claude zeroed in on a star called TIC 4206066 that showed two separate cycles of dimming, one roughly every 3 days and another every 11 days. That suggests that TIC 4206066 is orbited by two planets, one of which loops around the star more tightly than the other and thus takes less time to complete a full rotation.
Rabtsevich instructed Claude to look for those same repetitive patterns in archival TESS data. Sure enough, data from 2018 and 2020 recorded the same pattern of dimming coming from TIC 4206066. At Rabtsevich's direction, Claude then found a planet-detection algorithm, one written and made publicly available by Kunimoto, and ran it to validate the signals.
Rabtsevich published his findings to the platform Zenodo as a preprint1 on 25 September. His work has not been peer reviewed, but TESS astronomers have completed an independent analysis of the signals and have added them to their official list of planet candidates.
