Pope Leo XIV has demanded justice for environmental defenders killed for protecting the Amazon and backed efforts by Indigenous peoples to stop their lands from being ravaged by logging and illegal mining.
On the penultimate day of his South American tour, Leo on Sunday travelled into the Peruvian Amazon and channelled one of Pope Francis’s core teachings that links care for the most vulnerable with care for God’s creation.
“The cry of the Earth and the cry of the poor are inseparable,” Leo told Indigenous representatives and missionaries gathered along the Ucayali River in the north-eastern city of Pucallpa.
He said he came to Pucallpa to support them in their search for justice and peace and referred to what environmental groups estimate have been 57 murders of environmental defenders between 2010 and 2024.
“The blood of these brothers cries out to God and demands justice,” Leo said to applause from the crowd, many of whom were wearing traditional feathered headwear and face markings of the region.
In addition to deforestation, illegal gold mining has become an increasingly lucrative business in the Amazon, devastating forests and polluting rivers. Illegal mining and coca production has also driven deforestation.
Leo addressed the combination of threats in one of his most forceful speeches to date, drawing on his years of experience as a Peruvian bishop and Francis’s constant preaching about the environment and the plight of Indigenous groups.
During the encounter and a subsequent mass in Pucallpa, he demanded a different model of economic development that respects the Earth and the Indigenous peoples who care for it, rather than the “irresponsible exploitation” by outside actors.
“Very little has been done – and very slowly at that – while the means to exploit and pollute creation have accelerated,” he said at mass. “In the face of these evils, let us not bury the Lord’s gifts, for he gives us the strength to repair and heal the suffering caused by an insatiable thirst for domination.”
In Peru, most murders of environmental defenders go unpunished; only a few have resulted in convictions.
The most recent conviction came in the case of Kichwa leader Quinto Inuma Alvarado, who was murdered in 2023 when he was ambushed by hitmen on a river in the San Martín region, the Amazonian territory he was defending.
Leo also referred to a notorious rise in sexual violence against Indigenous Awajún and Wampis schoolgirls in the northern Amazon, and blasted the “impunity” and “corruption” surrounding them.
The Awajún and Wampis Women’s Council has recorded 370 cases of sexual violence in the Condorcanqui province between January and July.
Leo’s last full day in Peru was also his most ambitious, crisscrossing the nation to minister to communities high in the Andes mountains. Peru’s air force released footage of the pope inside a helicopter and said it was “an unforgettable memory” to take Leo around the country.
His first stop was the mountain city of Cusco, where huge crowds greeted him.
“It is all worth it,” said Margarida Salomon, 63, who said she came from Sucre in Bolivia to see the pontiff, spending about $500 to take a bus and sleep in a tent. “I am still getting to know this pope, but the fact he is here in the Andes means a lot.”
The visit to Cusco, 3,400m (more than 11,000ft) above sea level, tested Leo’s stamina as he continues recovering from lung cancer surgery last month. Leo sat in the chair of his popemobile during part of the ride around Cusco, but it wasn’t clear if that was to preserve his energy or to steady himself on the uneven streets.
In general, the 71-year-old Leo has seemed unfazed by the toll of the three-nation South American trip, the longest of his pontificate.
