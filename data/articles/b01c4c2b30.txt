Highlights and challenges from covering an historic month in South America
NPR's South America Correspondent discusses her work covering an historic election upset and a papal visit.
ROB SCHMITZ, BYLINE: As an international correspondent, sometimes you're on hand to witness history in the making as NPR's Mike Shuster did in 1989 as East Germany was collapsing...
(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED NPR CONTENT)
MIKE SHUSTER: The East German authorities took a giant hammer to the wall last night and simply knocked a bit of it down. With a large digging machine, the workmen smashed two 40-feet-wide holes in the wall to the cheers of crowds on both sides.
SCHMITZ: ...Or NPR's former Johannesburg bureau chief Ann Cooper, heard here reporting from South Africa in 1994 as Nelson Mandela was inaugurated as the country's first Black president.
(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED NPR CONTENT)
ANN COOPER: There was a lot of symbolism today of the reconciliation that Mandela preaches, and I think this - you know, the great variety of South African guests, you know, was a very potent symbol of that.
SCHMITZ: It's rare, though, that two historic events occur on your watch just weeks apart. Well, that was exactly what happened for NPR South America correspondent Carrie Kahn. Last month, she covered an historic election in Brazil, where President Luiz Inacio Lula da Silva came from behind to win a runoff. And then Pope Leo XIV's first visit to Latin America as pope. So when we sat down for Reporter's Notebook, I asked her what covering these two big stories back to back was like.
CARRIE KAHN, BYLINE: It's been exhausting. But it's also incredibly amazing. You know, I just had this front seat to two very historic events on the continent. And to get to watch the mobilization just of millions in two very different public events, it was really - it was a career high point. Is that overstating it? But it really was. It was quite amazing. It was.
SCHMITZ: I mean, you've been covering Latin America for a long time.
KAHN: Yes. I don't want to date myself, but yes.
SCHMITZ: (Laughter).
KAHN: A couple decades.
SCHMITZ: That is a long time. I mean, let's talk about the papal visit. Here you are reporting on ALL THINGS CONSIDERED on the day that Leo arrived in Peru, a country that he called home for 20 years.
(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED NPR CONTENT)
KAHN: It's a huge party. This country is so happy the Pope is here.
UNIDENTIFIED GROUP: (Singing in non-English language).
SCHMITZ: That was obviously just a really celebratory mood there.
KAHN: It was. It was just amazing. There was a lot of bands and performers and local choral group all along Pope Leo's visit. There was a lot of waiting around, too, and so there were a lot of group celebrations breaking out. That was a group of young people that were singing in the streets. It was just wonderful. There was a lot of emotion in this visit.
And, you know, this trip came just weeks after he had a portion of his lung removed, you know, after being diagnosed with cancer. So the beginning was a little stiffer. You know, he was kind of standoffish, and you always wait for the pope to put out his hand before you touch the pope. But then they were hugging, and it was just - it was emotional all around. And, you know, it really - it was just surprising, all the emotions...
SCHMITZ: Oh.
KAHN: ...And the tears. This was this very plain, simple man.
SCHMITZ: Sure.
KAHN: And he just touched a lot of hearts with his simplicity and his very simple message. It was that everybody needs to work for the common good, the bien comun, the good of everyone. You know, as his mentor Francis preached, too, you know, individualism, vanity and hypertechnology is not what humanity needs now. It needs community and civility. And I think that really touched a lot of people in these troubling times.
SCHMITZ: You covered Pope Francis' trip to Mexico in 2016. How did this visit compare?
KAHN: It was just really different. It was a different time then.
SCHMITZ: Right.
KAHN: We hadn't seen so much polarization. And, you know, Francis was warmly welcomed to Mexico, but he's from a more liberal wing of the church, of Mexico's church. It was and still is very conservative. So it just felt different.
SCHMITZ: Got it.
KAHN: It was - you know, he wasn't as warmly embraced in Mexico as Leo was welcomed in Peru. And I think people, Catholic or not, during Leo's trip really just wanted to feel hopeful.
SCHMITZ: So I want to turn here to Brazil's election, and here you are again on October 4, when the first round had just finished.
(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED NPR CONTENT)
KAHN: Lula will have to find a way to close the gap in this runoff. He's not going to really be able to win over the 8% of voters who went for the other candidates. They were far-right and right, and their voters will fall in line with Bolsonaro, not with Lula.
SCHMITZ: So, Carrie , I mean, you can hear in your reporting here that Lula was facing long odds. And in fact, no one in modern Brazilian history had come back to win a second round after coming in second place in the first round. In the three weeks that followed, was it easy to see where this was going?
KAHN: OK, I would totally be lying if I told you that I perceived this changing landscape or a huge political shift. I did not.
(LAUGHTER)
KAHN: I wasn't making any predictions.
SCHMITZ: Thank you for being so...
KAHN: Well, actually...
SCHMITZ: ...Honest with me, Carrie.
KAHN: (Laughter) I just - I actually - you know, I thought Bolsonaro had it, you know, on election night. Where was I? I was in Brasilia at Bolsonaro's headquarters, waiting for him to come out...
SCHMITZ: Yeah.
KAHN: ...You know, with the hundreds of other reporters. It wasn't just me, but - that were waiting for him to give his victory speech.
SCHMITZ: I want to ask you a question just about covering South America as NPR's correspondent there. You know, when I was NPR's Shanghai correspondent - I was in charge of covering China - I would have this map of China, and I'd put pins in different parts of China that I went to or reported from just to make sure that I was covering the entire country of China because it's such a diverse country when you actually zoom in.
You are covering an entire continent. What is your approach to make sure that you're covering the stories that our audience needs to hear without trying to cover, you know, every story out there?
KAHN: I guess maybe I have to go get some pins.
(LAUGHTER)
KAHN: That's a good tip.
SCHMITZ: (Laughter).
KAHN: See if I'm really covering all over the place.
SCHMITZ: (Laughter).
KAHN: You know, it's tough. It's daunting. The continent is huge. I can travel a full day by plane and still be in Brazil. It's amazing.
SCHMITZ: That's incredible.
KAHN: It is. It's huge. I...
SCHMITZ: A full day...
KAHN: Yeah.
SCHMITZ: ...Just to stay in the same country.
KAHN: To get to the Amazon, you have to take...
SCHMITZ: Wow.
KAHN: You have to take, like, two or three planes, and they don't fly all the time, and it's costly, and it's time-consuming.
SCHMITZ: Right.
KAHN: I don't know. I always have to think about, will this story interest Americans? Under Trump - Trump 1 and Trump 2 - we do spend a lot of time, you know, covering what he is doing in Latin America. And, you know, in the second term, his administration has flexed so much U.S. power in the region that we're not - we haven't seen in decades.
SCHMITZ: Absolutely.
KAHN: So it's all new. And so that kind of pushes the agenda a lot, you know, from seizing Nicolás Maduro in Venezuela and the U.S. now pretty much running the country. And now I can get visas to go in there. You couldn't get visas before. It was very difficult.
SCHMITZ: Yeah, that - what a change.
KAHN: Yeah. There's rising gang violence and organized crime infiltrations all over the continent, so there's plenty to cover. And as you know, you know, you just work with a lot of amazing local journalists throughout the continent, in every country.
SCHMITZ: Right.
KAHN: I just had a great time with a journalist in Uruguay that I've never worked with before and my regular producer in Peru. And we spent a lot of time brainstorming some stories that we could work on in the future, so there's no shortage in the continent.
SCHMITZ: So, Carrie, you've been covering Latin America and beyond, and I'm just curious. Do you ever get homesick?
KAHN: Homesick - wait, where's home?
(LAUGHTER)
SCHMITZ: I knew you would say that.
KAHN: Of course. I don't know. I haven't lived in the U.S. for 20 years. I guess I do. For the U.S. - I'm from Northern California. Well, actually I grew up in Southern California, but I lived a lot of my life in Northern California. My kids are there, so yes, I would love to go back. My home is also in Mexico, where I have a lot of family. I don't know. I feel very comfortable in Latin America, and it feels like home.
SCHMITZ: That is NPR South America correspondent Carrie Kahn. Carrie, thank you so much.
KAHN: You're welcome.
