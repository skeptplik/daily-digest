Ten days after fleeing his home in Yemen's capital, Sanaa, Abu Khalil is selling socks on the streets of Riyadh.
"I walked out of Sanaa, I crossed the front line," he says, adding that he "hitched a ride into Saudi Arabia".
Families in Yemen are "struggling to provide for [their] children", he says, as he tries to earn enough to send some money home.
He asks us not to use his full name because his wife and four children are still in Sanaa, and he is frightened of what might happen to them if he is identified speaking out against the Houthis. "I ran away from the Houthis but I couldn't bring my family with me. May God protect them," he says.
Backed by Iran, the armed group has controlled much of northern Yemen since a civil war erupted more than a decade ago. It is fighting the internationally recognised government, which is supported by neighbouring Saudi Arabia.
And Saudi cities, such as Riyadh, are now coming under regular drone and missile attacks from the very group Abu Khalil fled.
An informal ceasefire in Yemen unravelled in July, and since the fighting escalated, the Houthis have stepped up attacks on Saudi Arabia. They have said they were retaliating against deadly Saudi air strikes in Yemen and what they say is a Saudi blockade of ports and airports in Houthi-controlled territory.
One Houthi strike on Riyadh's international airport on 10 October killed 13 people and injured more than 300 - the deadliest attack on Saudi soil in years.
Having been granted rare permission to report from Riyadh, we spoke to locals, including Majid, a 25-year-old engineer who works for the Saudi state oil company Aramco. He was passing through the airport that day, rushing for a flight to Jeddah for a religious pilgrimage.
He says he was watching a mother push her children in a pushchair when the terminal was hit.
He remembers seeing a man covered in blood. "I went to him in shock, asking him, 'Are you OK?'" He says he saw injured and unconscious passengers being taken to the airport's first aid clinic.
"Some of them, I still don't know whether they were alive or not."
The experience left him shaken. "The threat is real," he tells me. "But I will not let the Houthis make me feel unsafe in my own country."
The airport attack was far from an isolated incident. BBC analysis of data from the US-based Armed Conflict Location and Event Data Project (Acled), a non-profit organisation, which collects data on political violence and protests around the world, identified 274 attacks on Saudi Arabia in almost five months between 1 July and 25 November.
According to Acled, most of these were intercepted, but at least 41 were not and in some cases debris from the interception also caused damage.
Drones featured in almost two-thirds of the attacks, while ballistic missiles were used in more than half, according to the data.
In the previous five months, Acled recorded just one Houthi attack on the country.
Backed by Iran, the Houthis have developed an increasingly sophisticated arsenal, dramatically extending their ability to repeatedly strike deep in Saudi territory.
For years, Houthi attacks were largely concentrated near the Yemeni border, with Riyadh targeted far less frequently. But since the collapse of the unofficial ceasefire, the Saudi capital, more than 700km (430 miles) away from the Yemeni border, is now coming under regular attack.
As well as the airport, oil installations and prominent buildings in the capital have been in danger.
Saudi Arabia has spent billions transforming its cities, attracting international investment and presenting itself to the world as a safe place to live, work and visit - the attacks have put this promise to the test.
Two prominent skyscrapers have been hit in Riyadh's financial district this month.
Eleven people were injured in a strike on the Public Investment Fund (PIF) Tower, which is the tallest in Saudi Arabia and is the headquarters of the country's sovereign wealth fund. The other was the Aramco building, home to the state oil company.
As we drove past the Aramco tower, the damage was a clear reminder of the threat facing the kingdom.
But within days, the building's exterior had been completely repaired, any visible sign of the attack erased. It was perhaps a reflection of Saudi Arabia's determination to project an image of stability and business as usual, even as the attacks continue.
The government has condemned "terrorist attacks targeting civilian and economic facilities" and said it would "take all necessary measures" to defend itself.
Even so, flights to the capital have been severely disrupted by the strikes, which have also targeted other locations, such as Medina and the airport in the eastern city of Dammam.
Many passengers travelling to Riyadh are being routed through different airports and making the final leg of the journey on land.
The flight we took to Dammam was almost empty. Riyadh's streets tell a different story, though.
Restaurants are bustling, families browse at shopping centres and people go about their evenings seemingly undeterred by the threat overhead.
But getting people to speak openly about the conflict is difficult in a country where strict censorship laws and restrictions on freedom of expression make many wary of talking to the media.
Many of those who did speak to us expressed confidence in their country's ability to protect them. At a busy market, Badr says the airport attack was worrying, but life has quickly returned to normal. "I sleep comfortably," he says, praising the country's security forces.
Another man, Rashid, gestures towards the stalls around us: "The market is booming. People are buying and selling, going about their business. Everything is good."
But the normality he describes is periodically disturbed by a sudden buzz on the phone with an alert warning of an incoming attack. Then come the loud booms of air defences intercepting missiles and drones overhead.
We experienced one at 04:00 one morning, and the knowledge that not every attack is being intercepted can leave people afraid.
British expatriate Alex Malouf, who has lived in Saudi Arabia on and off since 2006, says he worries about the psychological impact on his 11-year-old daughter, who has experienced security alerts while at school.
"There have been times when my daughter has said, 'Daddy, I'm scared.' It has been something which I think has shaken our reality."
Despite the attacks, Abu Khalil says he feels much safer in Saudi Arabia than at home in Yemen where fighting has intensified.
The Houthis' renewed offensive in western Yemen has seen the group capture territory along the Red Sea coast and threaten strategic routes towards the southern city of Aden, the government's temporary base.
The fighting has also intensified around Taiz, a government-controlled city that has endured years of siege and conflict.
Road closures resulting from the latest Houthi siege there have severely restricted deliveries of essential supplies, leaving more than 800,000 people cut off from humanitarian assistance, according to the UN children's agency Unicef. Food and fuel that can be found have shot up in price.
More than 360,000 people have been forced from their homes in Yemen since August, according to the International Organization for Migration (IOM). And the UN Human Rights Office has verified 323 civilian casualties since then, including 76 killed, with children accounting for half of the dead.
A Saudi-led coalition of Arab states has responded to the Houthi advances and attacks on the kingdom with intensified air strikes, adding to the dangers faced by civilians. One strike in Sanaa earlier this month killed 44 people, according to Houthi authorities. Saudi Arabia says these operations were to protect the Yemeni population and global trade from the Houthis.
Saudi-led bombing campaigns during the earlier years of the war killed thousands of civilians and devastated homes, hospitals and schools.
The Houthis have also inflicted enormous suffering on civilians through shelling, missile and ground attacks on government-held territory, as well as repression in areas under their control.
Abu Khalil hopes that he will soon be able to bring his wife and children over to Saudi Arabia, away from the dangers in Sanaa, and says he "will not go back until the Houthis are gone".
