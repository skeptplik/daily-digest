The Supreme Court rules on Friday (November 27, 2026) on one of Canada's most closely watched cases -- a Quebec law barring some provincial employees from wearing religious symbols.
The law has been the subject of intense legal debate since it was first passed by Quebec's government in 2019.
That is partly due to the implications for Quebec, a majority French-speaking province where the decades-long battle to enforce secularism is a core issue and where the Catholic Church once wielded enormous influence over public life.
The case reflects a conflict playing out elsewhere in the world as strictly secular states such as that of France face pressure typical of the Anglo-Saxon world for people to be able to practice their religion freely, including wearing whatever symbols of it that they wish.
The Quebec case is also seen as a major test of how far provinces can go in defying provisions of the federal constitution by invoking the so-called "notwithstanding clause."
That clause, a quirk of Canada's legal system, effectively allows provincial governments to pass laws that may breach Canada's constitution, known as the Charter of Rights and Freedoms.
Quebec pre-emptively invoked the "notwithstanding clause" when passing its secularism bill, anticipating it would be challenged over constitutional protections guaranteeing freedom of religion and expression.
Conservative-led provinces have also invoked the clause to defend legislation relating to transgender youth.
In 2023, Saskatchewan used the clause to pass a law requiring schools to obtain parental consent before changing the pronouns used for children under 16.
Alberta has also invoked the clause in relation to laws banning transgender girls from participating in female sports and restricting hormone therapy for individuals under 16.
The Supreme Court's ruling in the Quebec case will be scrutinized for potential national impact.
'An absolute right'?
The Quebec law states that the province "is secular."
It says public employees in certain positions of authority, such as judges, police officers and teachers, cannot wear religious symbols.
It bars full-face coverings by public servants, people accessing public services in government buildings, and students from elementary school through university.
University of Montreal constitutional law professor Daniel Turp told AFP that, so far, Canada's charter has been interpreted as enshrining Canadian multiculturalism.
"The Supreme Court has somewhat elevated freedom of religion to the status of an absolute right to which no limit or restriction could be imposed," he said.
But Quebec is arguing for the primacy of other rights, including freedom of conscience, suggesting that it can at times be at odds with freedom of religion.
The law has been challenged by a wide range of groups, including the Canadian Civil Liberties Association and the National Council of Canadian Muslims.
A Quebec appeals court upheld the law in 2024, prompting opponents to take the case to the Supreme Court.
The pivotal ruling comes just a month after a separatist, staunchly secular provincial government was elected in Quebec, promising to broaden restrictions on religious displays in public life.
