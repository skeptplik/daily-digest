With temperatures in the Pacific Ocean at record-high levels and forecasts of an extreme El Niño climate event, intense marine heatwaves are expected around the world in the coming year1,2. Scientists must prepare to study these extreme conditions now, so that managers of marine resources and decision makers can develop and implement response strategies and communities can plan for a hotter future.
El Niño is part of a recurring natural cycle in Earth’s climate in which warmer waters in the central and eastern Pacific alter ocean and atmospheric circulation, affecting weather worldwide. Marine heatwaves associated with El Niño provide opportunities to study ecosystems under real-world stress conditions and on scales that cannot be replicated in a laboratory.
Marine heatwaves can be forecast weeks or months ahead, giving researchers time to design field studies, deploy instruments and collect baseline data. Investigations can be designed to test theories and improve our understanding of ecological processes, thresholds and recovery mechanisms in a rapidly warming ocean.
Such knowledge can inform decisions about how to manage marine resources, such as whether to close fisheries. Much has been learnt about marine heatwaves since the last El Niño, in 2023–24 (see ‘Hot water’). But these lessons need to be consolidated and scaled up into consistent, long-term preparedness plans.
Fortunately, effective approaches established in one place can be extended to others. Australia is a leader in developing early-warning systems and response plans for adaptation to a more extreme ocean climate. Other nations (including the United Kingdom, the United States, New Zealand and Peru) have improved their awareness of these events and put in place observation systems, forecasts and briefings, policy mechanisms and (in a few cases) adaptation measures.
But further action is needed, particularly in locations that have previously been unaffected by severe marine heatwaves. Here, we highlight seven priorities in increasing marine heatwave preparedness.
Forecast and early-warning systems
Seasonal forecasts with lead times of several months are now being delivered in various regions, including the United States3 and Australia4, and such systems are being developed in the United Kingdom and New Zealand5 and for the Mediterranean Sea6.
Forecasting relies on physical and statistical models to predict probable ocean temperatures7. Data from sustained surface and below-surface observations are essential for initializing models and verifying predictions, building users’ confidence in forecasts.
But managers want to know more precisely what the duration and intensity of a marine heatwave is expected to be. Forecasts become less accurate the further ahead they look and at fine spatial scales, such as along coasts. Improved ocean monitoring, a better understanding of the drivers of marine heatwaves and continued refinements of model physics are needed to improve forecast accuracy7.
Advances in machine learning are expected to be transformative. Next-generation models that incorporate ocean physics might be able to predict unprecedented marine heatwaves more accurately than current technologies can. Downscaling global climate models to inshore regions using machine-learning techniques could also increase the usefulness of forecasts in places where most marine-resource users operate.
Coordinated briefings
Planning for and communication about impending marine heatwaves are also improving1. In 2023, engagement was piecemeal and often only in response to events that were already in progress. Activities are now more proactive and structured.
Coordinated briefings that translate forecasts into action provide ways of engaging with affected groups and planning ahead. Such communication strategies should be implemented around the world4,8. National briefings provide a broad overview of marine heatwave risks and impacts. Regional and industry specialists can provide further local advice.
For example, Australia’s national marine climate briefing series4 has been running since 2023. Similar meetings (led or contributed to by some of us) have taken place for the western Pacific Ocean (G.S.) and in the United Kingdom (C.R.), Brazil (K.E.S.), Singapore (C.M.S.) and New Zealand (C.M.S.).
Response plans
Seafood-producing firms and conservation managers need a menu of actions to choose from when a marine heatwave looms. Options include adjusting collection strategies, temporarily closing fisheries and modifying aquaculture operations1.
Australia has developed regional and industry-specific response plans for marine heatwaves8. Response teams for fisheries are active during warming events and provide coordinated mechanisms for assessment, communication and management actions4,9.
Early-warning systems and interagency coordination are key components of timely responses. Industry has also adopted operating protocols to reduce the impacts of marine heatwaves on animal health and food-product quality. For example, abalone fisheries in New South Wales have changed how sea snails (Haliotis spp.) are fished, handled and prepared for live export10. Other regions could benefit from similar plans4.
For example, fishing of Peruvian anchoveta (Engraulis ringens), the source of around 20% of fishmeal and fish oil globally, has already been curbed by the current El Niño. In 2023, the fishery had to be closed, resulting in an estimated loss11 of US$1.4 billion. This year, an alert in February prompted Peruvian authorities to bring the fishing season forwards by two weeks and reduce the total allowable catch of anchoveta and white anchovy (Anchoa nasus) by more than 30% to 1.9 million tonnes. This meant that fisheries could catch about 450,000 tonnes before the heatwave intensified and the season was ended in May.
Adaptation actions
In 2023, marine heatwave preparedness was recognized as inadequate in most regions. The emphasis then was mainly on raising risk awareness in sectors such as fisheries and aquaculture1. However, these and other industries need to adapt to a changing ocean, too.
Awareness has now grown among sectors and institutions, and behaviours have begun to change. For example, Australian marine climate briefings draw engagement from aquaculture and fishery managers, marine tourism operators, policymakers, researchers and individual fishers4.
In the United States, forecasts of marine heatwaves trigger advice to fishers regarding increases in interactions with marine mammals as the overlap between animals’ hunting grounds and deployed fishing gear expands, which can result in fishery closures. Coral-reef managers involved in restoration efforts can scale back replanting efforts and start collecting genetic material.
In the Mediterranean Sea, limiting diving tourism at coral reefs to minimize damage during marine heatwaves has been proposed, mirroring practices that were adopted in Asia during the 2023–24 marine heatwaves11.
In the Cook Islands’ black-pearl industry, seasonal forecasts have been proposed for supporting decisions around adaptive management aimed at reducing the thermal stress of oysters. These include increased reliance on hatchery production under controlled temperature conditions and preferential harvesting of pearls during cooler periods of the year.
In the United Kingdom, a national marine heatwave network and code of best practice have been established, enabling the exchange of transdisciplinary knowledge. Similar transfers of experience, capacity and lessons learnt between jurisdictions can accelerate preparedness globally and help to embed risk management into routine decision-making processes4.
Physical and ecological monitoring
The 2023 El Niño led to calls to strengthen monitoring and research capacity to improve the detection of, understanding of and response to marine heatwaves. Observation networks are now becoming a core component of preparedness and response plans, providing the information needed to support forecasting, impact assessment, management interventions and long-term adaptation strategies12.
Targeted uses of gliders and intensified monitoring strategies can bolster research, situational awareness and management decisions12. Research has expanded into understanding ecological resilience and adaptation. For example, genetic studies are identifying traits that might enhance the capacity of key fishery or habitat-forming species13 to persist under increasing thermal stress.
