A recurring theme in the debate over the problems at NHS Grampian has been concern at a revolving door of senior managers in Scotland's NHS.
A damning report published earlier this month found that failures to learn from mistakes in Grampian had caused "repeated and avoidable harm" to patients.
Health Secretary Angela Constance has now sent in a team of four expert leaders to help drive urgent change by providing "external challenge, scrutiny and practical support".
But three of them previously held senior positions at other boards that also faced serious criticism.
Patient safety commissioner Karen Titchener has voiced "reservations" about those being asked to improve safety in one board when so many other boards had their own problems to solve.
And the lawyer representing a whistleblower who highlighted serious concerns at NHS Grampian has compared the situation in Scotland to "shuffling of deckchairs on the sinking Titanic".
Eyebrows have been raised about names on the health secretary's team of hand-picked experts who are being sent in to help with NHS Grampian's turnaround.
It is being led by Jim Crombie, deputy chief executive of NHS Lothian.
In 2018, as interim chief executive, he was forced to apologise after an independent investigation found a culture of "bullying and harassment" at the board.
Crombie also apologised for failings in 2024 after a whistleblowing report found mothers and babies had come to harm in the maternity unit at Edinburgh Royal Infirmary because of staff shortages and a toxic workplace culture.
Dr Chris Deighan, a former medical director at NHS Lanarkshire, is also on the team.
He was criticised recently by lawyers for the Scottish Hospitals Inquiry for being an unhelpful witness after he gave evidence on his role in the infection scandal at the Queen Elizabeth Hospital in Glasgow while he was at NHS Greater Glasgow and Clyde.
A third rescue team member, Dr Pamela Johnston, was previously executive medical director at NHS Tayside.
In 2023, she apologised to patients after a review found senior managers at Ninewells Hospital had failed to adequately supervise disgraced neurosurgeon Sam Eljamel, despite a rising number of complaints.
The health secretary has said these external troubleshooters will work with NHS Grampian to turn things around.
She has given them six weeks, and has also made two appointments to the board.
Meanwhile, Constance is sticking by Grampian chief executive Laura Skaife-Knight to get the job done - even though Skaife-Knight herself was found to have misled HIS over the way the death of a patient was investigated.
There are also questions about the performance of former bosses at NHS Grampian, with HIS referring to "long-standing" issues with culture and leadership in that executive team.
At the helm from 2020 to 2023 was Caroline Hiscox, who is now NHS Lothian chief executive - and being tipped to head one of two new super health authorities that would be formed if the government pushes forward with reforms to merge the mainland boards.
She was running NHS Grampian in 2022, at the time of a review into 19 avoidable patient deaths in surgery at Aberdeen Royal Infirmary, which was not made public.
Surgeon Dr Mohammed Habib, who leaked the report last week, said he was risking his job to speak out because he believed senior managers had tried to cover up what was going on.
His lawyer Aamer Anwar, a long-time ally of patient safety campaigners, says the current board and chief executive of NHS Grampian should go.
Anwar says there seems to be a permanent crisis in the NHS in Scotland, and people seem to have to accepted a "shuffling of deckchairs on the sinking Titanic, where you have one individual, who hasn't done a good job in one place, is then shuffled off to another place.
"They're all sitting on six-figure salaries so I don't expect the public to have any sympathy for their situation.
"They are just moved around and around in a very small circle, and everybody knows each other, so that causes concern."
'We need to bring in fresh blood'
On Tuesday, the health secretary is set to announce a review into how concerns over the surgical mortality rate at NHS Grampian were handled by the NHS and by the Scottish government.
Labour MP Dr Zubir Ahmed, who worked in Scotland for the majority of his career as a surgeon, said this must look at the relationship between politicians and senior NHS management.
In an interview with Radio Scotland Breakfast, he said: "It doesn't sit right with me that in NHS Scotland we don't get much of a turnover of senior managers, they move from one place to the next."
Dr Ahmed said getting "the right people in the right jobs" was very important.
Former SNP MSP Alex Neil, who served as health secretary between 2012 and 2014, said there was "too much of the same people doing a job swap almost on a rotational basis" within the NHS.
"I think we need to bring in fresh blood from outside - either outside the health service in terms of management, or outside the country."
We asked the Scottish government about these concerns and about the choice of experts sent in to help with improvements at NHS Grampian.
In response, a spokesperson stressed that leadership and accountability for NHS Grampian rested with the chief executive and the health board.
"This is not a leadership team," they said.
"This is an intervention support team which is providing broad external support in organisational and operational recovery, clinical governance, patient safety and performance to support NHS Grampian's improvement. It will not be making decisions.
"The health secretary has been clear she welcomes the involvement of the patient safety commissioner in scrutinising the progress NHS Grampian must make, and we understand she is due to visit Grampian this week."
Constance has decided that the situation at NHS Grampian does not meet the "exceptional circumstances" needed for direct ministerial intervention in the running of the board.
Such a step - known as stage five in the government's escalation framework - has only ever been used once, in Tayside in 2018.
Instead, Grampian will remain at stage four, where it has been since May 2025 due to issues around finances, leadership and governance.
NHS Ayrshire and Arran is also at stage four for financial management, governance and leadership.
That means external support is being provided by a government-appointed "assurance board" at senior levels because the health board leadership has been unable to deliver improvements.
The NHS boards in Borders, Dumfries and Galloway, Highland, Lothian, Orkney and Tayside are all at stage three for different reasons.
This means tailored support and scrutiny is provided by the government because of "risks materialising".
The rest are at level two, with increased monitoring by central government - but none are at stage one, where they would be delivering in line with expectations.
In total, that means eight of the 14 regional health boards in Scotland have some level of government direct intervention at level three or above.
