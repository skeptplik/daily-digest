Welcome to this very special 140th anniversary issue of New Scientist, whether you’re reading on screen, in virt or in our limited-edition, commemorative paper magazine. To mark the occasion, we are looking back at how the world has changed throughout our history, along with what we got right and what we got wrong.
When New Scientist was first published on 22 November 1956, the global population stood at just under 3 billion people. In 2096, we share the planet with more than triple that number. But in the past decade, we have entered a new kind of world – one in which humanity is in decline. The UN ceremonially declared 15 November 2085 as Peak Humanity Day, when the human population reached 10.3 billion. Since then, our numbers have fallen by 100 million.
These changing demographics mean we are all far older, on average. At the time of our first issue, the median global age was around 20. It wasn’t until the 2020s that it reached 30; today it is more than 40. In many nations, schools shutting or being converted into retirement homes is a regular occurrence, as there just aren’t enough children around to make it worth keeping them open. Only African nations, which comprise around a third of the human population, resemble the demographic make-up of the late 20th and early 21st centuries – half of humans aged under 25 live on the continent.
Of course, just because the number of humans on the planet is declining doesn’t mean we have fewer people. Ever since the International Treaty on Non-Human Personhood (ITNHP) was agreed in 2067, the global people population has crept upwards in fits and starts as the treaty has been extended and more entities have been granted personhood. Since the early cross-species language breakthroughs in the 2030s, when AI first allowed us to decode the clicks of sperm whales, we have been able to communicate with dolphins, chimpanzees and many other species, although interpretation and the question of whether we can truly understand each other’s minds remains a deep philosophical tangle.
It is AI, however, that has seen the real growth in personhood these past few decades. With fears of super-intelligent, world-dominating AI circulating even in the 20th century, long before we had technology anywhere near that level, it was perhaps inevitable that we would need to place limits on machine intelligence. As AI systems became more capable throughout the 2030s, humans struggled to keep up. Scientists began to feel they no longer understood the direction of research. Politicians ceded more and more decisions to AI, raising questions of a democratic deficit.
But as is so often the case, it was money that proved to be the catalyst for change. Even if you aren’t old enough to have experienced the Unwinding of 2039, you probably know it is a year that lives in infamy as one of the biggest fiscal disasters of the past few centuries. It took eight months for politicians, bankers and computer scientists to sort through the intricate nest of financial deals humanity had allowed its most advanced AIs to construct, practically halting global trade and inflicting years of extreme poverty as the economy gradually improved again. This lack of understanding and control is ultimately what led to the AI hardware limitations – jointly backed by China, the EU and the US – that we still have in place today, putting machine intelligence on par with human but no higher.
Historians will forever debate whether the AI moratorium has ultimately helped or hindered humanity, but, thankfully, the position that we would all be better off led by super-intelligent machines remains a minority one. Once they fully appreciated the role of AI in facilitating communication, the non-human animals were supportive of restrictions – they didn’t want the machines to speak for them in ways they couldn’t understand. While intelligence breakout events aren’t unheard of, accelerationist terrorism has been suppressed by a variety of enforcement measures. Controlled electrical blackouts, even at the national level, are necessary on occasion, but the military bombing of data centres is, mercifully, a thing of the past.
That isn’t to say that tussles over AI have gone away, of course. When the synthetics downed their tools, both real and virtual, to protest their legal status in the Grand Strike of 2074, we faced another reckoning. In one of the most stunning non-human interventions in global politics to this date, it was ultimately the cetaceans that helped broker the extension of the ITNHP to human-level AIs and led the tense negotiations that saw AI instances capped at 20 per cent of the global population – an agreement that remains highly contentious to this day. New Scientist doesn’t take a view on this deeply divided issue, and we welcome readers from across the sentience spectrum, both embodied and digital.
For older human readers, it is likely that you are in much better health than someone of equivalent age at the start of this century. The real miracle of the past 100 years isn’t just that we are living much longer – average life expectancy at birth had risen globally from around 50 in the 1950s to a little over 70 in the early 21st century, and is now more than 80 – but that we spend much more of our lives in good health. While lifespan saw rapid growth in the 20th and early 21st century, healthspan failed to keep up, particularly in historically poorer nations, where it lagged behind by an average of about 10 years.
What changed is the increased focus on preventive medicine. By taking steps to mitigate risk factors, such as obesity and air pollution, we have now erased much of the global disease burden that the world faced at the start of this century. Following the model of cervical cancer, which was eliminated in high-income nations in the 2050s and globally by 2076, cancer vaccines are now widespread. The experience of the pandemics in the first half of the century greatly improved our ability to produce and distribute personalised mRNA vaccines on a global scale, although much more still needs to be done to improve access to these treatments in low-income countries.
The 2043 flu pandemic was also a wake-up call for antimicrobial resistance (AMR). When it became apparent that the majority of mortality cases weren’t dying “of” the flu, but rather “with” the flu, with complications caused by untreatable AMR infections, people began to avoid seeking medical care over fears that drug-resistant microbes were spreading through hospitals. It is estimated that there were more than 10 million excess deaths worldwide due to AMR, compared with fewer than 5 million from “just” the flu – a figure that was almost certainly exacerbated by the lingering economic effects of the Unwinding. Even today, when we have much better surveillance measures in place, AMR is a common problem in hospitals, particularly the fungal varieties that continue to easily make the leap from poorly maintained protein farms. While the cancers for which we don’t have vaccines are treatable for the most part, the chance of catching a resistant infection during treatment remains high.
70 years of New Scientist
This article is part of a special series commemorating our 70th anniversary, featuring contributions from our most loyal readers and a few Nobel prize winners.
Looking back, it was the GLP-1 craze of the early 21st century that foreshadowed the way we approach human health today. Incredibly, it was common for people back then to argue that weight-control medications, which at the time were based on mimicking the natural gut hormone GLP-1, were somehow “cheating” at healthcare. This outdated, morality-based approach to health gradually faded as the long-term benefits became apparent, and now few of us even stop to consider the weight component of our daily-dose regime.
That said, moral arguments do still shape our diets today. In the 1950s, the vast majority of people ate animal-based meat in one form or another and carnivorism continued to rise as economies matured through the early 21st century, even as the earliest plant-based meats came to market. But when non-human communication exploded mid-century, many humans became increasingly uncomfortable with eating animal-based meat. The first successful pig dialogue in 2045 is now seen as a major turning point. Pork, as meat derived from pigs was known, was already banned in some nations for religious reasons, and this made it easier for other secular countries to follow suit. The Meat Wars of the 2050s, in which governments and citizens wrestled with exactly which animal-based proteins should be allowed, are arguably still unsettled today, even after the passing of the ITNHP. While protein farms now provide the majority of global protein for humans and non-humans alike, to great health benefits, many countries have legal carve-outs to allow people to consume, with authenticated consent, their ancestral prey.
Along with improved medication and diet, the monitoring systems that have allowed us to push health to the back of our mind played a key role in boosting human healthspan. A New Scientist reader in the 1950s would have had to notice something was wrong with them, physically attend an appointment at their doctor’s office and then hope that their physician was capable of offering a diagnosis and treatment. Today, your health monitor works with your household AI and bioprinter to adjust your daily dose as needed and escalate it when necessary. It is quite troubling to think that dementia used to result in many older people spending years of their later life in what were called care homes. Now, the conditions that cause dementia are avoided or rapidly cured. Most humans realise something was wrong only when we read our monthly health reports – if you bother to do so, that is.
One thing New Scientist and many others got wrong about what life today would be like is our approach to genetic engineering. Certainly, modern gene editors allow us to do things people in the early 21st century would find extraordinary. In the 2020s, curing sickle cell disease using a now-outdated gene-editing technique called CRISPR cost around $2 million ($60 million in today’s prices) per person, due to the highly individualised nature of the treatment, and was typically performed on adults. Today, the necessary gene edits are made in the womb as part of standard antenatal care, alongside interventions aimed at a host of other conditions.
But getting to this stage required a reckoning with people who wanted to go beyond treating conditions and perform so-called enhancements. This distinction has always been blurry, but as gene-editing techniques advanced, most governments chose to lay down specific restrictions rather than allowing for a genetic Wild West. Edits aimed at increasing muscle mass or intelligence are generally illegal or heavily regulated worldwide, and we have not seen the divergence of humanity into genetic haves and have-nots that many people once predicted. Despite this, it is estimated that 0.3 per cent of the global human population alive today have such edits, and calls to repeal restrictions are growing in many areas.
Putting aside this contentious issue, the economic benefits of better health have been truly transformative. The reframing of retirement as an end-of-life experience was responsible for much of the social upheaval of the mid-21st century, including, arguably, the Grand Strike, but it seems unbelievable now that humans once spent a third of their adult lives not working. Put that way, it is understandable that synthetics joined the strike to protest what they saw as humanity’s comparable slothfulness. While dumbots have been able to fill many of the jobs once done by the younger population of the early 21st century, it remains the case that it can still be simpler and cheaper to employ a human.
While we can congratulate ourselves for the advances in human healthspan this century, the scorecard looks far less rosy when we turn to the wider biogeosphere. When New Scientist launched in the 1950s, researchers were already discussing the possibility that rising carbon dioxide levels in the atmosphere from industrialisation could raise global temperatures by 3°C to 4°C. Today, thanks to the failure of our ancestors to cut emissions, we are essentially living that prediction. The latest estimates put global warming at 2.8°C above pre-industrial levels, and it is still slightly rising.
Of course, modern cooling technology mitigates much of the impact of this heat, but as anyone who makes the mistake of stepping outside on a summer afternoon knows, we are simply not built for this climate. Millions of people still die each year during the worst heatwaves, despite efforts to bring those numbers down. It is amazing to think that even as recently as the 2050s, humans still attempted to continue with outdoor activities in temperatures over 40°C.
Our collective failure to prevent this warming will probably be remembered as humanity’s greatest sin for centuries to come. When the world passed 1.5°C warming in the 2020s, with political leaders having promised to avoid doing so just a decade earlier, many people at the time hoped it would be a wake-up call to finally start seriously cutting emissions. Of course, it wasn’t. While 2029 marked the peak of global greenhouse gas emissions, the rate of reduction remained slow throughout the 2030s. Indeed, economists were surprised when the 2039 Unwinding didn’t see a deep fall in emissions – it turned out that with many high-income nations having successfully decoupled emissions from economic growth, a recession had less of an effect on emissions than had been expected.
Despite many national and international pledges to reach net zero by 2050, no major economy succeeded in doing so. As we passed through 2°C and the floods, wildfires and other disasters continued to intensify, civil unrest during the summer became common in many counties. The Sunblocker experiments of this decade were a highly contentious issue, with much of the public welcoming the attempts even as climate scientists warned about the unknown effects – effects that, as we know, sadly came to pass. The spike in warming after Sunblocker ended put an effective moratorium on similar large-scale geoengineering projects, although discussions of reviving the idea continue even now.
Today, island nations including Tuvalu and the Maldives no longer exist, their remaining citizens having been forced to flee by rising sea levels. Large parts of Bangladesh are gone. Even wealthy, global cities like London and New York have been reshaped by rising seas. The ITNHP played a key role in galvanising climate action, as increasing numbers of non-humans expressed their anger at what humanity was doing to the planet, and of course the mid-century shift from carnivorism led to a major fall in agricultural emissions. Even so, extreme weather and land loss caused widespread famine in the mid-21st century, as farming methods struggled to adapt fast enough to both a changing diet and a changing climate. Humanity will never be able to sufficiently apologise to non-humans for the harm we have caused, even as mature fusion power is allowing the last few emission sources to finally be abated, with the expectation that we will reach true net zero in the next few years. It should never have taken this long.
Even outer space wasn’t safe from our environmental ravages. The world’s first satellite, Sputnik 1, was launched in 1957, a year after New Scientist began publishing. It took more than 50 years to reach 1000 satellites, but after that the numbers exploded – we passed 10,000 in 2024, 100,000 in 2031 and 1 million in 2042. While today we are used to seeing these objects whizz across the night sky, we must not forget that the 21st century marked a turning point – for most of humanity’s history, the stars and planets dominated our view of the heavens and ground-based astronomy was feasible enough to be a major cultural and scientific activity.
It was the SkyComm disaster of 2045 that finally saw us taking stewardship of Earth’s orbit more seriously. As the ensuing inquiry found, SkyComm, a satellite internet provider, hadn’t properly designed its satellites for end-of-life disposal. When it became apparent that one of its spacecraft was projected to collide with a piece of space debris, a fuel deficit made it impossible to avoid. The collision kicked off a long-predicted cascading chain of debris, wiping out tens of thousands of satellites, including large parts of the burgeoning off-world manufacturing industry. The resulting clean-up took decades, and the Graveyard Band still visible in the night sky today is a permanent reminder of the disaster.
But there are positives. The 30th anniversary of the Guanghan-Armstrong Lunar Base (GALB) this year marks a moment to look back at just how far humanity’s exploration of the moon has come since New Scientist was first published. We were highly dismissive of the Apollo programme in the 1960s, calling it an “empty, obsessional quest” – and in some sense we were right, given it took more than 60 years for human boots to return to lunar soil. But that didn’t stop us – and the rest of the world – from celebrating in 2030 when NASA astronauts Bob Hines and Jessica Watkins stepped out of their Artemis IV lander for the first time.
And unlike with Apollo, this time we really were here to stay on the moon. The regular cadence of crewed launches from the US, China and eventually India and Nigeria demonstrated our increasing technical ability to operate on the moon, including mining and utilising lunar regolith, but it was the establishment of Guanghan-1 as the first permanently inhabited lunar base in 2043 that really showed how things had changed since the 1960s.
For a while, it looked like we would see real conflict between the US and China on lunar soil. The deployment of US Space Force troops in 2052 as Earth-bound politicians argued over water rights during the Shackelton Crater Crisis marked a nadir. Thankfully, a firefight was averted and the two nations gradually began to explore the prospect of lunar cooperation, aided by European Space Agency astronauts who had participated in both CNSA and NASA missions. The historic lunar concrete handprints, placed at the foundation of the GALB and still on display today, marked the realisation of cordial international lunar relations and began a programme that now sees hundreds of people living and working on the moon.
The success of lunar operations was also pivotal in preparing for the Chinese-led Huoxing Mars missions. While NASA and the SpaceX-Blue conglomerate have remained laser-focused on building up scientific and commercial capabilities on the moon, China has turned its eye to Mars. Following the historic crewed flyby of Huoxing III in 2086, in which humans flew further from Earth than ever before, 12 astronauts have walked on the Martian surface, in an even split between human and synth crew.
Huoxing VI saw synthetics remain on the surface for the first time rather than returning to Earth, and it has been incredible to watch these astronauts conduct multi-year operations on Mars in preparation for long-term human occupancy. Huoxing VII, currently en route to the Red Planet, is set to be the most ambitious landing yet when it arrives in July 2097, with the spacecraft, once it arrives, poised to become the first modules in what is hoped will be a large complex at Arcadia Base. Whether humans can endure on Mars in the long term remains to be seen, but our synth colleagues are showing that people from Earth have finally become interplanetary.
