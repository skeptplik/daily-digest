At the DevGAMM Lisbon conference this week, veteran game designer Howard Scott Warshaw gave a talk called "From Atari to AI". Warshaw was behind a number of famous titles for the Atari 2600, including the classic Yars' Revenge, but infamously he also designed the game E.T. the Extra-Terrestial, the poor quality of which has been cited as a factor in the US video game crash of 1983, when US games industry revenue plummeted from over $3 billion in 1983 to around $100 million in 1985.
Unsold copies of the 1982 game were later buried in the New Mexico desert, and Warshaw would eventually leave the games industry to become a psychotherapist. He wrote an autobiography in 2020 called Once Upon Atari: How I Made History by Killing an Industry, in which he detailed the pressure he faced to produce E.T. in a matter of weeks so it could be released in time for Christmas.
At DevGAMM Lisbon, Warshaw made a connection between the "glut of crap on the market" ahead of the 1983 crash and the huge number of poor games being made using AI now, saying it's "going to have the same impact on a larger scale."
Warshaw acknowledged that AI is a "super controversial topic", but said that one thing it does "incredibly" is leverage programming capacity. "People who used to have to spend weeks, months, or years programming something can now do it in days with AI support," he said.
"So what does that mean in game development? What it means is we're going to see a lot more games produced. You ever hear of the crash of '83 or '84, the big video game crash? They said the problem that happened in the crash, and I think that was true, was there was a glut of crap on the market, because the machine opened up and anybody could just pop a game out, and they did.
"AI is going to have the same impact on a larger scale"
"So way more games came out, and good games were still being made, but they were way overshadowed by the crap: and that created a real problem in the game industry. AI is going to have the same impact on a larger scale, because on the one level, so many more people can now realise and create a video game than ever could before just using AI. And that's okay, but there's going to be a lot of crap, because most games suck. That's just the way it is.
"Game design is just like movie ideas and novel ideas, and everybody wants to write a book about their life and it's going to make a great story. There's some things in life that everybody can see themselves doing, and the truth is most people who do it can't do it very well. And what AI is going to do is make that more possible for people who can't do it to do it.
"What I mean by that is a lot of people are going to be able to realise their ideas, and most of them aren't going to be very interesting. But it is also true that there are going to be a lot more really good games and some really interesting ideas, because at the same time that it enables a lot of people to put out garbage, it's also going to enable the people who really do have some innovative and interesting, and solid ideas to realise those, too, so it will move it forward."
"The new challenge we're going to have is to have a way to winnow out the crap"
Warshaw said that in the past, technology advances swallowed up a large portion of time and attention when developing games, but with the advent of AI, he thinks the focus will shift from technology development to game design. "And I think that's actually going to be a super positive thing for the video game industry," he said. "The new challenge we're going to have is to have a way to winnow out the crap and get to the good games."
At the end of the talk, GamesIndustry.biz asked Warshaw to clarify his comments comparing the crash of 1983 with the glut of poor-quality AI-made games now, and whether he envisages a similar crash occurring.
"I don't really see the prospect of a crash in the video game industry," he said, "because video games back then, people were speculating. In the early 80s, people were saying games were just a fad. A lot of people were saying they were a fad, and the crash just meant that investment left the field and nobody wanted to pay for doing games, because they weren't sure if games were here to stay.
"By now, decades later, games are such a pervasive, omnipresent part of our lives, the idea that games are going to go away, I don't think ever happens. The idea that games become something different entirely, that is still available."
"I really don't see a crash per se. What I do see is a reorganisation"
He said that because games are now such a fundamental part of society, "I really don't see a crash per se. What I do see is a reorganisation, where we need better filters to figure out a way to get rid of the garbage games and not waste time with that, and to highlight what are really innovative and exciting things."
Beyond the deluge of AI-made games on platforms like Steam, the games industry has been facing a number of interlinked crises over the past four to five years, as outlined in a talk by Amir Satvat at Gamescom Dev in August. Various senior industry figures have previously made a connection between the current issues and the US video game crash of 1983, including Epic Games head Tim Sweeney and Romero Games co-founder Brenda Romero – although Romero later clarified her comments on Bluesky, saying the comparison was more about "how it feels to live and work in this industry as a developer, not in terms of industry revenue."
