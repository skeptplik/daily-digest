Jessica Chastain’s career has taken many turns. For a time, she was one of the Academy’s most beloved actresses, appearing in films that made their way deep into the awards conversation. She was nominated for her acting work (Zero Dark Thirty, The Eyes of Tammy Faye) or as part of a larger ensemble (The Martian, Interstellar). More recently, she was nominated for a Tony and helmed the buzzy horror movie Other Mommy, which made an impact at the box office but got trashed by critics. Her Apple TV thriller The Savant was postponed because of its subject matter, inspiring a wider conversation on politics. But for all those twists, turns, and bumps in the road, Heartland might be her most delirious project yet.
Written and directed by Shana Feste, the Netflix-original film feels as if the director of Country Strong watched a couple of episodes of Yellowstone and decided to take a stab at a western-noir, centering her story on a jaded country star. The result is a missing-person mystery with three musical numbers woven into it. Heartland might be the first western-noir-musical ever made, and I wish Feste took advantage of how weird that is. Instead, it’s pure straight-to-Netflix fodder.
Nashville with a dark twist
Chastain stars as Misty Jones, a country singer living in seclusion. The first time we see her, she's crying over her dog, who’s been shot and killed by the neighbor. Misty threatens the neighbor and shoots out her windows. Then she gets drunk and has a one-night stand. Her successful past is alluded to when she opens her fridge to reveal moldy food sitting right next to a Grammy Award.
The plot kicks in after Misty gets a call from her brother Donny (Paul Sparks), forcing her to reckon with her Nashville past. His daughter, rising country star Charlie Hart (Carter Faith), has gone missing. He wants Misty to find her. I guess a musician is better equipped to find missing people than the Nashville police? I don’t know, but let’s just roll with it.
Misty embarks on a road trip back home to look into the case, getting in touch with the most important people in Charlie’s life, including her manager (Garrett Hedlund), her rude boyfriend Jace Jacobs (singer Ross Lynch), and her publicity team. Misty finds that no one cares for Charlie; they were all just using her for her to sell albums and tickets. She also uncovers a conspiracy that grows more and more tangled with every passing scene.
In Heartland, Nashville is depicted as a kind of rural Gotham, hiding corruption and schemes on every corner. The film makes it clear that Misty is not welcome and the more she probes, the bigger the target on her back becomes. “Nashville’s changed,” warns one of Jones’ old friends. “And you and me? We ain’t famous anymore.” Okay?
A wild premise played straight
There aren’t many surprises in Heartland, with Charlie’s disappearance resolved in a twist you can see coming the minute Misty has her first drink in Nashville. Still, the film boasts a surprisingly talented cast, ranging from Sparks, who’s reduced to phone call scenes where he’s nagging at Misty, to Chastain herself.
The Oscar-winning actress is fully committed to the part, looking more and more haggard as the film progresses. She gets dragged onstage several times, resulting in some fun numbers that mix original music and covers. Chastain does her own singing, holding her own alongside real-life performers. Charlie’s boyfriend Jace Jacobs invites Misty onstage to intimidate her, only for the crowd to be swept off their feet by the unexpected comeback of a retired country superstar. Winnie Jo Lean (Jennifer Nettles, from the musical duo Sugarland) joins Misty onstage to perform the film’s official song, called, you guessed it, “Heartland.”
Other noteworthy performers include Hedlund, who’d previously worked with Feste in Country Strong, and an inexplicable cameo from John Hawkes as Misty’s former husband Clark. As always, Hawkes is electric onscreen, but he’s featured in one scene.
I can imagine a better version of this movie, one that goes all in on its strange fusion of genres. Instead, Heartland is formulaic, with nothing new to say about the music industry or the perils of being a young woman in it. It’s for die-hard Chastain fans, or something to play in the background if you love a mild-mannered train wreck.
Heartland is now streaming on Netflix.
