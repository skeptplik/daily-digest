Where big U.S. unions stand on Trump's Canada tariffs
U.S. labour movement has long supported targeted tariffs, but unions criticize Trump's approach
While U.S. President Donald Trump insists his tariffs will bring a boom in factory jobs, the unions who represent hundreds of thousands of manufacturing workers are largely skeptical of his approach.
Nowhere is that skepticism more apparent than organized labour's view of Trump's tariffs on imports from Canada.
Despite their long-standing support for targeted tariffs as a tool for protecting U.S. jobs, the leaders of some of the biggest unions in the U.S. are calling on the Trump administration to take a different approach to Canadian products.
Brian Bryant, international president of the IAM Union, formerly known as the International Association of Machinists and Aerospace Workers, says he'll take that message directly to Trump's point man on tariffs, U.S. Trade Representative Jamieson Greer, at a meeting scheduled for early December.
"We're in favour of strategic tariffs where they're used appropriately to correct bad behaviour," Bryant told CBC News. "Canada's not the problem here."
Bryant says the Trump administration is applying tariffs haphazardly and should focus its attention on countries that pay cheaper wages.
The IAM Union is particularly concerned about the Trump administration's tariffs on Canadian steel and aluminum, Bryant says.
"We have many manufacturers in aerospace, general manufacturing, shipbuilding that are dependent and reliant on that," he said. The Trump administration should not be "adding to their costs for those because that's going to jeopardize our members' jobs."
Progress in tariff talks
Canadian officials and their U.S. counterparts have said in recent weeks the two sides are making progress toward a trade deal.
While the Carney government is seeking as much of a reduction in tariffs as possible, Canada-U.S. Trade Minister Dominic LeBlanc has said Canadians should not expect a deal that brings zero tariffs.
Unions in the U.S., some of whom represent significant numbers of Canadian workers, are watching the talks closely and urging the Trump administration to shift its position.
Roxanne Brown, international president of the United Steelworkers (USW), signed a joint letter with Bryant this summer calling on the White House not to impose the threatened 50 per cent tariffs on some $28 billion worth of Canadian goods.
The international president of the United Steelworkers, Roxanne Brown, centre, is surrounded by union representatives during a Sept. 15 news conference in Laval, Que., addressing tariff and trade tensions between the U.S. and Canada. (Christinne Muschi/The Canadian Press)
"We had great concerns about the impacts those tariffs were going to have on our members' jobs," Brown told CBC News. "We wanted to be on the record to really push back against those tariffs."
Trump went ahead with the additional tariffs, which apply to a range of Canadian products including wood products, alcohol, cheese and sporting goods.
Steel tariffs need study, says USW
When it comes to steel production, the USW supports tariffs on what Brown calls "bad actors," countries that unfairly subsidize their industry or engage in dumping of cheap steel.
"There should be a clear study into that dynamic between the United States and Canada. If it doesn't exist, the tariffs shouldn't exist," Brown said.

She says the environment between the Trump administration and Ottawa right now is "pretty combative" and her union has been asking the White House for months to take a more sensible, practical approach with its northern neighbour.
"That trading relationship has benefited both of our countries, it's benefited the jobs in both of our countries and for our union, that's a really important relationship to maintain," said Brown.
United Auto Workers president Shawn Fain delivers the keynote address at the annual national conference of the union's political arm, on Feb. 9 in Washington, D.C. (Evelyn Hockstein/Reuters)
One major union whose leadership has at times supported Trump's tariffs is the United Auto Workers (UAW). Its president, Shawn Fain, despite having urged his members to elect Democrats in 2024, strongly welcomed the Trump administration's initial sweeping tariffs, including those on Canada and Mexico.
"Tariffs are a powerful tool in the toolbox for undoing the injustice of anti-worker trade deals," the UAW said in a statement in March 2025. "We are glad to see an American president take aggressive action on ending the free trade disaster that has dropped like a bomb on the working class."
UAW president backs 'strategic and targeted' tariffs
Fain and the UAW appeared to buy into Trump's argument that auto tariffs would bring good-paying jobs back to the U.S., and the White House communications team amplified his support.
In the past year and a half, after several Michigan leaders of the UAW publicly broke with their union president, Fain has pulled back somewhat on his full-throated backing of tariffs.
He campaigned in the midterms for Abdul El-Sayed, the winning Democratic candidate for U.S. Senate in Michigan, where tariffs on Canadian exports became a key election issue. In a webcast conversation with El-Sayed on Oct. 16, Fain insisted his support for tariffs has always been restricted to "strategic and targeted" ones.
"The unfortunate side of this is this administration has just managed to do blanket tariffs on everything and try to use it as political leverage," Fain said.
Ford vehicles are shown on the assembly line at the company's assembly plant in Louisville, Ky. U.S. President Donald Trump has threatened threat to raise tariffs on automobiles and auto parts from Canada to 50 per cent on Jan. 1. (Darron Cummings/The Associated Press)
The UAW did not respond to CBC's request for an interview this week.
Cathy Feingold, international director of the AFL-CIO, the largest federation of unions in the U.S., says the White House should be working with countries like Canada to address global trade challenges rather than punishing them.
"We completely oppose the chaotic nature in which the tariffs of the Trump administration have been imposed, especially on some of our closest allies and partners like Canada," Feingold told CBC News.

She says the AFL-CIO specifically opposes the 50 per cent tariffs imposed in August under Section 338 of the U.S. Tariff Act, the first time in nearly a century the section had been used.
"It's raised costs on both sides of the border. It disrupts cross-border industries. It's threatening union jobs on both side of the border," Feingold said.
She says she's hopeful the results of the midterms and the incoming Democratic control of Congress creates an opportunity for a shift toward trade policies that benefit workers in the U.S., Canada and elsewhere.
However, Trump has threatened to raise tariffs on automobiles and auto parts from Canada to 50 per cent on Jan. 1, a move that would put significant pressure on the bottom line of the three big Detroit-based automakers, Ford, General Motors and Stellantis.
