Mykola expects to drive his battered beige passenger minibus 20 to 24 times a day past the holes in the road on Kyiv’s damaged Northern Bridge that were caused by a Russian ballistic missile attack on Tuesday.
Buses are now the only motor transport allowed across the normally busy multi-lane crossing, though on their way into the city centre they have to skirt the tears in the tarmac, using the only functioning lane to get by.
“So what if there’s a hole? The bridge hasn’t collapsed. You need to know a bit about bridge construction,” says Mykola, 65, at the wheel of a half -full afternoon bus as it clatters on its way west.
Mykola operates a marshrutka, a single deck private bus, whose drivers are famed in Kyiv mythology for being able to multi-task: chatting, smoking and collecting fares, while driving as fast as possible to take passengers from rivals.
Passengers pay 25 hryvnia (42p) in cash and typically one passenger gets the unofficial job of taking money and handing back change on behalf of the driver. It turns out this has become the Guardian’s task, in order to conduct an interview.
“It would be very difficult to destroy the bridge. It would take a huge number of missiles, and if one or two hit it, it wouldn’t be the end of the world,” Mykola continues, with behind-the-wheel speculation that is so far accurate. By lunchtime the holes have been covered up, perhaps to avoid unsettling passengers eyeing the Dnipro River below.
Alongside its attacks on the capital’s electricity, heating and water systems, Russia has been attacking Kyiv’s bridges since early October, trying to cut off the city centre in the west from the newer, largely residential east, home to about a third of its population.
Moscow struck the bridges twice again this week, first on Tuesday – overshadowed by Ukraine’s partial destruction of the Kerch road bridge to Crimea. Then again during a noisy Thursday night, hours after Vladimir Putin promised there would be a “far more painful” response to the demolition of his pet project.
As of Friday, in an already traffic-snarled city, Kyiv’s other main road bridge, the Southern Bridge, was allowing motor traffic only from east to west. The new Podilskyi Bridge, damaged early on Friday, was similarly restricted, and the city’s mayor, Vitali Klitschko, told people to avoid car travel after a night in which six were killed.
The Northern Bridge remains open for public transport – plus any cyclists, scooter riders and walkers willing to brave the damp late autumn weather and ignore missile and drone alerts. But below, earthworks have begun to prepare for a floating pontoon bridge, one of eight planned, in case of the worst.
Dmytro Bespalov, a transport planning expert with Kyiv’s National University of Construction and Architecture, said it was difficult to destroy a suspension bridge like the Northern. They have “a strong construction, with a lot of repairability”, meaning damage can usually be fixed. Though there are holes in the roadway, its supporting structures were so far unscathed, he added.
Russia is thought to have been aiming ballistic missiles at the supporting pylon, requiring a precise and difficult to execute attack, though that is what Ukraine did at the culmination of its complex attack on the Kerch road bridge, which was brought down after a Flamingo cruise missile struck its supporting arch.
Yet, Bespalov cautioned that Kyiv was strategically vulnerable if Russia targets the bridges with “dozens or hundreds” of missiles, because the city did not have enough bridges from before the war. “In 2021, Kyiv was in third place in Europe for congestion on the TomTom traffic index,” he said, behind Istanbul and Moscow.
Particular concern is reserved for the pontoon bridge alternative. They can bear less traffic: “It would take 20 pontoon bridges to have half the current capacity,” Bespalov said – and they are far more vulnerable to missile and drone attack. “I don’t think it would be possible to use pontoons during a red [missile] or yellow [drone] alarm” – not least because vehicles could end up in the water.
At the first busy bus stop on the eastern left bank, Nataliya, 40, and Iryna, 39, are happy to make the crossing over the Northern Bridge. They say they have long got used to the difficulties of war: “The main thing is to get through the first 24 hours at home after the initial discomfort, and then we’ll adapt,” said Nataliya, a customer service specialist.
Iryna added: “If we start to despair, that’s exactly what they want. A bridge can be built. The main thing is that when the attack comes, there are no civilians or military personnel around. It’s just concrete, that’s all. They’ll either repair it or come up with something new.”
It is a core attitude of Kyiv’s remaining residents, who are already contending with hours of electricity shortages and a lack of heating, with the full chill of winter yet to arrive. People want to deal with problems as they arise rather than worrying excessively about what may come – though it is not always easy amid the daily attacks, including a daytime raid on Thursday in which a drone exploded near the Dutch embassy.
Oleksandr, 42, a construction worker, was waiting to get across the bridge. “If the bridges are destroyed we’ll have to leave,” he conceded. If that happened, he and his wife would move to her parents’ house across the river. “I bought them a generator there; they have their own water well. We’d already dug the well during the war. They used to have gas heating, but we’ve converted it to wood-burning. We’re preparing,” he said.
