Hakeem Jeffries poised to become 1st Black U.S. House Speaker, after internal vote by Democrats
Full vote in House, including Republican members, will take place in early January
Democrats nominated Hakeem Jeffries on Wednesday to be Speaker of the U.S. House of Representatives, as he brushed past skeptics and was on track to make history as the country's first Black American with the gavel.
Jeffries, of New York, secured the nod during closed-door voting, ahead of a full House vote when the new Congress, including Republican members, convenes in January. CNN reported that there were 11 No votes out of 262 cast.
The speaker of the House is second in the presidential line of succession, behind the vice-president.
Jeffries ran unopposed but faces a restive party eager for a change after voters ousted Republicans in the recent midterm elections in a rebuke of U.S. President Donald Trump and the party in power.
Phone calls and social media posts have been flooding the offices of returning Democrats and newly elected members demanding a fresh start. Protesters heckled Jeffries last week during a Democratic conference in Denver.
Ahead of Wednesday's vote, Jeffries said he was taking nothing for granted. He met with the caucuses that make up the House Democrats and talked to almost all members in what will be their large majority.
"I embrace those conversations," he said.
After the vote, Jeffries thanked his party for the support in a message on X.
"Together, we will deliver bold change. And make a big difference in the lives of the American people!"
Elevated to leader in 12 years
Jeffries will be part of a leadership trio with Rep. Katherine Clark of Massachusetts and Rep. Pete Aguilar of California, which would also represent historic firsts — Clark as the first female majority leader and Aguilar as the first Latino to serve as the majority whip. They also ran unopposed.

Jeffries, 56, was born in Brooklyn to a mother who was a social worker and a father who was a substance abuse counsellor. He attended Binghamton University for undergraduate studies, earning a master's in public policy from Georgetown University in Washington, D.C., and a law degree from New York University.
After two unsuccessful bids for a state assembly seat in New York, he succeeded in 2006. He was elected to U.S. Congress eight years later, joining the Congressional Caucus.
There are seven races yet to be projected in the 435-seat House of Representatives. Democrats have sewn up 257 seats, and with one of the biggest incoming majorities in decades, Jeffries will have room to spare, unlike current Speaker Mike Johnson from the Republicans.
When Rep. Nancy Pelosi of California decided to step aside from leadership in 2022 after the party's electoral defeat that handed Republicans the majority, it set the stage for this era. Pelosi, No. 2 Democratic Rep. Steny Hoyer of Maryland and Rep. James Clyburn of South Carolina had often moved together, rising, and then stepping back, from their long-held positions.
Jeffries, Clark and Aguilar have risen together, as a team, as the first new generation of leaders of the post-Pelosi era and will soon serve as leaders in the majority for the first time.
"There is one candidate in this race: Hakeem Jeffries," Democratic Rep. Alexandria Ocasio-Cortez said at the Capitol. "I think that everyone is far more concerned what that leadership means, what is it promising."

Seven incoming House progressives said in a joint statement that they voted against Jeffries.
"While we have seen meaningful progress, there is still work to be done to ensure that the direction our party takes this coming Congress reflects what our constituents are demanding of leadership," they said. "Those conversations are ongoing."
The seven are:
- Darializa Avila Chevalier, New York.
- Adam Hamawy, New Jersey.
- Frederick Haynes, Texas.
- Melat Kiros, Colorado.
- Donavan McKinney, Michigan.
- Chris Rabb, Pennsylvania.
- Claire Valdez, New York.
The Senate Democrats will decide on their leader early next month. Chuck Schumer of New York, who has been majority leader before, faces a challenge from Sen. Chris Van Hollen of Maryland.
Returning Rep. Yassamin Ansari of Arizona said the situations in the Senate and the House "are quite different."
She said Jeffries has been a collaborative leader who is listening to ideas from progressive caucus members like her and others.
"His job is to reflect all of us, not just a faction," Ansari said.
"It is more productive to focus on preparing on what we're going to do in the majority and what we need and want to get done in those first 100 days."
