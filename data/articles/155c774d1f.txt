The number of young women using hormonal contraception fell drastically in the past decade, with social media fuelling the “hormonal hesitancy” phenomenon.
New findings from the fourth National Survey of Sexual Attitudes and Lifestyles (Natsal-4), published in The Lancet, found that almost one in five women in Great Britain at risk of unintended pregnancy were not using effective contraception.
The research revealed a significant shift in contraceptive choices among younger women in particular. Use of hormonal contraception such as the pill among women aged 16 to 34 fell, while use among 16- to 24-year-olds dropped from 64.6% to 50.3% over the past decade.
Concerns about hormonal contraception have become prominent online, feeding into a phenomenon known as “hormone hesitancy”, an innate suspicion of hormonal methods including the contraceptive pill, implant and injection.
Doctors expressed concerns that the turn against hormonal contraception was being driven by online content promoting “natural” or holistic methods of contraception, which often framed synthetic hormones as harmful.
Misinformation online includes suggestions that hormonal birth control causes lasting harm to future fertility, or can have unknown side-effects.
Online content creators have also promoted online technology, such as fertility-tracking apps, which are less reliable than many other methods of contraception.
Those working in the sector have said that reduced access to sexual and reproductive health services was an issue, allowing harmful online content to fill the resulting void.
The study also found that the percentage of women aged 16 to 44 who said they definitely did not want children had more than doubled, from 7.7% to 16.6%.
The rise was predominantly driven by younger women, aged 16 to 34, among whom the proportion not wanting children had increased almost fourfold, from 4.4% to 16.6%.
The Natsal-4 survey, which happens once a decade, canvassed almost 7,000 people in England, Scotland and Wales between the ages of 16 and 59. It included findings that Britons were having less sex in general.
A third of people aged between 16 and 24 said they had never had sex, up from a fifth when the study was last carried out 12 years ago, while those under 44 said they were only having sex twice a month on average, down from once a week in 1990.
Dr Zara Haider, the president of the College of Sexual and Reproductive Healthcare, said: “The number of women who say they definitely don’t want children has doubled. Yet almost one in five women risk an unintended pregnancy because they aren’t using effective contraception. Cuts to the funding of healthcare services and limited access to good sex education, at school and beyond, may well be a factor.
“At the same time, concerns about hormonal contraception have become increasingly prominent in online conversations, contributing to what is known as ‘hormonal hesitancy’.”
She added that content about personal experiences could perform very well on social media and be a “fantastic resource”, but warned that “one woman’s negative experience will not necessarily be another’s”.
She said it was important women had “access to balanced information and feel able to explore their contraceptive options with the support of a healthcare professional”.
