Deaths caused by measles are not peaceful. In his hospital in Uganda, Dr Eric Wobudeya treats young infants after the virus has attacked their lungs and airways, or triggered pneumonia.
“These are children who are dying, and they are struggling for oxygen,” he said.
Around the world, children are facing the consequences of low or falling measles vaccination rates.
This week, international health officials announced an alarming development: that the US, Mexico and Bolivia had lost their measles-free status. They joined the unenviable club of nations that once had the deadly infection under control, but have seen it return – including earlier this year the UK, Canada, Austria, Armenia, Spain, Azerbaijan and Uzbekistan. Countries lose measles elimination status if the same strain of the disease has spread within their borders uninterrupted for over a year.
It is a trend driven by factors including misinformation, disruption to services during the Covid pandemic, and even the immunisation successes of previous decades that mean people have forgotten how dangerous measles can be. Aid cuts from wealthy countries including the US and UK are likely to exacerbate the situation in poorer countries, experts fear.
The frustration among health advocates is palpable. “We are seeing measles cases in places where the disease was under control, and children are paying the price,” said the Unicef executive director, Catherine Russell. “We have a measles vaccine that protects children and has saved nearly 59 million lives since 2000. We should not be losing ground to this preventable disease.”
Measles infection weakens a child’s immune system, leaving them more vulnerable to other infections for months or years, particularly if they are malnourished. It can cause brain damage, hearing loss or blindness. In 2024, according to World Health Organization figures, an estimated 11 million people caught measles – most of them under five – and 95,000 died.
The virus is extremely contagious, lingering in the air for up to two hours; up to 90% of unprotected people exposed to measles will catch it. There are fears it is the canary in the coalmine for the resurgence of other vaccine-preventable diseases.
Wobudeya, a senior consultant paediatrician at Mulago national referral hospital in Kampala, says measles disappeared from his hospital for a time.
Speaking at the Union World Conference on Lung Health, held in Brazil this week, he said: “Until 2023, we were not seeing measles, for a period of about maybe 10 years. We had a whole [measles] ward – that ward went dry, and actually it was translated into another ward.
“But in the last three or four years, measles came back. What causes measles to come back, is basically failure to vaccinate.”
Global measles vaccination rates have stalled below the 95% threshold needed to create herd immunity and prevent outbreaks of the virus. In 2025, 84% of eligible children received a first dose of vaccine against measles, dropping to 77% for the second dose.
The news from the Americas is not only bad for the countries involved. A measles outbreak anywhere can spread to other countries through travel, experts warned. While the risk is lower in countries with relatively high levels of vaccine uptake, almost everywhere will have vulnerable pockets. In Hackney in east London, for example, only 58% of five-year-olds starting school in 2025 had received two doses of the MMR vaccine.
Some children cannot be vaccinated because their immune system is somehow compromised, for example by blood cancer. Others are too young for routine vaccination. They rely on high vaccination coverage in the wider population to reduce their risk.
Their parents may face difficult decisions, said Dr Boghuma Titanji, an assistant professor of medicine at Emory University in Atlanta, US. “How do you keep these children safe in shared spaces like public transportation, daycares, schools, or just navigating the world at large? Do you keep them indoors, have them wear masks all the time, or live in perpetual fear of an infection that could be life-changing for your child?”
In the US, the health secretary, Robert F Kennedy Jr, has spread anti-vaccine messages, including questioning whether the MMR vaccine – which protects against measles, mumps and rubella – causes autism. The myth of a link between the jab and autism was first spread by the British doctor Andrew Wakefield in a paper in 1998. He was struck off the medical register over the discredited research.
“The US has historically been a public health leader and pacesetter for health interventions globally. I worry that the prevailing anti-vaccine narratives, which have contributed to declining vaccination rates and the resurgence of measles in the US, risk being exported to other countries as well,” said Titanji – potentially hitting “vaccine confidence, acceptance, and uptake” in those countries.
But the anti-vaccination movement is not the sole driver of declining vaccination levels, said Prof Alex Richter, an immunology expert at the University of Birmingham.
“We also need to consider the lack of positive messaging about the benefits of vaccination and the fact that many people no longer remember how serious diseases such as measles can be,” she said. “When people rarely encounter a disease, it becomes easier to underestimate the risks it poses.”
Practical barriers such as “difficulties booking or attending appointments, language barriers, unstable housing and challenges accessing healthcare” should not be overlooked, she added. “If we want to reverse this trend, we need to address both vaccine confidence and vaccine access.”
Simple systems such as offering out-of-hours appointments or sending reminders have been shown to boost uptake.
Many parts of the world have never been free of measles. The Democratic Republic of the Congo is dealing with the virus at the same time as authorities try to tackle an Ebola outbreak. In Bangladesh, a measles outbreak is raging, with almost 240,000 suspected cases and 1,282 deaths since March.
“I see cases surge when poverty levels are high and food security is low. And these conditions increase the risk for children to develop severe complications,” said Dr Duot Kezekiah, a general surgeon at Kuajok state hospital in South Sudan.
In countries like his, with struggling health systems, a lack of laboratory facilities means the virus may go unidentified for too long, the extensive support needed for a patient be unavailable, and the isolation that is needed to protect other patients is impossible.
Dr Ben Kasstan-Dabush, an assistant professor of global health at the London School of Hygiene & Tropical Medicine, said the Covid-19 pandemic had disrupted routine vaccination programmes around the world “and brought a need for intense catch-up and resources to identify and invite children who remained undervaccinated”.
He said efforts to increase vaccination coverage could be hit by “major cuts to funding for global health and development from the US, and particularly the UK”.
But losses in measles elimination status can be reversed. And even in some of the hardest places to reach, new children are getting their jabs. Since 2022, the Reach consortium, led by the International Rescue Committee, has negotiated with governments and de facto authorities to vaccinate more than 1 million children in Chad, Nigeria, Ethiopia, Sudan, South Sudan and Somalia, living in areas that had been closed off to humanitarian teams.
“We cannot afford to be complacent. We need to make it easier for people to get vaccinated, challenge misinformation wherever it arises, and remind the public why vaccination matters,” said Dr David Hughes, a senior lecturer in the school of biology at the University of St Andrews. “Protecting against measles is a collective responsibility (being vaccinated yourself protects your family, friends and strangers we commute with), and we must act to prevent a disease we know how to control from becoming a serious threat again.”
