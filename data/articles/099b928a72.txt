“Nightmare at 20,000 Feet” is one of the all-time great episodes of The Twilight Zone. Starring William Shatner, the story is about a man who is taking his first flight after having had a nervous breakdown. During the flight, he looks out the window and sees a creature, called a gremlin, on the wing that taunts him and tears apart pieces of the plane. No one can see the gremlin but him, and even his own wife attributes it to his fragile mental state. The story is a brilliant commentary on paranoia and Shatner was captivating in the role. It was so good that it sometimes overshadows Shatner’s other great episode of The Twilight Zone, which premiered 66 years ago today.
Nick of Time
In the second season of the legendary anthology series The Twilight Zone, William Shatner made his first appearance on the show in an episode titled “Nick of Time.” In it, he plays a man named Don Carter and Patricia Breslin plays his wife, Pat Carter. Together, they portray a young couple traveling to New York City when their car breaks down in the small town of Ridgeview, Ohio. They are told that it is going to take a few hours for the mechanic to fix it, so they head to a small diner named the Busy Bee Cafe for some lunch.
While they are at their table, they notice their napkin holder is attached to a mechanical fortune-telling device where, if you insert a penny, it will answer any question you ask it by dispensing a small card with an answer. They’re simple yes or no answers, like a Magic 8 Ball might give, but Shatner’s character, who is deeply superstitious, grows obsessed with the machine, especially after it appears to be right about things. For example, he’s up for a promotion at his job, so he asks the device if he got it, and it says he did. Then Don calls his office to confirm that he did indeed get the promotion.
As the Mystic Seer’s answers become more foreboding, Don becomes increasingly fixated on it to the point that he is unable to leave the restaurant without consulting the machine. Throughout, the viewer never quite knows if the machine actually can predict the future, or if Don is just reading into things.
The whole story is a commentary on the “tyranny of fear and superstition,” as the story’s closing narration describes it. And with a rabbit’s foot and four-leafed clover on his keychain, Don Carter is clearly meant to be a deeply superstitious man. It’s also an equally astute commentary on obsession, which Shatner portrays with a convincing sense of desperation. It’s kind of funny to see how much his acting has been mocked over the past several decades when his early work is so full of subtlety and humanity. Don Carter feels like a man trapped by his demons, and you really do feel for him.
This episode is also notable for having one of the great props of early television. The Mystic Seer machine was patterned after real novelty fortune-telling devices that sat in some diners after World War II. Mechanical devices like this, or miniature jukeboxes at tables, were common back then. The only thing that was unusual about this one was the grinning little devil head on top, which sat on a spring. It is a simple design, but it also seems to be mocking Don for his obsession and the control this device has over him. Decades later, the Mystic Seer has been made into a prop replica a few times over for Twilight Zone fans. It's easy to see why.
Like the Mystic Seer itself, the brilliance of this Twilight Zone episode is its simplicity. The setting is just a small town with a diner in it, and the setup is simply a superstitious man who becomes preoccupied with a fortune-telling machine. Yet Shatner’s portrayal of obsession and desperation makes for something really entertaining and poignant.
