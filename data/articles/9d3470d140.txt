Every comic strip has an evolutionary arc. Every cartoonist changes their style over time as they hone their craft, develop their sense of humor shifts, master their particular style, and/or pander to their audience. Sometimes an early character may all but disappear from the comic, or some incidental member of the cast may take up the spotlight. It's part of the fun of going on archive binges, whether it's old newspaper comics or long-running web series. Comics are living things, and few of them stay in the places where they started.
A useful example is Tom Batiuk's Funky Winkerbean, which went from a high school comedy to a surprisingly depressing slice-of-life strip that rarely featured the title character. You can also point to Bloom County, which is essentially the Opus Show after the first couple of years, or Doonesbury, which often goes for months at a time without checking in on any of its core protagonists.
In Peanuts' case, it was a process of gradual accumulation. In the 1950s, Snoopy was depicted as a normal dog who rarely had any dialogue, but Charles Schulz gradually added more elements to Snoopy's character as he went. By the 1970s or so, Snoopy was both Charlie Brown's lazy dog and the protagonist of what amounted to his own, much stranger comic. Sometimes the kids can tell what he's doing, and sometimes they can't.
In the 1990s, Snoopy's various adventures came to dominate much of Peanuts. At the same time, Schulz also switched some of Peanuts' focus away from his established cast of kids and towards an obscure character from the '70s. That led to the biggest theft of the spotlight in Peanuts, as from 1993 onward, much of the comic became a story about Rerun Van Pelt.
A new baby brother?
Rerun is older than many Peanuts fans realize. He was initially introduced in 1972 as Lucy and Linus' newborn little brother, much to Lucy's dismay. (She wanted a sister.) Rerun appeared a few more times over the course of the '70s and '80s, but Schulz, by his own admission, quickly ran out of ideas for how to use him. For about 20 years, Rerun ended up in the Peanuts cameo squad alongside other half-abandoned characters like Patty and Violet.
In 1993, Schulz pulled Rerun out of obscurity. He was now five years old and a new entrant to kindergarten. Schulz's widow Jean has theorized that Rerun's prominence in the '90s was due to Schulz becoming a grandfather, as he once again had little kids running around in real life.
However, it came to pass, Rerun was one of the main characters in Peanuts' final decade. With most of Peanuts' established cast having already gone through most of their big developmental milestones, on or off-panel, Rerun's jokes revolved around early childhood adventures, like learning how to tie his shoes. He was also occasionally subjected to being taken on long bike rides with his unseen mom, who'd strap Rerun into a seat on her bicycle as she went around town.
Rerun's single best running joke, however, was his sense of confusion. It wasn't enough to be told how things are done; Rerun wanted to know why. If you think about it, there's a great deal of our culture that we're raised to take as read, like the rules of basketball or randomly shaking your neighbors down for candy on Halloween. Rerun refused to simply accept things without question, which worked to shine a spotlight on parts of life that the reader might have taken for granted.
One particularly funny example is from the comic for June 12, 1994. Lucy is trying to teach Rerun about pitching cards into a container, a.k.a. card toss or card flip. She hands him a deck of cards and tells Rerun, "See how many cards you can throw into the basket." Naturally, Rerun simply throws the entire deck at once.
It's a down-to-earth sort of punchline for '90s Peanuts, which had drifted much of the way off into Snoopy's brand of magical realism, where it was never entirely obvious how much of his fantasy life was actually happening. Rerun by comparison was a back-to-basics sort of character, and many of his stories and jokes in the '90s are a return to the sort of early childhood humor that initially put Peanuts on the map.
