In July, Montana became the first state to license clinics to offer patients who are not terminally ill drugs and devices that the Food and Drug Administration has not approved. Supporters call state Senate bill 535 a first-in-the-nation expansion of the “right to try.” Critics say it lets patients, including medical tourists from other states, pay out of pocket for unproven therapies. They are both right, in different ways. While the expansion of access to experimental treatments may provide patients with hope, the risks that patients may pay high costs for ineffective and even unsafe treatments remain high.
The goal is to expand access to experimental therapies, with stem cell, anti-aging therapies, and biologics being likely use cases. But the law leaves major questions unanswered: How much will patients pay? Who will bear the cost if complications arise later? And, because these therapies are being used outside a rigorous research setting, how will anyone know whether they actually work?
For patients, a primary concern is likely to be cost. SB535 makes patients financially responsible for the experimental treatment — with the price set by the company supplying the drug or device. The required patient agreement must describe “all the anticipated costs” of the treatment itself. But patients will likely face additional costs, including travel to Montana for those out of state, and follow-up care.
Patients are also, in theory, responsible to pay out of pocket for medical care related to any complications — they are not supposed to go through their insurance companies. But how will insurers determine if a medical problem stems from an experimental treatment? That’s left ambiguous by the law. Long-term medical effects will be particularly hard to trace, especially if a product hasn’t yet had robust safety testing and documentation of its long-term side effects. Thus, care for complications may fall on patients, or it may be paid by insurers who had no say in the use of an experimental treatment.
And how are people to judge whether a treatment is worth its price? For approved therapies, there’s trial data on survival rates, plus continued post-market study. These data help clarify the expected benefits for specific patients. SB535 does not require companies to conduct comparable analyses, nor are companies bound to set prices with any value framework in mind.
Proponents of SB535 argue that skipping costly trials will lower development costs and, eventually, prices. However, if companies wish to generate evidence for conventional FDA approval, they will need to go beyond the requirements in SB535. If they fail to collect detailed patient characteristics such as history of disease and patient demographics, it will be hard to determine how the drug might work in a larger patient population. Supporters say the data will resemble real-world evidence, but the validity of real-world evidence still depends on clear study methods and consistent data collection and reporting.
It’s also possible that SB535 could undermine the rigorous testing needed for FDA approval. That’s because SB535 does not require the same patient protections, data collection requirements, or oversight that the FDA requires. Some companies may forgo the clinical trial FDA pathway entirely, while others may find that direct access disincentivizes patients from enrolling in trials.
None of this means patients with few options for treatment should be dismissed or ignored. But they can make an informed decision about how much to pay for treatment only if the true costs and risks are visible. Montana currently has no systematic way to assess how patients interpret costs, what they actually incur and pay, or how those costs relate to short- and long-term outcomes.
If other states want to expand access to experimental treatment, there are other models they could look to, such as CMS’s Coverage with Evidence Development. It requires systematic collection and reporting of data on clinical effectiveness and short- and long-term harms.
States could also require companies to collect data on outcomes for 15 years, just like the FDA does for cell and gene therapies. These types of data can enable transparency and may help patients make informed decisions about potential treatments in consultation with their care team.
Montana can still move in that direction by requiring standardized cost disclosure before consent, a registry with defined long-term follow-up, and independent evaluation of financial and clinical outcomes.
Without those steps, we will not know whether state right to try laws can be a cost-effective way to generate evidence and access to emerging therapeutics. Patients who try may be left physically and financially vulnerable, with little knowledge gained from their risk.
