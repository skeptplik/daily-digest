Anthropic will be valued at $1.5tn (Â£1.14tn) when the five-year-old AI firm makes its stock market debut on Thursday, becoming one of the most valuable companies in the world.
The firm behind the chatbot Claude said its shares will be priced at $83 when it lists on the Nasdaq, a tech-focused stock exchange based in New York.
Anthropic's flotation will make it the first US firm focused exclusively on AI to go public. The seven founders, who set the business up in 2021, will become multi-billionaires.
It will also mark a crucial test for the fast-growing industry, which faces questions over whether the huge amounts of money being poured into the technology will pay off.
The firm said earlier this month it would offer its shares for between $70 and $78 each, which valued the company at up to $1.3tn.
But demand for Anthropic's stock debut - commonly referred to as its initial public offering, or IPO - is very high, according to one early investor in the company who spoke to the BBC on condition of anonymity.
They said shares were more than 10 times oversubscribed ahead of the public listing, meaning demand exceeded the number of shares available.
If that turns out to be the case, Anthropic's valuation could quickly blow past its target on Thursday when trading begins.
Leah Bramwell from Canaccord Wealth said demand for the shares had been "well supported across the board" with strong interest from both institutional and retail investors.
She said that "gives comfort that we're not looking at just a sort of retail-driven frenzy into into AI names".
Crucial test for AI
Depending on how strong demand is, Anthropic could exceed the early market valuation of Elon Musk's SpaceX, which went public in June worth at $2.2tn.
However, in the weeks that followed, SpaceX's share price fell sharply.
As it stands, Anthropic will raise more than $68bn from investors and an additional $26bn from share sales to technology firms including Nvidia which has a long track record of funding firms that buy its AI chips.
OpenAI, the maker of ChatGPT and Anthropic's biggest AI rival, is expected to go public next year.
Until then, Anthropic will be the only public company in the US that is entirely focused on the technology, or one that was founded and built around AI tools and products. Its scope is different from tech giants like Google and Microsoft that also develop AI but are involved in other tech services.
There is excitement around Anthropic's listing, but also some concern from investors around its ability to generate future revenue and profits
Yang Wang, a principle financial analyst with Counterpoint Research, said the central issue for Anthropic is whether its business "can keep pace" as hundreds of billions of dollars in financial obligations come due in the next couple of years.
"Nevertheless, it is not an everyday occurrence for public investors to gain direct exposure to a frontier-model developer with the potential to shape the next stage of productivity," Wang said.
Rapid rise
Anthropic was started by seven former OpenAI employees, led by brother and sister Dario Amodei and Daniela Amodei
Dario Amodei has been outspoken in his belief that AI tools may soon be so powerful that they present a threat to human existence.
The company has urged developers to slow the development of AI, arguing that advanced systems could pose "catastrophic" risks to humanity if they are not handled with safeguards.
OpenAI has also voiced similar sentiments and delayed its plans to list on the stock market to address AI safety concerns.
Nonetheless, Anthropic is looking to grow quickly and gain more access to financing through the public markets.
Stock market trading in technology stocks has been volatile in recent weeks following the US midterm elections when Democrats took control of both chambers of Congress.
Investors are worried about tougher regulation of AI, after Democrat candidates opposed the data centres needed by the technology.
Marta Norton, who heads strategy at investment firm Empower, said the decision by Anthropic to go public now, in a time of market volatility, comes down to the simple fact that the company needs access to more money.
"AI is financially demanding," Norton told the BBC. "The public markets open up the financial spigot a little bit more and so the time has come."
But broader anxieties have started to grow about the AI industry's business model, which is reliant on expensive computer chips that are in short supply.
The technology is dependent on controversial data centres and funding such operations require deals with other tech companies.
The US Federal Reserve has expressed concern about the rapid growth of AI-related debt being financed through "complex and opaque" structures, according to minutes from its October meeting to discuss interest rates.
A few Fed members also noted that "AI-related exposures were becoming increasingly concentrated among a small number of firms".
