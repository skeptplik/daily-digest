Tenants in Madrid say Toronto-based Brookfield is pricing them out of formerly public housing
Canadian investment dollars are one of the villains in Spain's election fight over housing
At a Madrid apartment complex just outside the city's centre, red maple leaves hang upside down in windows along with defiant posters urging Canadian investment money to get out of their neighbourhood.
"They are pushing us out of our houses," said Rafael Cuadrado, who has lived in the complex for 20 years.
The main target of the residents' wrath is Toronto-based Brookfield Corporation.
A sign on a window of a Madrid apartment accuses Toronto-based Brookfield of treating tenants unfairly. In a statement to CBC News, Brookfield said it complies with all 'applicable Spanish tenancy legislation, including statutory tenant protections and extension requirements.' (Jason Ho/CBC)
One of Canada's premier financial and investment institutions, Brookfield owns 900 units in Cuadrado's residential complex, along with approximately 4,100 others around the Spanish capital.
Cuadrado and other residents accuse Brookfield of planning to let their leases expire and then flip their homes for market prices that few here can afford. He says several of his neighbours have already received notices from Brookfield's management company informing them their tenancies will not be extended.
CBC News has seen one letter sent to a tenant who was told their expiring lease would not be renewed and they would have to turn over the keys to their apartment.
It's not illegal, but the residents say it's unfair.
"Our daughter came here when she was two years old. This is our first house ... so this is our life," Cuadrado told CBC News in his living room.
Many of the residents are long-time tenants who moved into the buildings when they used to be part of Madrid's public housing stock. Most pay rents well below the current market rate, as a succession of lease extensions and government-mandated rent caps have kept their outlay relatively low.
Raphael Cuadrado lives in a Madrid apartment complex owned by Brookfield, one of Canada's top investment firms. He and other residents fear the company plans to flip their units when their leases expire. (Jason Ho/CBC)
Cuadrado says he pays 930 euros a month for his 800-square-foot, three-bedroom apartment. The unit would likely fetch about 1,500 euros on the open market.
"Why do we have to leave our house because there is a big investment company coming from abroad to get a profit [from] us?" he said. "No way."
'Vulture funds'
Anger over housing in Spain has hit a breaking point.
The eviction of Madrid resident Maricarmen Abascal in late September, after the 87-year-old woman was unable to pay a hefty rent increase, sent protesters into the streets. Authorities evicted her on the back of a stretcher and she died in hospital a few weeks later.
Her eviction prompted Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez to call an election for Nov. 29, with housing the central issue.
Spain's public unions, which allied with the tenants, are casting money from institutional investors, such as pension plans, as one of the primary villains. And some of them are Canadian.
"'Vulture funds' are what they call them," said Alicia Del Rio, who is with Sindicato de Inquilinas de Madrid (Madrid Tenants Union), one of the country's most prominent advocates for renters.
Del Rio blames a succession of cash-strapped Spanish governments for laying out the welcome mat for foreign investors, who she says have in turn treated Spanish residential properties as a commodity to be flipped, with little regard for the people living in them.
Alicia Del Rio is with Sindicato de Inquilinas de Madrid, Spain's most prominent tenants union. (Jason Ho/CBC)
"[The government] gave the message, 'Welcome to Spain, this is going to be your paradise.'"
Canadian owners
Canadian retirement funds and investment firms are designed to seek out safe returns — wherever they can find them. And Spain is seen as an attractive place to grow that money.
Along with Brookfield, Canada Pension Plan Investments has a roughly 900-million-euro portfolio ($1.4 billion Cdn) in the Spanish housing sector, although it's focused on student housing rather than residential homes. It owns approximately 13,000 dorm-type beds across Spain and Portugal, concentrated in cities with large universities.
In a statement to CBC News, CPP said its investments help "expand the overall supply of housing available to students without removing existing homes from the conventional residential market."
HOOPP — Health Care of the Ontario Pension Plan — owns approximately 700 million euros ($1.1 billion Cdn) worth of Spanish properties, including 27 residential buildings and 2,300 Madrid homes.
The Spanish tenant unions haven't raised any specific concerns about HOOPP's activities or its properties.
Whether foreign investment firms are cornering the country's housing market has become one of Spain's most fiercely debated issues.
Numbers from the Bank of Spain say institutional investors control only a tiny share — roughly two per cent — of the country's total housing stock.
The tenant unions, however, say the fact that many of those properties used to be public, non-market housing for middle- or lower-income families matters far more than their share of the national total.
Spain's Prime Minister Pedro Sanchez appears at a campaign rally in Palma de Mallorca on Nov. 14, ahead of the Nov. 29 general election. (Francisco Ubilla/Reuters)
They also claim that the concentration of apartments in central areas of cities gives companies like Brookfield outsized power to drive up rents across entire neighbourhoods.
Brookfield used to be chaired by Prime Minister Mark Carney, but he had left the company more than a year before it bought the Spanish homes in March 2026 from U.S. private equity giant Blackstone.
Blackstone held the buildings for 13 years. Residents claim Brookfield's intention is to sell the apartments once their existing leases finish.
Residents of the Madrid building told CBC News that when Brookfield eventually puts their apartments on the open market, they believe they could fetch upward of 600,000 euros apiece — a price few of them can pay.
"It is a constant anguish that is affecting our mental health," said Yutzy Fany Vargas Jimenez, who has lived in the complex for 20 years.
Engaging residents
In a statement to CBC News, Brookfield says any allegation that it is forcing people out of their homes is false.
"We comply with all applicable tenancy laws and tenant protections, and allegations that we terminate rental contracts in breach of these protections are inaccurate," said the statement.
None of the residents that CBC News spoke to had suggested Brookfield had cut short an existing lease. Rather, their concerns were about what will happen when their current tenancy contracts expire.
"We are working closely with our local operating partner, to engage with residents and address their concerns, while continuing to invest in the maintenance and quality of these homes and providing opportunities for residents who wish to become homeowners," the Brookfield statement continued.
The company didn't explicitly address whether it plans to terminate leases when current contracts expire, as the residents claim. However, the company did say it will review individual tenancy matters on a case-by-case basis.
Residents of a Madrid apartment complex say Brookfield intends to force them out of their homes when their leases expire. The company told CBC News that all of its rental arrangements in Spain 'reflect the applicable Spanish legal framework, including statutory requirements governing lease duration.' (Jason Ho/CBC)
Election issue
The cost of housing has become a central issue in Spain's upcoming election, although the roots of the problem can be traced back almost two decades.
Sara Herrero, vice-dean for academic planning and financial management at EAE Madrid business school, says after the 2008 global financial crisis, Spain was broke and its government turned to private capital to bail it out.
"The situation was really horrible," Herrero recalled, as governments and municipalities were forced to unload real estate they had on their balance sheets.
She said at the time, international funds were among the only buyers willing to inject billions of euros into the Spanish economy, helping banks clean up their toxic debt.
"What if they didn't come? What would have happened with these many residential houses if these supposed 'vulture funds' didn't come to Spain?" she said.
"They were doing a legal thing."
Sara Herrero is vice-dean of academic planning and financial management at EAE Madrid, one of Spain's top business schools. (Jason Ho/CBC)
Soaring prices
But recently, property values in the country have soared, rising 34 per cent in the past five years.
"The problem is the basic economic problem of supply and demand," Herrero said. "It's the perfect storm of low salaries, high population growth and high taxes."
The Bank of Spain also estimates upward of 300,000 homes have been converted to short-term tourism rentals, further displacing long-term tenants.
The left-wing government of Prime Minister Sanchez is trying to harness the housing anger to help with his re-election bid — even though the price hikes have all happened under his watch.
People in Madrid take part in a 24-hour nationwide general strike on Nov. 11 demanding wages that allow access to what demonstrators say is decent housing. (Violeta Santos Moura/Reuters)
Sanchez even appeared to give tacit support to the unions leading the general strike, as he passed tenant-friendly decrees that would likely be reversed if he fails to get re-elected.
His opponents on the political right, the People's Party and the far-right VOX, accused Sanchez of creating the crisis by passing legislation that made it hard for landlords to stay profitable, thereby discouraging new supply.
The latest opinion polls in Spain continue to favour a right-of-centre coalition taking power after Nov. 29, although they also indicate Sanchez's party has shown some recent momentum.
Tenant Raphael Cuadrado says he has repeatedly written to the Canadian Embassy in Madrid, hoping someone there can help residents who are worried about their future.
So far, he says he has not received a reply.
"This is day-by-day fighting, and this is not a life," he said.
In a statement to CBC News, Global Affairs Canada said any questions about Brookfield and its activities in Spain should be directed at the company.
