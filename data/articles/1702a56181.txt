We talk a lot about graphics in video games; we know how important it is to play on a decent screen so we can see what we’re shooting at … or running away from. But what about sound? Explosions, dialogue, the wheezy breathing of a nearby monster – arguably, audio quality is just as important.
Many modern games now support 3D or spatial audio, which lets you detect exactly where a sound is coming from in the game world. In survival horror adventures, you can hear the zombie lurking in the next room, and in popular shooters such as Fortnite and Apex Legends, you can pinpoint the footsteps of approaching enemies.
Sadly, most of us can’t afford elaborate surround sound speaker systems for our living rooms, but that’s where gaming headsets come in. They not only let you play without disturbing anyone else in the house (or office), but are also brilliant for spatial audio. I’ve tested an array of models to suit most budgets and needs, and, hopefully, with my help, you’ll never be surprised by a zombie again.
Why you should trust me
I have been playing video games for 45 years and writing about them for my entire career as a journalist, so I really understand what makes a good piece of gaming hardware. During that time, I have reviewed hundreds of joypads, monitors, keyboards and, yes, headsets, developing a range of tests to sort the quality gear from the overpriced gimmicks.
How I tested
I tested 20 headsets, paying close attention to comfort, which is vital for long gaming sessions, as well as sound quality, noise-cancelling capabilities and any other special features. As someone who plays video games every day for a living (it’s a tough job, etc), I was able to put every headset through hours of testing with a variety of games. I wore them while blasting strangers in Call of Duty, scoring volleys in EA Sports FC and building scale reproductions of Norman castles in Minecraft.
I also made sure I was getting good spatial information to detect exactly where my next opponent/victim was coming from. And naturally, the microphone has to make your voice crystal clear when talking to (or yelling at) teammates in the heat of battle.
To gauge the overall build quality, I put them through the ultimate trial: lending them to my teenage sons. If anything came back in one piece, I knew it was well constructed. My aim was to find headsets that felt light yet sturdy, sounded good, and aided – maybe even improved (if that’s possible) – my gaming performance.
Once tested, the headsets were donated to SpecialEffect, a charity that makes video games accessible to people with disabilities.

Wired vs wireless gaming headsets
The first decision you’re likely to make is between a wired and a wireless headset. The former tends to be a little cheaper; they’re also convenient and don’t require batteries. Wireless headsets free you from the tyranny of cables and often offer a range of connectivity options, including the standard 2.4GHz radio frequency, which will require a dongle receiver to be plugged into your PC or console, and Bluetooth for your smartphone. They do introduce a tiny amount of latency (the delay between your game generating a sound and you hearing it), but it is extremely low (about 10 milliseconds) in modern headsets.
Are open-back or closed-back headphones better for gaming?
Another key decision is whether to buy open-back or closed-back headphones. The former have grills or holes in the back of the ear cups to allow sound to move through and create a more natural audio experience. Closed-back headphones tend to create a seal around your ears so sound can’t escape: they’re better for booming bass and privacy, but can create distorted extraneous sounds – and make your ears hot. Most gaming headsets are closed-back, and this would be my recommendation for most gamers, as they put you right inside the virtual environment with no distractions. Many also offer active noise cancellation (ANC), a technology that emits sound waves to counteract and reduce incoming noise – great if you’re playing in a loud environment.
What audio quality do I need?
Audio quality is dictated by the drivers, which sit in each earcup, turning electric signals into sound waves. They come in different sizes, ranging from 40mm to 90mm, with the latter being the loudest, but not necessarily the best. Smaller drivers cope better with high frequencies, so they’re good for hearing footsteps; larger drivers provide heavier bass, so they’re great for games with pounding soundtracks or lots of cool explosions. Another important specification is frequency response, which is the range of audio frequencies a headset can reproduce: gaming headsets are usually in the 20–20,000 Hz range.
Spatial sound is important because it immerses you in the game world and helps you locate nearby enemies or loot chests – the good news is that many headsets support it, because the processing is done by the PC or console via software such as Dolby Atmos or Windows Sonic. However, accuracy is affected by the driver quality and the acoustic tuning in the headset. Some headsets come with apps that let you fine tune the range of the frequencies within the audio signal – ie the EQ (equalisation) settings – so that you can boost the bass, mid-range or treble.
How much should I spend?
Decent headsets that offer acceptable comfort, build quality and audio start at about £40 for a wired model and £60 for wireless. At about £100-£150, you’re getting a headset made with better materials and higher-quality drivers, as well as other features such as Bluetooth connectivity for your phone. At the top end, £250 upwards, you can expect highly configurable, studio-quality audio, supreme comfort and often the ability to connect to multiple consoles, switching seamlessly between them.
Can you buy a secondhand/refurbished gaming headset?
The problem with headsets is that they have a lot of delicate parts. They’re quite an intimate piece of equipment. Earcups will trap grease, sweat and skin particles from previous users, so unless these can be replaced, it’s unhygienic, to say the least. Also, you won’t know whether they’ve been looked after well simply from looking at a photo online. If you’re determined to buy secondhand on a site such as eBay, make sure the seller has excellent feedback and offers returns so you can inspect the headset when it arrives. Otherwise, look at stores and marketplaces such as Back Market or CEX that offer warranties on used goods.
Alternatively, Corsair sells refurbished headsets on its website. Honestly though, if money is an issue, I’d rather buy the best new budget or mid-range headset I can afford rather than a secondhand model with no warranty and an unknown hygiene history.
