Another Hunger Games movie, a prequel to the initial series, premieres later this month, and with it comes another press tour full of fantastically far-fetched fashion. As the promotional events begin to stack up for Hunger Games: Sunrise on the Reaping, we are reminded of the familiar lore of this dystopian empire: In a fictionalized future America called Panem, the 12 districts that supply resources to the rich and powerful people of the Capitol are forced to send two children to fight to the death each year. The excess of the Capitol is signaled in the films via outlandish style, which the newest film’s cast members have been attempting to channel in their recent looks. The goal is to be extreme—but in an era of extremes on the red carpet, these over-the-top ensembles actually don’t look so out of place.
Elle Fanning plays a young Effie Trinket, who is essentially a Capitol stylist dressing the young “tributes” in all sorts of fanciful fashions—and doing the same for herself. (In the first film series, the character wore a lot of McQueen.) So far, Fanning’s press looks have deliberately channeled that outlandishness. For a London fan screening, she wore a gown from Robert Wun’s Fall 2026 couture collection that subverted ideas of childlike wonder. Her ensemble began in miniature with a music-box-style ballerina atop a bubblegum pink fascinator. The corresponding human-size gown features a doubled-up bodice, the outer layer appearing to peel off, above a full tulle skirt, all in that same dusty pink.
For her other appearances, Fanning has established a de facto uniform of precisely tailored skirt suits, each paired with some sort of coordinating fascinator tilted just so above her brow. She has selected a soft pink pleated skirt suit from Valentino complete with a ruffled collar, gloves, and a butterfly-trimmed chapeau; a sharp-shouldered version in a deep, almost reptilian satin green from Demna’s Gucci; and a floral, bow-embellished one from Vivienne Westwood. The looks are decidedly feminine in a manner that is at once both futuristic and retro in very Capitol fashion.
Fanning’s stylist Samantha McMillen has been vocal about the references to the character, with captions like “Gucci Effie | @demna puts “The Hunger” into @thehungergames,” and “Elle bringing 🦋 “Sunrise” 🦋 to the Academy Gala.” And it’s not just Fanning doing this either. Costar Mckenna Grace recently recreated a Katniss Everdeen Capitol tour look in which Katniss’s dress quite literally caught fire. Created by Vietnamese designer Cong Tri, who is known for his sculptural silhouettes, the recreation nixed the pyrotechnics but printed graphic flames onto the skirt and bodice. With the help of stylist Molly Dickson, Grace also revived Katniss’s wedding dress with a frothy white, silver-embellished gown by Monique Lhuillier.
I haven’t seen the most recent films, but all of them examine the same concept of this dystopian society propped up by the Hunger Games themselves at different points on a timeline. In the first film specifically, the older Trinket uses their version of a press tour to craft a narrative around Katniss and her fellow tribute Peeta Mellark and win favor among their audience through larger-than-life fashion. The aforementioned fire dress referenced Everdeen’s district’s coal mining industry, and the wedding dress was meant to create the illusion of a love story between the two of them. The series of looks is designed to be projected to the surrounding communities—and garner as much attention as possible. The characters can quite literally benefit later in the life-and-death-style games from the loyalties and sympathies the theatrics spawn.
It’s only in recent years that this idea of method dressing, or theming press tour looks around an actor’s current project, has become commonplace. It began in earnest with the work of Law Roach and Zendaya around the Dune and Spider-Man franchises, Challengers, and The Drama. It was perhaps commercially crystallized in 2023 by Andrew Mukamal and Margot Robbie with Barbie—a press tour that spawned so much digital attention it earned its own coffee-table book—and, more recently, Wuthering Heights. Now, it has proof of concept, attracting stars like Cynthia Erivo and Ariana Grande in their promotion of Wicked, the new cast of Sense and Sensibility, and Lili Reinhart’s rom-com references for The Love Hypothesis. At its most reductive, the tactic is a surefire way to drum up conversation on social media; at its best, a clever way to add depth to a project’s storytelling.
As press tours become more and more oriented towards social media, it’s difficult to isolate the influence of method dressing on the red carpet’s current proclivity towards extremes. Everyone is trying to capitalize on the sort of hype that the Met Gala ensembles can drum up. Your reference needs to be loud enough to cut through the noise—pyrotechnics optional. It’s perhaps why avant-garde, couture, and couture-level designers like Wun, who dressed a large handful of stars at this year’s Met Gala, are carving out more and more space among these sorts of promotional events. At the same time, brands like Standing Ground and Matieres Fecales—it’s only a matter of time before Fanning wears one of those skirt suits—are also gaining traction as stylists treat the red carpet more like a magazine editorial than an occasion to get dressed up.
Then last night, Grace added another layer. She and Dickson have begun to look outside the films to reference looks of Jennifer Lawrence’s own. Lawrence played Katniss in the first movies. For a Madrid premiere, she wore a Miss Sohee couture dress that paid homage to the Givenchy naked dress Lawrence wore for this year’s Golden Globes. It all feels very meta, calling into question the role of fashion in these promotional spaces, real or fictional. On the IRL Hunger Games red carpet, the reference is dystopian extravagance—but with the rise of method dressing and the corresponding celebration of larger, louder looks, has dystopian extravagance already become the norm? In this case, it may not be life and death, but it could make or break your movie.
