Eight years after GlobalFoundries abandoned the development of its 7nm FinFET technology, the company is preparing to return to 7nm-class performance, this time by going via a different route. Its FDX Fusion process, built on FD-SOI (fully depleted silicon-on-insulator) technology, promises 7nm-class performance using advanced planar transistors, with no EUV lithography necessary.
In an interview with Tom's Hardware Premium, GlobalFoundries' Sudipto Bose, vice president of the FD-SOI business unit, shared new details about its FDX Fusion technology, including its 8nm-class logic density, advanced planar transistors, manufacturing costs, and plans to begin commercial production in 2028. The company also explained how it intends to compete with conventional FinFET technologies and why it believes FD-SOI has a future beyond the 22nm-class generation. Some elements of this interview transcript have been edited for flow and clarity.

7nm-class FD-SOI node
GlobalFoundries' FDX Fusion is a new manufacturing technology that promises 8nm-class logic density and 7nm-class power and performance without EUV lithography or FinFET transistors, which greatly reduces design costs and manufacturing costs at the cost of significantly lower transistor density compared to 7nm-class nodes.
Instead, the process relies on advanced planar fully depleted silicon-on-insulator (FD-SOI) transistors, strained silicon substrates, and aggressive optical patterning techniques to achieve transistor dimensions comparable to those of conventional 8nm-class FinFET nodes.
FDX Fusion also retains support for adaptive body biasing and combines high-density digital logic with RF, analog, and embedded memory capabilities. The technology will be manufactured using deep ultraviolet (DUV) lithography, although its tighter gate and metal pitches will likely require extensive multipatterning for the most critical layers.
Anton Shilov: What are the key innovations of FDX Fusion compared to predecessors and other nodes that are currently used?
Sudipto Bose: The key things that we're improving from the current-generation FDX, the first one is fT/fmax, this is 500 GHz, or 0.5 THz fT/fmax. This helps the first segment that I talked about, which is connectivity. This helps automotive radar, and this definitely helps the data center segment as they try to improve data speeds. So this is not really competing with silicon germanium. Think of it as silicon germanium at the very high end, and FDX in the 100 – 200 Gb per lambda space. That is one. The second one is the inference at the edge. So we can call it physical AI, and we look at physical AI as sense, think, act, and connect. But the kind of feature set we are putting in starts with improving the digital gate density. This is 2.2 times more dense than the current generation FDX (22FDX and 22FDX+).
Sudipto Bose: We are putting in a lot of flavors of eNVM, a lot of flavors of SRAM, because in edge AI, and in fact for any AI, the real game is how much memory you can pack without increasing the cost. So we are adding a lot of flavors of those. The way we architected FDX Fusion is that we set a goal of how much performance we can deliver to our customer base without increasing their operating expenses.
If you look at classic 7nm, 6nm, they require EUV tools. The mask set is costly; the tool chain is costly. The FDX Fusion platform that we've built isn't going to use EUV tools. It is also based on planar technology. So there are two advantages to this. One is that we can support a much more competitive mask set price compared to 6nm. We can also offer [customers] a lot more flexibility in terms of cycle time and in how many places we manufacture, because FDX Fusion does not need EUV tools.
So, to summarize the answer? We are trying to improve RF and analog performance, digital gate density, and the classes of memory that we offer. What applications are we trying to target? It is connectivity, edge AI, high-frequency mmWave, automotive radar, and definitely applications inside data centers.
Anton Shilov: You just mentioned transistor density. The press release says that it has more than twice the transistor density of the original 22FDX process, but you never actually published a transistor density number for this. Could you share any numbers?
Sudipto Bose: I will share a number with you. But I will also give you a little bit of context on how our customers look at our technology portfolio. If you look at the current generation, the one that we are manufacturing today, the one where I said we have 90+ customers ramping. Not designing, ramping. This basically comes from the flexibility of FD-SOI. You have the box, the depleted channel, and you can use body bias. So we have customers who are able to achieve 16nm-class power and performance with 22FDX. What do I mean by 16nm-class power and performance?
It essentially means that although our gate density is not as close to 16mm, when you look at the system-level requirements like CPU clock, but think of it as TOPS per watt, we are matching 16nm; in some cases, we are matching 12nm-class.
[...] If I were sitting in a time machine and went back seven years, instead of calling it 22FDX, we could have called it 16FDX, right? Or we could have called it 16nm-class FDX. So with that analogy, if you come to FDX Fusion, our gate density of FDX Fusion will be exactly the same as the industry's 8nm standard today.
It is 14 million gates per square millimeter; that is the density.
Note: Assuming he uses a two-input NAND gate as its reference, each gate contains four transistors, which translates to 56 MTr/mm², which is comparable to what Samsung's 8nm-class nodes use.
Sudipto Bose: Now, why do we call it 7nm-class? Or we actually thought of calling it 6nm-class, but then we said, let's wait for some silicon before we go too far ahead. It is because digital gate density is one aspect of a solution. It defines your physical size, the die size, and that is great. But then you need power and performance. And with the adaptive body bias (ABB) capability that FD-SOI has, and with the standard cell metallization innovations that we have brought in FDX Fusion, once a device is done and you again look at TOPS per watt, it should be equivalent, if not better, than what 7nm can achieve. That is our product target. So for a customer, the value it brings [customers] is: 'I do not have to use an EUV tool line. I do not have to use a mask set that costs many millions of dollars.'
I have something which costs a few millions of dollars, has great cycle time, but at the same time, I am getting TOPS per watt, which is similar to or better than 7nm. The die size might be bigger, but again, it should not be no bigger than what 8nm is today. And that is the messaging to the industry; that is why we say 7nm-class instead of saying, 'Hey, this is 7nm density.' We are trying to reflect what our customers have told us. That in the edge world, in the physical AI world, the size of the die is one aspect, but if the die does not meet the power requirement of the application, then the whole system will not function. So that is the push.
Anton Shilov: That is perhaps why you actually called the technology FDX Fusion instead of using some fancy number.
Sudipto Bose: Yes, because the moment we put a number [out], people put us in a box. And we want to give customers the optionality of, 'Listen, you want to design a transimpedance amplifier using the fT/fmax that we have? That's an analog circuit. No one cares about digital gate density. Go build it.'
'You want to build a simple MCU, which is just SRAM and IOs? We have 14 million gates per square millimeter. Go build it.' 'You want to build a mixed-signal design like a single-chip radar, Wi-Fi, a satcom digital beamformer? Here are the few other knobs that you can turn to get the best PPA or the best system performance that you need.'
That is why we chose the name Fusion; we are trying to imply [that] we give you many options. It is the combination of all the good things the world needs to design the physical AI dream that we have put in front of ourselves, like the whole stack concept of 'sense, think, act, and connect'. Let us see if it sticks. I think it is a bold move for us to go against the grain of the way the foundry industry typically names its platforms, but we feel pretty strong about it.

Note: For those of our readers who are not familiar with ABB, this is a technique that dynamically adjusts a transistor's threshold voltage by changing the voltage applied to the transistor body. Forward body bias reduces threshold voltage to improve performance, while reverse body bias increases it to reduce power leakage. This enables chips to optimize performance and power consumption according to workload requirements. ABB is one of the key advantages of the FD-SOI technology.
Anton Shilov: Since you mentioned this, does FDX Fusion retain the active body biasing capabilities of its predecessors?
