In the summer of 2024, I was in Jerusalem. While talking to a senior Israeli diplomat at the Ministry of Foreign Affairs in the western part of the city, which has been Israel’s seat of power since the 1948-49 war, I asked him about Israel’s relationship with India. India and Israel had built a very close strategic partnership over the past couple of decades. At the same time, India continued to maintain its institutional support for the Palestine cause. I referred to India’s votes at the UN supporting Palestinian national self-determination. India also backed resolutions that condemned Jewish settlements in the occupied West Bank and the Israeli occupation of Syria’s Golan Heights. The diplomat said, “India is doing what it has to do, but those positions will not have any impact on the Israeli-India ties.” He also said the partnership had evolved and progressed over the years and under different governments. “There is a consensus in Delhi and there is a consensus in Jerusalem on how we should move forward.”
When I saw Opposition Leader Rahul Gandhi’s remarks on India and Israel at an event at the University of Chicago on November 18, the ambassador’s words crossed my mind. “I support a strong relationship with Israel. We have strategic interests, including buying the best weapons for our armed forces. But that cannot mean silence on Gaza,” said Gandhi. “I condemn the October 7th attacks and the killing of innocent people. I also condemn what Israel has done in Gaza. One wrong cannot justify another,” he added. His comments kicked off intense debates in India. Several of his allies and left-liberal supporters criticised him for advocating strong relationship with a country that, according to investigations by UN-appointed panels, committed genocide against Palestinians in Gaza.

Others said there is nothing wrong in what Mr. Gandhi said given the close relationship between the two countries. Israel faces increasing international isolation today over its militarism and violence. In the U.S., Israel’s biggest supporter, the political tide is turning against the Jewish nation, as was evident during the midterm elections. But in India, as Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu himself pointed out recently, Israel has a lot of supporters. And Mr. Gandhi’s comment reflects the consensus in the Indian political elite across the ideological divide that the relationship with Israel is very significant. Daniel Carmon, a former Israeli ambassador in India, wrote in social media after Mr. Gandhi’s comment that he had several “in-depth meetings” with Mr. Gandhi in the past at the latter’s residence. “The more meetings we had, the clearer his understanding became of the Israeli position and the importance of bilateral relations,” he wrote about Mr. Gandhi.
