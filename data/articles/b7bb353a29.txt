The UK has one of the most creative and innovative video games industries in the world. From independent studios developing original ideas to established businesses creating globally recognised franchises, British developers have consistently shown that they can compete on the international stage.
The Government rightly recognises the creative industries as a priority growth sector, and over the past two decades we’ve seen a significant improvement in how policymakers engage with our industry. There is now a much greater understanding of the economic contribution games make, the highly skilled jobs they support and their potential as a major British export.
But as the industry evolves, we need to ensure the policies designed to support it evolve too.
We have an opportunity to have a much bigger conversation about how the UK supports the development and ownership of British intellectual property.
Entertainment is changing
Traditionally, video games, film, television and publishing have been treated as distinct industries, each with their own business models, funding structures and tax incentives. Now the boundaries between them are becoming blurred.
A successful video game can become a hit television series. A comic book character can inspire a major film franchise. A compelling fictional universe can find audiences across multiple formats, platforms and generations. At Rebellion, this is something we understand first-hand.
Alongside developing and publishing video games, we own 2000 AD, the home of iconic characters including Judge Dredd and Rogue Trooper. We also operate across comics, books, film and television, bringing stories and characters to life in different ways. Our recent Rogue Trooper film is an example of how intellectual property can move between creative formats, reaching new audiences while building on an established British franchise.
For businesses like ours, these aren't separate industries. They're different expressions of the same underlying intellectual property, supported by teams of talented people working across disciplines.
Yet the UK's system of creative tax reliefs remains largely organised around individual formats. We have incentives for video games, film, television and other forms of production, but comparatively little recognition of the intellectual property that connects them.
Backing British IP
Creating a successful game, character or fictional universe can take years of investment, experimentation and risk. But when an idea succeeds, its value can extend well beyond the format in which it was originally created. For British studios, the opportunity is to build the next globally recognised entertainment franchise and, critically, to retain ownership of that intellectual property here in the UK.
We only need to look across the Atlantic to see the potential. American entertainment giants like Disney and Marvel have built global businesses around characters and stories that span films, television, games and publishing. There is no reason why Britain cannot build more businesses with similar ambitions, developing homegrown intellectual property into franchises that reach audiences around the world.
When British businesses own their IP, they have greater control over how it is developed, licensed and commercialised. They can reinvest the returns into new projects, create skilled jobs and build sustainable businesses that continue to generate value over decades. This is particularly important for independent developers, who often face difficult decisions about how to fund ambitious projects while retaining control of the ideas they create.
There is no simple solution, and any reform would need to recognise the different economics of games, film, television and publishing. But I believe there is a strong case for the Government to explore how existing creative tax incentives could better support businesses developing intellectual property across multiple formats.
That means asking whether the framework we have today properly reflects how modern creative businesses operate, and whether it does enough to encourage long-term investment in British-owned IP.
The future of the industry
The UK already has many of the ingredients needed to build the next generation of global entertainment franchises, like exceptional creative talent, world-class studios, a strong independent development community and a rich history of storytelling.
The challenge now is ensuring that the businesses creating those franchises have the support and confidence to scale internationally while remaining rooted in Britain.
Government engagement with the games industry has come a long way, and that progress should be recognised. Now we have an opportunity to build on it. If we want Britain to remain a global leader in creative innovation, we need to think beyond individual games, films or television productions, and recognise the long-term value of the intellectual property that connects them.
