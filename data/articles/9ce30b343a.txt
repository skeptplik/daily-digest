Everything you need to know about the World Chess Championship
India’s Gukesh Dommaraju is defending the world chess championship against fast-rising Uzbek grandmaster Javokhir Sindarov in the youngest title match in the competition’s 140-year history. The best-of-14-games contest is scheduled to take place from 24 November to 12 December at the Fondation Martin Bodmer outside Geneva, with an overall prize fund of $2.5m (£1.9m).
- Game 1 starts at 3pm in Geneva, 2pm in London, 9am in New York and 7.30pm in India
- Sindarov will have the white pieces for Game 1 and make the first move
- Gukesh and Sindarov are both 20 years old, making this the youngest matchup in world championship’s 140-year history
For two elite players born in the same general part of the world barely six months apart, their competitive history is remarkably sparse: just three encounters in classical chess. Sindarov holds a 2½-½ advantage with two wins and one draw in their lifetime head-to-head, which dates back to their first encounter as tweens at the Moscow Open in 2018. Their most recent meeting happened in January at the prestigious Tata Steel tournament in Wijk aan Zee, where a tense positional struggle gave way to a wild, time-scrambled endgame before the pair settled for a draw after 78 moves. Sindarov also holds a 2-1 edge in their three rapid and blitz contests this year.
Gukesh’s form has largely gone off the rails since reaching the summit 23 months ago in Singapore. After making it to No 3 in the world with a peak rating of 2794, the Indian suffered a prolonged run of disappointing results, including a joint-last finish at March’s Prague Chess Festival and last-place showings at Norway Chess and the Chennai Grand Masters. He subsequently withdrew from the classical portion of the Grand Chess Tour and had tumbled to No 32 in the rankings by September, when his live rating briefly dipped below the 2700 barrier for the first time in four years, although his performances improved at that month’s Chess Olympiad.
Many of the world’s top grandmasters have expressed deep pessimism about Gukesh’s chances in Geneva due to his lengthy slump and Sindarov’s sustained upward tick. So too have the bookmakers, who have installed the Uzbek as a clear frontrunner at roughly 1-3 odds despite Gukesh being the champion. Carlsen has described Sindarov as “a pretty significant favorite”, citing the Indian’s tendency to take excessive risks. “His evaluations are way too optimistic,” the world No 1 said recently. “That makes him take way more chances than he should. I think especially now his confidence hasn’t been too high, he just tries to do a little bit too much.”
Yet one factor in Gukesh’s favor is his firsthand experience in the cauldron of a world championship match, where the intense psychological and physical rigors of facing the same opponent for weeks on end can be as punishing as the chess itself. Caruana, the world No 3 and former world title challenger who pushed Carlsen to the brink in 2018, has cautioned against writing off the champion despite his struggles. “The kind of ability he had – or has, whatever you want to say – it doesn’t disappear overnight,” Caruana said in September. “So, in my opinion, it can reverse at any moment.”
Here’s a review of the format for the world championship match. Gukesh and Sindarov will play up to 14 classical games with each player awarded one point for a win and a half-point for a draw. Whoever reaches seven and a half points first will be declared the champion (and no dead rubbers will be played).
The time control for each game is 120 minutes per side for the first 40 moves, then 30 minutes for the remainder of the game, with a 30-second increment per move starting with move 41. That means games can stretch beyond seven hours, testing both players’ concentration and endurance. Players are not permitted to agree to a draw before black’s 40th move, a rule designed to discourage short, uneventful games. A draw claim before then is only allowed in the case of threefold repetition or stalemate, subject to approval by the chief arbiter or deputy arbiter.
If the score is level after 14 games, a series of tiebreaks will be played on 12 December, starting with four rapid games at 15 minutes per player plus 10 seconds per move. If still tied, the players will contest two games at 10 minutes plus five seconds, followed if necessary by two blitz games at three minutes plus two seconds. Further single blitz games will be played until a winner emerges. Three of the past five world championship matches have been deadlocked after the classical portion and required tiebreaks: 2016, 2018 and 2023.

Hello and welcome to Geneva for day one of the World Chess Championship. Over the next three weeks, India’s defending champion Gukesh Dommaraju and Javokhir Sindarov of Uzbekistan will compete in a scheduled best-of-14-games match for the sport’s most prestigious title and a share of a $2.5m prize fund. The setting is the Fondation Martin Bodmer in Cologny, an affluent suburb overlooking Lake Geneva, where the two 20-year-olds will make history as the youngest pairing ever to contest a world championship match in the competition’s 140-year history.
Gukesh became the youngest undisputed world chess champion in history when he defeated China’s Ding Liren in Singapore in December 2024, aged just 18, shattering the record held by Garry Kasparov, who was 22 when he dethroned Anatoly Karpov in 1985. An aggressive player known for creating sharp, tactically complex positions aimed at unsettling opponents, the Chennai-born grandmaster has struggled badly for form since winning the crown, sliding from a career-high No 3 to No 26 in the world rankings and raising questions over his chances of retaining the title in his first defense.
Sindarov, meanwhile, has emerged as one of the most formidable talents of a new generation of Uzbek grandmasters. Born in Tashkent, he became a grandmaster at 12 years and 10 months, the second-youngest in history at the time, and secured his place in Geneva by winning the eight-man Candidates tournament earlier this year. A fearless, resourceful player in a rich vein of form, he enters his first world championship match ranked fourth in the world, up from No 25 last year and a remarkable 22 places above the reigning champion, and with a favorable head-to-head record against Gukesh in classical chess.
We’re roughly 80 minutes away from the ceremonial opening moves. Fide president Timur Turlov will do the honors for white, with five-time world champion and Fide deputy president Viswanathan Anand moving for black. Plenty more to come between now and then.
