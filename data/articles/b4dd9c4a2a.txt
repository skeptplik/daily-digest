Feeling tired and need a pick-me-up but finding your morning cup of coffee isn’t touching the sides? Instead of the predictable choice (a second coffee), you could instead opt for a seemingly endless array of other drinks and foods that now come with added caffeine.
You might energise yourself before your morning gym session with a few caffeinated scoops of a pre-workout supplement. Afterwards, you could refuel with a caffeinated protein shake or a protein bar with added guarana – whose seeds contain caffeine.
If you’re hankering for a sweet treat, there are caffeinated gummies and chocolates (the latter of which already naturally contains caffeine). If it’s “clean refreshment with functional energy” you’re after, as the creator of one Australian brand describes it, why not have some caffeinated sparkling water instead?
Don’t actually want to drink or eat anything? There are also caffeine tablets, caffeine pouches – which have raised concerns given their close resemblance to nicotine pouches – caffeine strips, and caffeine chewing gum. You can spray the stimulant directly on to your tongue: one product, marketed as a sugar-free energy spray, will promises to deliver 64mg in two squirts – more than the amount typically found in a cup of black tea.
Tired of the barrage of ingestible caffeinated products? May I suggest rubbing some into your face in serum form, to “energise skin for a more well-rested appearance”?
You get the picture: caffeine, the world’s most consumed psychoactive drug, has now become ubiquitous as an additive. Its effects are well known – but with all the buzz, are we at risk of too much of a caffeine high?
Caffeine v coffee: what are the health effects?
The effects of caffeine ingestion are well studied. It is “one of the safest stimulants we have”, says Dr Ian Musgrave, a molecular pharmacologist at the University of Adelaide.
Research suggests that coffee consumption is associated with reduced risk of developing type 2 diabetes, Parkinson’s disease and Alzheimer’s.
But there is an important distinction here: “Caffeine is the stimulating chemical in coffee, but a lot of coffee’s benefits aren’t because of caffeine,” says Clare Collins, professor of nutrition and dietetics at the University of Newcastle.
“Coffee beans are a plant, so apart from caffeine, they contain a range of what are called phytonutrients” – plant-based chemicals which have benefits including antioxidant and anti-inflammatory properties, she says. Even decaffeinated coffee is associated with a lower risk of type 2 diabetes, studies have found, with the antioxidants chlorogenic acid and caffeic acid thought to have antidiabetic properties.
Caffeine reduces drowsiness by temporarily preventing a compound called adenosine from binding to receptors in the brain. It also boosts the release of adrenaline, increasing heart rate and constricting blood vessels, which raises blood pressure.
“It improves alertness and physical performance, not as much as people imagine that it does – but there is an effect,” Musgrave says. “It’s good enough that athletes like to use it – it’s not good enough to ban it [as a performance-enhancing drug].”
Caffeine was removed from the World Anti-Doping Agency’s banned drug list at the beginning of 2004. That “opened the floodgates” for it to be added to supplements targeting sports performance, Collins says.
“Caffeine is the number one drug most used by amateur athletes,” Musgrave says. He views its increasing use as an additive as down to a wellness trend towards “improved physical performance, not necessarily just for sport but also for body sculpting”.
Consumer trends analysts have also characterised caffeine’s ubiquity as being part of a broad push towards “functional” foods and drinks, which also encompasses trends such as proteinmaxxing.
“Caffeine is cheap, it’s legal, it’s really hard to poison yourself with it – but people don’t understand that because caffeine’s in so much, you can potentially get high levels which can result in caffeine intoxication,” Musgrave says.
How much is too much caffeine?
Guidelines suggest that adults consume no more than 400mg of caffeine a day, and a maximum of 200mg in a single serving.
“Caffeine intoxication is typically seen at concentrations of more than 300mg intake of caffeine in one go,” Musgrave says. “An average espresso has something in the order of 75 to 85mg of caffeine, but … if you have a workout supplement, an energy drink, and top it off with a extra espresso at the end of your workout, you can easily go into caffeine intoxication.”
Because caffeine content “varies even from one cup to the next at the same barista”, it’s difficult to be sure of exact consumption, Collins says. “The only way you can manage that risk is by not going overboard in consuming supplements that contain caffeine.”
Symptoms of caffeine toxicity include an elevated heart rate or arrhythmia, nausea, vomiting, muscle twitching and seizures. Caffeine overdose has resulted in deaths, although they occur infrequently.
A review of more than 28,000 adverse-event reports to the US Food and Drug Administration found that caffeinated energy drinks and stimulant supplements were associated with higher reporting of cardiac arrest and heart attacks, particularly for 18- to 34-year-olds, an age group typically not at high risk.
Sensitivity to caffeine’s effects varies between individuals, as well as the rate at which the stimulant is cleared by the body. “There are some people at higher risk of caffeine intoxication,” Musgrave says. “People who have a version of the enzyme that breaks down caffeine more slowly will have higher caffeine [in their system] for longer.”
“It can take anywhere from four to eight hours to metabolise caffeine,” Collins says. The smaller your liver, the longer it takes, she says. For that reason, the recommended daily maximum is much lower for those under 18, as well as pregnant and breastfeeding women.
Musgrave believes the warnings on caffeine-containing foods and beverages should be easier to find and more clearly articulate who is at risk. “Most of the labelling is woefully inadequate.”
“If you’re going to go hard on caffeinated beverages,” Collins says, it’s important to “know where it’s coming from”.
