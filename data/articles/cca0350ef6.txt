Hong Kong – Japan’s yōkai have come a long way from the shadows of local folklore. These uncanny, impish creatures haunt the global imagination, evident in their presence on the walls of Hong Kong’s M+ contemporary art museum, part of the ”Myths and Monsters: The Art of Fantasy in Asia” exhibition.
On display until April 4, 2027, the show exhibits some 430 works, surveying two centuries of Asian storytelling through paintings, textiles, manga, anime and multimedia art. Japanese works form one of its strongest threads: From ukiyo-e prints and “Godzilla” posters to “Akira” sketches and Mariko Mori’s video art depicting a cyborg shaman, the exhibition demonstrates how Japan has been shaping Asia’s fantasy landscape.
It’s no coincidence that Japan’s yōkai art flourished during the period featured in the first chapter, “Spirits Among Us: Fantasy in the 1800s.” In the late 19th century, Japan faced demons both foreign and domestic, the latter in the form of a coup and civil war that would end more than two centuries of shogunate rule. Kyosai Kawanabe’s 1874 ukiyo-e “School for Spooks” lampoons the new Meiji government with a scene depicting Shoki, the Chinese “king of ghosts,” dressed in Western garb instructing a classroom of ghoulish yōkai students.
