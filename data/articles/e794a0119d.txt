Japan faces diplomatic dilemma amid joint pressure from nuclear neighbors
As the Ukraine war grinds on, ties between Russia and neighboring Japan are at their worst in decades. Tokyo wants more support from Washington, but some Japanese are concerned they may not get it.
AILSA CHANG, HOST:
And now to Russia's war in Ukraine. This war has raised tensions not only in Europe but also in Asia. Ties between Russia and neighboring Japan are at their worst in decades. Tokyo would like more support from Washington, but as NPR's Anthony Kuhn reports, some Japanese people are concerned they may not get it.
ANTHONY KUHN, BYLINE: In a video released by Russia's federal security bureau, FSB agents charge into a hotel room on Sakhalin Island in Russia's Far East on October 13.
(SOUNDBITE OF ARCHIVED RECORDING)
UNIDENTIFIED FSB AGENT: (Non-English language spoken).
YUKI IMAI: (Non-English language spoken).
KUHN: The agents pin Japanese journalist Yuki Imai to the ground and handcuff him. They question him and take him to Moscow to face trial on charges of espionage. Japan's government denies the charges. The FSB says Imai was reporting on soldiers from Russia's Far East who were deployed to Ukraine. Shinji Hyodo, a Russia expert at the National Institute for Defense Studies in Tokyo, argues that the journalist was just doing his job.
SHINJI HYODO: (Through interpreter) I believe the Russian side intends to use this as a political bargaining chip to exert pressure on Japan.
KUHN: Hyodo says that underlying the current tensions are Japan's sanctions on Russia for its invasion of Ukraine. Russia now sees Japan as hostile, and bilateral relations have gone into a tailspin.
HYODO: (Through interpreter) I think it's fair to say that Japan is currently facing its greatest diplomatic challenge since the end of the war. This is no longer just a bilateral issue between Japan and Russia.
KUHN: There's also what Hyodo calls the China factor. China has been putting pressure on Japan since last year, when Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi said that a Chinese blockade of Taiwan could justify a Japanese military response. Russia relies on China's support in its war on Ukraine, and it's joined China in pressuring Japan. Another factor is a long-running territorial dispute between Russia and Japan.
(SOUNDBITE OF FACTORY MACHINERY)
KUHN: In August, Russian President Vladimir Putin visited a seafood factory, hospital and school on his first visit to one of four disputed islands called the Southern Kurils by Russia and the Northern Territories by Japan. The Soviet Union seized the islands at the end of World War II. They never signed a permanent peace treaty because of the dispute and because the U.S. warned them not to. This episode was called the Dulles intervention. James Brown, an expert on Japan-Russia relations at Temple University in Tokyo, explains.
JAMES BROWN: This was the suggestion from John Foster Dulles, the secretary of state, in 1956 that if Japan were to make an agreement with the Soviet Union, settling the territorial dispute, well, then perhaps the United States would remain forevermore in holding, administering Okinawa.
KUHN: So the Soviet Union kept the islands, and the U.S. returned to Japan the island of Okinawa, which remains a key U.S. military hub in Asia. Brown says Japan's media generally don't mention the U.S.'s role in the territorial dispute so as not to offend its only ally as it faces three nuclear powers - Russia, China and North Korea.
BROWN: Japan's situation is essentially they have no Plan B, which means that they have to rely on the United States. They have to flatter the Trump administration. They have to do everything possible to avoid abandonment.
KUHN: The U.S. has criticized Russia's arrest of the Japanese journalist, but Shinji Hyodo says that the U.S. generally doesn't seem very interested in Japan's conflict with Russia. And he says Japan is concerned about the Asia-Pacific Economic Cooperation summit in Shenzhen, China, this month.
HYODO: (Through interpreter) There is a possibility that a trilateral summit involving the leaders of the U.S., China and Russia could take place, potentially leading to the U.S. being swayed toward the positions held by China and Russia.
KUHN: It's unclear whether Japan's Prime Minister Sanae Takaichi will meet with either Russian or Chinese leaders at the summit. Anthony Kuhn, NPR News, Seoul.
