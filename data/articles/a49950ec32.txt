The UK’s competition watchdog has called for a shake-up of the £8bn private dentistry market, after an investigation identified a “catalogue of concerns” including inflation-busting annual rises and evidence of targets to sell more expensive treatments and dental payment plans.
The Competition and Markets Authority (CMA) said patients faced substantial differences in prices in different parts of the UK and “commercial practices, targets and incentives [that] may not operate in patients’ best interests”.
The report found dentists typically had a financial stake in treatment revenues, either as practice owners or through revenue-sharing arrangements.
“We have also seen evidence of targets to convert NHS patients to private care, sell more expensive treatments, and increase sales of dental payment plans,” the CMA said. “Together, these incentives and practices create a risk that patients are sold treatments that are not in their best interests.”
The interim report found prices rose by an estimated 7% a year on average between 2023 and 2025 at large dental groups, well above inflation and wage growth, with costs across the industry forcing some people to delay or forgo treatment.
The CMA gave the example of a composite white filling that cost about 36% of mean weekly disposable income in the north-east of England and 35% in Wales. It also identified significant price differences, even in the same local area.
In Preston, prices advertised online for a composite filling ranged from £80 to £223, while in Caerphilly the price of an amalgam filling ranged from £72 to £159.
“Some variation is to be expected, but the CMA is concerned that people are paying more than they should because they are unable to shop around,” the report said.
The CMA said about 60% of patients across the UK now paid for at least some private dental care, including many on low incomes, but that it was difficult for them to “confidently shop around and get a good deal”.
The report found one in six private dental practices in the UK did not publish price lists online and that there was “substantial variation in indicative profit margins between dental practices”.
Another concern identified was data suggesting that practices primarily serving NHS patients did not necessarily earn lower profit margins.
The CMA also said there was a shortage of dentists, with the estimated job vacancy rate in England at about 8%, significantly higher than the 2% rate across the UK economy.
The UK also has fewer dentists per head than most other European countries.
The CMA said the government should play a more direct “market-shaping role”, using its position as a major purchaser and commissioner of dentistry to “influence incentives and outcomes” for private and NHS patients.
“Millions of people across the UK rely on private dentistry as an essential service, but the market isn’t working well for them,” said Sarah Cardell, the chief executive of the CMA. “People can’t easily compare and switch between providers, dentists are in short supply, commercial incentives to grow revenues may not operate in the patients’ best interests and there are weaknesses in the regulatory framework. As a result, many people are struggling with affordability, and those on lower incomes and in certain parts of the UK are hardest hit.”
The CMA said it was working with governments across the UK to finalise a wide-ranging package of measures to change how the market operated, with its final report expected in March.
Before the last general election Labour promised to invest heavily in NHS dentistry, as well as to provide more incentives for dentists to remain in the national public healthcare system.
On Thursday the British Dental Association (BDA) said it agreed with the CMA’s interim report and said it provided an opportunity for Labour to “keep promises made at the last election”.
The BDA had accused the government of using the study as cover for its failure to commit to any new investment in rebuilding NHS dental services.
Research it conducted with YouGov found that 39% of adults in England had found it hard to pay for dental care in the last two years. Among those, 43% delayed getting a dental procedure, 32% decided against getting any care, 15% took on debt and 13% reduced spending on essentials such as food, accommodation and heating.
“It is essential we have a thriving private dental market, but when it comes to giving patients access to affordable care, ministers hold almost all the cards,” said Eddie Crouch, the chair of the BDA. “The UK government can start by keeping their election promise, and fix and fund NHS dentistry.”
