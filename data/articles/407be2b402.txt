AfD chocolate Saint Nicholas in party's fan shop upsets religious leaders
A festive piece of merchandise on the right-wing populist Alternative for Germany (AfD) "fan shop" website has upset some religious leaders in Germany.
Called a "chocolate Nicholas," it appears to depict a semblance of Saint Nicholas in bishop's regalia and the party's colors and holding a book with "AfD" written on the cover where a Bible might be in more traditional iconography.
The Bishop of Essen, Franz-Josef Overbeck, was highly critical of the promotion in Monday's edition of the Bild newspaper.
"The AfD is appropriating Christian symbols for party campaigning," Overbeck said. "Showing the holy Nicholas in a blue coat with mitre, staff and the gospel with an AfD logo is not in order."
Overbeck acknowledged that other organizations like football clubs would sell chocolate Christmas figurines in club colors, but he said they would tend to refrain from including overtly Christian symbols when doing so.
Bild quoted an AfD spokesman as saying that as a "liberal-conservative party that clearly associates itself with Christian values," the item fit "particularly well" with the party image.
The main German protestant church group EKD's cultural representative, Johann Hinrich Claussen, said the offer showed the AfD had "no understanding of traditions and no respect" for Christianity.
He described the action as "embarrassing and revealing," and said that Christianity was "supra-partisan."
"The AfD knows little about what is holy to Christians. Otherwise, it would be aware that Bishop Nicholas of Myra, in what today is Demre in Turkey, stood clearly for uniting the occident and the orient. Beyond that, he is the patron saint of rescues at sea," Claussen said.
All welcome at Bavarian politician Aiwanger's funeral, partner says
The partner of deceased Bavarian politician Hubert Aiwanger has said that anybody is welcome at his funeral this coming Saturday.
Aiwanger was the economy minister in Bavaria's state government as the leader of the junior coalition partners to the center-right CSU.
"Hubert was a man of the people and so it stands to reason that anyone who liked or treaasured him is allowed to come to his funeral," Tanja Schweiger wrote on her Instagram profile overnight.
She was posting after a requiem in the state capital Munich paying tribute to the leader of the Free Voters (Freie Wähler) party over the weekend. Around 1,100 people attended this ceremony.
Aiwanger died suddenly and unexpectedly on November 4 during a working visit to Berlin, aged 55.
Prosecutors have since said he died of natural causes, after he was found dead in his hotel room.
Germany's Nations League fate at stake against Netherlands
Germany's national men's football team will play neighbors and rivals the Netherlands for the 50th time on Monday night.
And there's quite a lot at stake for Jürgen Klopp's team in the last group stage game of the Nations League competition at Berlin's Olympiastadion.
If Germany win, they will qualify for the knockout stages, but a defeat could lead to potential relegation into the second tier of Nations League football.
Klopp was upbeat in the pre-match press conference at the prospect of playing the Netherlands, who he said play "Xavi football," in the image of the skillful Spanish playmaker and World Cup winner coaching the team.
"I've always loved playing against teams like this. For all my coaching career," Klopp said. "When someone says, 'hey they can really play football,' then in my mind the party's already started."
A full house with some 71,000 fans is expected in the capital when the game kicks off. Klopp played on the Dutch reputation for liberal use of bicylces in his comments to reporters, saying he wanted to "put a stick in their spokes" and dislodge them from the saddle.
Work starting to link A44 'bridge to nowhere' to road network
The notion of a bridge over the valley between Ratingen and Heiligenhaus to the east of Düsseldorf in western Germany as part of the A44 highway is more than half a century old. And the physical bridge itself has stood, complete, ever since 2022.
Yet still the crossing is not connected to any roads at either end.
A construction project to build the last 4.4 kilometers of highway and link the bridge to the A44 highway will formally start on Monday.
State premier Hendrik Wüst, as well as the regional transport and traffic ministers will attend the ground-breaking ceremony.
A project first conceived some 55 years ago has faced repeated delays because of lawsuits, environmental protection studies and onerous planning and permit procedures.
The bridge across the Angerbachtal (Angerbach Valley) should improve the road connection between the North Rhine-Westphalia state capital of Düsseldorf, the Bergisches Land mountain range and the industrial cities in the Ruhr Valley. It's also meant to help remove some strain from the perenially busy A3 highway and to improve the road connections to Düsseldorf International Airport.
The last stretch of highway is expected to cost around €200 million (roughly $220 million), or roughly the annual costs incurred each year by forcing traffic to divert around it, according to estimates from Düsseldorf's IHK industry and trade association.
Construction is expected to take several years, no formal end date has yet been set.
Merz heads to Munich for CSU meeting
Chancellor Friedrich Merz heads to Munich early on Monday, for a meeting with the leadership of the Bavarian Christian Social Democrats (CSU), the sister party to his conservative Christian Democrats(CDU).
No concrete details about the talks were made public ahead of time, except for the CSU saying it would serve as a general exchange that had been planned for some time.
The trip comes a week after Merz nominated Ilse Aigner as the next German president, likely paving the way for her to become the first woman and first CSU politician to assume the role.
It also follows soon after the 2027 federal budget cleared a marathon meeting of the parliamentary budget committee in Berlin. This serves as one of the more conspicuous examples of the frictions and difficulties in the grand coalition between the Christian Democrats and the center-left Social Democrats — with the compromises it contains a point of frustration among the CSU and for state premier Markus Söder.
Manuela Schwesig set for vote on new term as regional leader
The northeastern state parliament in Mecklenburg-Western Pomerania will likely vote in Social Democrat (SPD) Manuela Schwesig for another term as state premier early in the afternoon.
The 52-year-old, who first stepped into the role in 2017, plans to lead a new coalition of the SPD, the environmentalist Greens and the socialist Left Party after state elections in September.
The right-wing populist Alternative for Germany (AfD) emerged as the largest single party in the sparsely populated state.
But still, the political calculus that emerged was arguably simpler than in Berlin — where the socialist Left Party is the largest party but could struggle to lead a coaltion — and in Saxony-Anhalt, where the AfD was by far the largest party but short of an outright majority.
Schwesig's SPD claimed 29 seats, with the Greens and the Left each taking 5, meaning that when combined, they hold a fairly clear majority over the AfD with its 32 seats.
The SPD's relatively successful performance in the state, at least compared to its national struggles, has also led to Schwesig being linked with a possible return to federal politics. The SPD plans to elect a new leadership duo early next year.
Schwesig has already had a spell in Berlin as the federal minsiter for families, pensioners and women, and briefly led the SPD on an interim basis in 2017, but after this she returned to her home state to seek the top regional job instead.
