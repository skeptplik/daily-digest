One Nation has vowed to cut Australia’s refugee intake by 90% and scrap the permanent parent visa program under its latest hardline plan to slash migration.
Pauline Hanson announced the policy on Tuesday, labelling it a “tough” approach to migration. It was quickly criticised by political opponents and advocacy groups who labelled the announcement a “predictable and cheap” attack on refugees.
The rightwing party said if it held power it would cut the refugee program from 20,000 places a year to 2,000 and claimed that Australia would still remain above the OECD average for places per capita.
“The cost [to] … the taxpayer is enormous,” Hanson told reporters. “The cost is, on average, about $500,000 a year in a lifetime of expenditure per person in the country.
“We’ve got to take a tough stance on this. My concern is for these people out there … that are doing it tough. You don’t bring more people into the country if they cannot fund their own bed or hospital beds.”
Hanson claimed that her plan would save the budget more than $140bn over 10 years. Migrants from countries on the Albanese government’s “do not travel” list, including Iran, Lebanon, Syria and Somalia, would still be banned from entering the country.
One Nation says analysis based on Treasury modelling, adjusted for inflation, found the lifetime cost of a humanitarian entrant was $523,000. Treasury modelling done in December 2021 found the cost was $400,000, with 90% of it borne by the federal government.
Labor has committed to retaining the refugee intake at 20,000 a year while the Coalition has promised to halve it to 10,000.
The foreign affairs minister, Penny Wong, accused One Nation of trying to divide the country. She said Australia had the right balance and questioned Hanson’s claim that Australia would remain above the OECD average under the minor party’s plan.
“I don’t accept those figures and I find when Ms Hanson speaks, you always have to check what is actually being said. There are countries which take more refugees, there are countries which take fewer. We think this [20,000 a year] is the right balance,” Wong said.
“We have unprecedented people movement around the world. We have displacement of more human beings than at any time since world war two. We make a very small contribution to that by our refugee intake.”
The OECD reports that Canada and Australia have the most generous refugee settlement programs per capita but it only uses data of refugees resettled through the UNHCR process.
The intake of other OECD countries including Germany or the US are made up largely of asylum seekers who cross into their borders and claim protection, meaning their intake levels appear much lower under the OECD figures Hanson has used.
Wong said it was “more of the same” from One Nation.
“I know that they think dividing Australians is the best response to the politics of the time,” the Labor frontbencher said. “I take a different view.”
The opposition leader, Angus Taylor, stood by his policy of halving the refugee intake to 10,000 but said “some of the greatest Australians came here through as refugees to this country”.
“Some of those who have built the most successful businesses – an extraordinary group of people. But we need to make sure the people who come here as refugees do share our values, and they’re the right people,” Taylor said.
The shadow treasurer, Tim Wilson, said he didn’t take One Nation’s policy announcements seriously because they changed frequently.
“I just take it as another statement. It’ll be replaced by another one in a few days’ time, and I don’t see any of the substantial policy work,” he told News24.
“Their answer just seems to be to announce a number, then to walk away from it the next week because they need another number.”
The Greens leader, David Shoebridge, accused One Nation and the two major parties of being “engaged in a race to the bottom to turn back refugees, lock them up in offshore detention, and blame them for the cost of living crisis”.
“Pauline Hanson’s attack on refugees is just the latest shameful chapter in a decades-long campaign against people seeking safe shelter,” Shoebridge said.
Meanwhile, the Asylum Seeker Resource Centre called it a “predictable and cheap attack” on refugees, and said Victorians would reject One Nation at the upcoming state election.
The ASRC’s deputy chief executive, Jana Favero, said the public wanted leaders to bring communities together, not divide them.
“People are sick of politicians finger-pointing and blaming our newest arrivals for problems,” Favero said. “Reducing the humanitarian intake will not make Victorians richer or safer. People want solutions, not scapegoats.”
Other nations including the US and Canada have tightened their migration programs, with Canada reducing its parent visa program.
Hanson said One Nation was not barring migrant parents from moving to Australia but they would have to be sponsored or they could visit their families on temporary visas.
“We’re not saying parents can’t come out … [but] they will actually be sponsored by the families not the taxpayers.”
