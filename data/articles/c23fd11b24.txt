Online marketplace Etsy, best known as a platform for selling handmade goods, recently updated its user agreement to allow itself to "develop, train, test and improve artificial intelligence and machine learning technologies" using content uploaded by sellers.
This has not gone over well, with some closing their Etsy shops in protest. In response, Etsy has assured users that it doesn't plan to train AI systems designed to copy their work or replace their creativity. Instead, the company said that the change is about improving the platform and providing "services and tools that can help you run your business."
I am sometimes sympathetic toward companies that get yelled at for normal terms of service agreements. If a company hosts user-submitted content on the web, for instance, it needs a license to distribute that content globally, and sometimes users misunderstand that. This is not one of those cases.
The new agreement—which Etsy has told The New York Times it's planning to revise—says that Etsy and its third-party partners have the right to use uploaded content "to develop, train, test and improve artificial intelligence and machine learning technologies, including to improve the Services and/or help keep them safe."
That reads to me like blanket permission to train AI on user content for any purpose. The tacked-on note about keeping users safe does nothing to limit what Etsy can do.
The artists who sell cool custom keycaps and other fun things on Etsy are already likely to struggle against knock-off sellers if they get popular enough. That came with the territory of online selling even before AI, but it is absurd to now insist that they willingly hand over all the things that make their stores unique—their art, their product descriptions—to systems capable of duplicating those things in hard-to-trace ways, with only a pinky swear to protect them.
We've all seen how the AI industry has tried to brush off the concerns of artists and others whose work it ingests. One company went so far as to create a chatbot bearing the name of a former PC Gamer editor without permission, pretending that this robo-editor could give writing advice like the real person. And that's without him even knowing they were doing it!

Even if Etsy truly does have good intentions, expecting artists to give this the thumbs up is ridiculous. It is not their responsibility to improve the platform by letting it mimic their creative work. The revised terms should, at the very least, exclude every seller from AI training as the default setting.
