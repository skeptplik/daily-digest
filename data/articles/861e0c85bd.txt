Shabana Mahmood has been accused of an “assault” on free speech over claims her officials tried to cancel an online talk by the climate academic Andreas Malm days after he was banned from entering the UK.
The event in Hackney, east London, was a launch party for the socialist magazine Tribune. It went ahead on 4 November in defiance of Home Office warnings.
While the Home Office has denied it tried to cancel the event, its officials warned Hackney council, which owns the venue, that the event could breach counter-terrorism laws because it was also due to be addressed by the Palestine Action cofounder Huda Ammori. The event took place on the day she was challenging the group’s proscription in the supreme court.
Hackney’s Green party mayor, Zoe Garbett, who intervened to ensure the event went ahead, has accused Mahmood of an “appalling assault on free speech” and written to her demanding an explanation.
Days before the event, Malm, a respected climate academic and author of How to Blow Up a Pipeline, was prevented from travelling to the UK. Officials told him that his presence was “not considered to be conducive to the public good”, but no further reasons were given.
Documents released to Tribune under freedom of information laws detail how Hackney council officials appeared to have been pressed by the Home Office to cancel the event, despite the department’s own assessment that it was “unlikely to meet the threshold for disruption”.
An email on 12 October from the Home Office’s extremism disruptions team said: “We received this referral from the counter-extremism group (CEG) about [Malm] due to visit the UK for an event in London on 4 November.
“[Malm] has made many statements in support of terrorists and direct action, particularly regarding Palestine and climate change.”
After Malm was prevented from entering the UK, in a decision that was widely criticised, Tribune said he would still speak at the event via a video link. A day before the event, a Home Office official emailed Hackney council’s Prevent and hate-crime coordinator to describe Malm and Ammori as “two speakers of concern”.
An official from the Home Office’s Prevent local delivery unit also told the council: “Ultimately, I can’t direct you to cancel the event. This is a decision for the council.”
But they went on to warn that the event could breach the Terrorism Act 2000 “whereby it is an offence if a person … assists in arranging … a meeting which he knows is to be addressed by a person who belongs … to a proscribed organisation”.
Mahmood’s officials also asked Hackney if managers of the venue had been told of their concerns about Malm and Ammori speaking at the event “even if it is to take place virtually”.
After those concerns were passed on, a manager at the venue called Tribune to say the event would have to be cancelled, according to its editor, Oliver Eagleton. This decision was then overruled by the mayor.
In her letter to the home secretary, Garbett said: “Encouraging the cancellation of lawful public events represents an assault on the right to free speech and under no circumstance will I be complicit in it.”
She added: “Despite initial Home Office assessments indicating that the event was ‘unlikely to meet the threshold for disruption’, pressure was applied to council staff to intervene with the operator and cancel the booking.”
The letter asked: “Why was pressure exerted on local council officers to consider cancelling or vetoing speakers, and how does the Home Office reconcile these actions with local authorities’ statutory obligations to uphold free speech and peaceful assembly.”
A Home Office spokesperson said it was “categorically untrue to suggest the Home Office pushed for this event to be cancelled”, pointing out it was a decision for the council, informed by Home Office advice.
Malm said: “The UK’s crackdown on free expression is terrifying. First they ban the books of Sally Rooney. Then they block people like me from entering the country because of our support for Palestine. Now it comes to light that they tried to cancel this magazine launch – just because they feared that the speakers might say something they disagree with. Where will this repression end?”
Ammori said: “By trying to prevent me from being able to speak publicly, the Home Office has further proven the case I was making in the supreme court that same day about the authorities misapplying the law to carry out unlawful arrests and unlawful acts of censorship such as this.
“Home Office officials wrongly advised the council that this entirely lawful political discussion would breach the Terrorism Act simply because I was due to participate. I have never been convicted of any crime, let alone a terrorism offence, but people convicted under the Terrorism Act are not gagged as a result and barred from speaking publicly. The suggestion that my participation in a political discussion could somehow make that discussion unlawful is ludicrous.”
