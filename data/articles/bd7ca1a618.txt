Every month, more than 12,000 applications are made to Social Security Scotland for disability support.
The number of Scots receiving these benefits has risen sharply since the Covid pandemic, and is projected to hit one million by the end of the decade.
This has been driven in part by younger people and claims for mental health conditions.
The cost of the system has been growing, posing additional challenges for Scottish ministers facing a tight budget settlement.
But with the UK government considering a radical redesign of support in England and Wales, they insist that Scotland's welfare state does not need to be trimmed.
Why has there been such an increase in claims in Scotland, and can the government afford to continue its more generous approach?
Scotland has long had a higher rate of ill health among adults than other parts of the UK, often attributed to its history of heavy industry and relatively high levels of poverty in certain areas.
But the change seen in recent years has different roots.
There has been a notable uptick in disability across the UK since the Covid pandemic - and it is most pronounced north of the border.
The percentage of Scotland's working-age population, aged 16 to 64, reporting having a disability increased from 19% in 2020-21 to 28% in 2023-24, external.
In children aged under 16, it doubled from 6% to 12%.
That has translated into a rising number of people receiving disability benefits - in total there are about 800,000 people in receipt of them today.
The Scottish Fiscal Commission says there has been a "shift in the age profile of applicants" since 2020.
Some 35% of the new applicants for Adult Disability Payment, external, since it was rolled out as Scotland's main disability benefit in 2022, have been people under 35. Over half are under 45.
Mental health is a key element in this, with 41% of the ADP caseload concerning "mental and behavioural disorders".
Almost half of those are "neurotic and stress related" conditions, such as anxiety and post-traumatic stress disorder.
If you look back before social security was devolved to Holyrood, mental and behavioural disorders accounted for only 26%, external of the claims for Personal Independence Payments, the main disability payment in the UK system.
Among children, these conditions now account for 64% of applications in Scotland - with the number of people under 16 receiving the Child Disability Payment for them more than doubling from 30,000 in 2020 to 75,000 this year.
Meanwhile, mental health conditions play a much smaller role in benefit claims from older people, accounting for 13% of the Pension Age Disability Payment caseload - behind both back and joint issues and diseases of the circulatory system.
Why is this happening?
There are inevitably some who characterise it as the product of an overly sensitive "snowflake generation".
But others note that the current cohort of young people spent their formative years in lockdown before emerging into a cost-of-living crisis and the destabilising rise of AI - with polling suggesting that the majority believe they will be worse off than their parents, external.
Mental health charity Scottish Action for Mental Health (SAMH) says young people today are "growing up significantly more mentally distressed".
Chief executive Billy Watson said they face a "relentless" pace of life, with social media constantly exposing them to unsettling news and world events and unrealistic social comparison.
Data published this week from the Scottish Health Survey, external suggested that mental wellbeing - measured using an internationally-recognised scoring system - had recovered to pre-pandemic levels.
However, the 16 to 24 age group had the lowest score of any included, and around a quarter of young adults reported poor enough mental health to suggest a possible psychiatric disorder.
An independent review for the UK government by Prof Peter Fonagy last month said the problems faced by young people were real, with dramatically increasing cases of mental distress including depression and anxiety.
But Fonagy also warned of an "increasing risk of overdiagnosis or misdiagnosis".
He said this was because "diagnosis has become the primary gateway to support that could and should be available earlier".
This was echoed by Alan Milburn, author of the UK government's review of support for young people in England and Wales.
He said that "in too many places, diagnosis has inappropriately become the key that unlocks help", which has "created pressure on people to seek a diagnosis".
As an aside, it should be noted that while Milburn's review centred on employment prospects, benefit uptake does not always read across directly to this; Adult Disability Payment is not an "out of work" benefit like Jobseeker's Allowance.
The employment rate among disabled people in Scotland is more than 50%, and on the flip side studies have suggested, external that almost half of those not in education, employment or training are not claiming benefits either.
Milburn's review, external proposed sweeping changes to the youth support network in England and Wales, including faster access to mental health services and a complete replacement of Personal Independence Payments.
But in Scotland, "Pip" has already been replaced by Adult Disability Payment.
There was a very deliberate wave of publicity around the launch of the new benefit in 2022 - alongside the versions for children and pensioners - in a bid to increase uptake among people who might have been entitled to the likes of Pip, but never claimed.

A report last year concluded that it was more "significantly more compassionate" and "kinder in tone and approach".
However, it is worth noting that more than half of initial applications for ADP have been rejected, with 43% approved compared with 54% denied - broadly in line with the system in England and Wales.
There has been a steady increase in rejections since ADP was rolled out in 2022 - in the first few months only a quarter of applications were rejected, but over the past year it has been 67%.
Asked about this, Education Secretary Mairi McAllan said Social Security Scotland undertook a "very robust and close examination of all applications", and that people had to prove they were in need.
Last year also saw a record number of referrals rejected from NHS child mental health services, with ministers saying specialist services are only right for a small proportion of young people.
Even with an increase in rejections, the overall number of claims and the cost of the devolved social security system has been rising - and ADP alone makes up more than half of the total cost.
