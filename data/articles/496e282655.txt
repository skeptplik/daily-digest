U.S. flights cleared to take off again after ground stop order caused thousands of delays and cancellations
The Latest
- Flights across the U.S. were grounded for more than four hours on Friday, causing travel chaos across the country and beyond.
- Thousands of flights were cancelled or delayed, including in Canada, after aging air traffic control systems in the U.S. were overloaded, triggering a ground stop order by the Federal Aviation Administration.
- The ripple effect of the FAA's order, which was in effect for more than four hours, will likely last many days as airlines work through the backlog of delays.
Updates
- November 14 What to know about flights in U.S. airspace Rhianna Schmunk The U.S. Federal Aviation Administration grounded all flights across the country for nearly four hours on Friday after a critical communications failure for air traffic control centres, ordering a complete ground stoppage for only the second time since 9/11. The shutdown upended travel plans for hundreds of thousands of passengers in the United States, but also for thousands more in Canada whose flights would've flown through American airspace. The White House said new upgrades overwhelmed "an incredibly old air traffic control system," setting off the outage that forced the FAA to keep planes on the ground. "It's going to continue to fail," U.S. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy warned late Friday. He repeated there was no evidence to indicate a cyberattack, but said investigators hadn't ruled one out. It will likely take days for airport operations to return to normal. Airlines will have to push through diverted aircraft, staffing complications and a backlog of weary passengers, many of whom were stranded at airports with little in the way of answers. Those who were stranded are still advised to check with their airline for scheduling updates or rebooking opportunities. The FAA has only grounded flights nationwide twice before in the last 25 years. After the terrorist attacks on Sept. 11, 2001, the agency closed air traffic for two days. Decades later, on Jan. 11, 2023, domestic departures stopped for about 90 minutes due to another communications issue.
- A timeline of what happened Mike Crawley According to Duffy, the FAA experienced telecommunications issues at 3:36 p.m. ET. He said there was intermittent loss of radar at its regional centres that control high-altitude air traffic between takeoffs and landings, as well as intermittent loss of communication with some aircraft. The ground stop order was issued at 3:53 p.m. ET. More than 3,500 planes were in the air en route to U.S. airports at the time, and that number gradually whittled down to about 400 as planes either landed at their destination airports or were diverted. The FAA lifted the ground stop order at 8:15 p.m. ET. By then, about 6,700 flights had been cancelled and 8,600 had been delayed, according to the FlightAware tracking website. At 8:30 p.m. ET, Duffy told a news briefing at the White House that the outage was caused by old technology that was overloaded.
- Duffy's hard sell for billions from Congress Mike Crawley The White House briefing in large part became a sales pitch by Duffy, who urged Congress to approve billions of dollars in funding to rebuild aging computer infrastructure within the country's air traffic control system. He told reporters that the Trump administration has been asking for the past two years for $30 billion to build a brand new air traffic control system. "Congress, let's make this system more safe," Duffy said. "I can't snap my fingers and build a network overnight, but do you want us to build this for America? And I think every member of Congress should say yes." What Duffy didn't mention is that the Congress that didn't greenlight the administration's funding request has been controlled by the Republican Party — his party — for the past two years.
- Duffy blames 'incredibly old air traffic control system' Mike Crawley Duffy is blaming old hardware for the air traffic control communications outage that triggered the nationwide grounding of flights. "What we have is an incredibly old air traffic control system," he told a White House briefing. "The president for the last year has been asking Congress to fully fund a full build of a brand new air traffic control system." Congress has been asked for $30 billion US for the project, but Duffy says now, in the wake of today's outage, he wants $50 billion. Duffy said the specific issue was instability in communications with planes and radar data at its 22 regional air traffic control centres that handle flights at higher altitudes. "It's an old system that went down and went down everywhere. It got overloaded," Duffy said.
- BREAKING: Ground stop order lifted nationwide Mike Crawley The U.S. Federal Aviation Administration has lifted a nationwide ground stop order that brought flights across the United States to a standstill. The ripple effect of the FAA's order, which was in effect for more than four hours, will likely last many days as airlines work through the backlog of delays. The FAA has lifted its extraordinary order that grounded thousands of flights across the U.S. "At 8:15 p.m. tonight we've opened up the airspace," U.S. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy told the White House briefing. Duffy said it will take some time before air travel returns to normal schedules.
- Mullin says no connection with Microsoft outage Mike Crawley Homeland Security Secretary Markwayne Mullin has kicked off a briefing at the White House by denying the air traffic shutdown had anything to do with the Microsoft outage of the past two days. "I know a lot of people are making that jump," Mullin said. "What we've seen, there is actually no cyberattack."
- Why some U.S. planes are taking off Mike Crawley Video feeds from a few U.S. airports are showing the occasional plane taking off despite the FAA's nationwide ground-stop order. You might be wondering why. These are planes departing coastal airports with flight routes taking them over the Atlantic and Pacific oceans. Such flights don't fall under the scope of the 22 regional air traffic control centres that have been hit by the communications outage that triggered the ground stop. These centres manage flights at higher altitudes as they cross the continental U.S. Flights have been allowed to take off for European destinations from such airports as Washington's Dulles International, and for Pacific Rim destinations from San Francisco and other West Coast airports.
- Air Canada cancels Friday flights to and from U.S. Geoff Nixon Air Canada, citing the ongoing FAA ground stop, says it's cancelling flights both to and from the United States for "the remainder of the evening." In a Friday evening post on X, the airline is also advising that "flights between Canada, the Caribbean and South America that transit U.S. airspace may also be impacted."
- Disappointment at U.S. departures in Vancouver Troy Charles I'm here at Vancouver International Airport as passengers in the U.S. departures wing struggle to sort out travel plans. The big board here shows 22 flights bound for the United States, as of 5:45 p.m. PT, eight of those flights are cancelled and seven are delayed. I spoke with a U-15 B.C. Lacrosse team that had an afternoon flight set for California to play in a Lacrosse tournament. Right before boarding the call came down that their flight was cancelled. The coach of the team says they are out of options and have had to pull out of the tournament. Another Vancouver resident had boarded a plane bound for Las Vegas for his birthday weekend when the captain came over the intercom informing passengers of the nationwide ground stop in the United States. Plenty of long faces and frustrated passengers here as they wait to find out if and when they may reach their destination.
