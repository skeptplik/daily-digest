One of the UK’s flagship electric vehicle battery projects has been thrown into turmoil after its second building contractor in five months walked away, adding to questions over when Jaguar Land Rover cars will use British-made batteries.
The £5.2bn gigafactory in Bridgwater, Somerset, is being built by Agratas, to provide batteries for Britain’s largest automotive employer, its sister company. Agratas and Jaguar Land Rover (JLR) are both part of Indian conglomerate Tata Group.
The UK government has pledged £380m in financial support for the factory because it is seen as crucial to the long-term survival of the British car industry, but the build has suffered several setbacks. The construction specialist contracted by Agratas, TSL, has decided to leave the project after failing to agree terms with Agratas, according to three sources.
Batteries are among the important parts of EVs, but global supply is now dominated by China, South Korea and Japan. Successive Conservative and Labour governments have strongly supported efforts to build a British battery supply chain, and it also fits with Andy Burnham’s hopes to “reindustrialise” the UK. Not all of the subsidy has yet been paid out.
Tata announced the gigafactory in 2023, aiming for a 2026 start of production with as many as 4,200 employees, before pushing it back to 2027. Replacing the lead contractor for the second time in five months is likely to add to doubts over whether Agratas can hit its latest target of January 2028 – although the company has not yet pushed back its planned start date.
A spokesperson for Agratas said: “We are on track to meet our customer requirements.
“The Somerset site is a large and complex industrialisation project, and all of our supplier partners are supporting us well as we prepare for the facility’s launch phase.”
The inability to reach an agreement with TSL, formally known as Tonroe Group, stemmed from disagreements over the timeline and cost of the project, two sources said.
TSL is expected to leave the site within two or three months, leaving no other main build contractor lined up. However, it is thought that NG Bailey, the project’s mechanical, electrical and plumbing contractor, could be one option to take over the entire build.
The issues at the heart of the dispute are similar to those with TSL’s predecessor, Sir Robert McAlpine (SRM). Agratas fired SRM at short notice in June, and one source suggested discussions over the terms of its departure remain ongoing.
Morale within Agratas has also been hit by the company’s decision to relocate some workers from Coventry, in a building shared with other Tata Group companies, to Somerset.
Some of those workers are expected to take redundancy rather than uproot families and move 120 miles away. If they did so it would continue a stream of senior staff leaving the business in recent months, two sources said.
The UK’s only operating gigafactory, AESC’s plant in Sunderland, north-east England, has also faced problems as carmakers have scaled back plans for EVs, with governments delaying bans on petrol and diesel cars. The Guardian revealed in August that AESC had shelved expansion plans after it failed to agree a separate deal to supply JLR.
It is understood that JLR has secured batteries from other suppliers, so its plans to increase sales of new electric Land Rovers and Jaguars will not be affected. However, those initial cars will not have British-made batteries, although the manufacturer could also avoid EU tariffs under “rules of origin” if it uses batteries made in Europe.
Agratas has made some progress at the Somerset factory, as well as another site in Sanand, western India. Some Agratas managers have travelled to India this week to share experiences with their counterparts in India and discuss the UK situation.
The shell of the building site in south west England is mostly complete, and National Grid has started work on the electricity substation that will provide the large amounts of power needed to make batteries.
People close to the project suggest Agratas could still succeed in producing batteries and avoid the fate of an earlier rival project, Britishvolt, which collapsed after failing to secure firm orders. The former Britishvolt site in north-east England is now being turned into a datacentre for artificial intelligence – a potentially attractive prospect for an empty building with a high-capacity connection to the electricity grid.
Agratas was approached for comment. TSL declined to comment. JLR declined to comment. NG Bailey was approached for comment.
