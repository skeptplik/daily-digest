Physical activity
If you are looking to meet recommended levels of daily activity, you don’t have to restrict yourself to a run. National guidelines stress any activity is better than none. 
“And so when we think about sex, that is physical movement. It works your heart and your lungs, works your muscles in the same way that other types of physical activity might do,” said Amanda Daley, a professor of behavioural medicine at Loughborough University. 
Studies have suggested having sex can burn around 100kcal, and raise heart rates to an average of between  about  90 and 130 beats per minute – on a par with a brisk walk.
Yet evidence can be contradictory and differ for men and women. For example, while some studies have suggested a beneficial link between sex and cardiovascular health, another study of older adults revealed men – but not women – who had more frequent sex had a higher risk of cardiovascular events.
The brain 
As Daley points out, sex can have an effect on mood.
“Sex is important in terms of boosting the good, happy mental health hormones and reducing the bad hormones,” she said, noting sex could raise levels of endorphins and reduce levels of the “stress hormone” cortisol.
The hormone oxytocin – often referred to as the “cuddle hormone” – also rises during sex, with researchers noting increases can, among other impacts, have anti-stress, antidepressant and anti-inflammatory effects, as well as influencing the immune system.
Some studies have also suggested sexual activity might be linked to slower cognitive decline, but researchers have noted more studies are needed to clarify cause and effect.
Immune system
“We do have good evidence that physical activity is very important for boosting your immune system,” said Daley. As such, it seems logical that sex might help here too. But the evidence is a little muddy.
One study looked at the sex lives of college students in the US, finding levels of the antibody immunoglobulin A in their saliva was related to how much sex they had. While levels were similar for those who had no sex, infrequent sex or sex three or four times a week, they were higher for people who had sex twice a week. 
Some experts have suggested the results might be down to the twice-a-week participants potentially being in steady and satisfying relationships, which could themselves bring benefits for immune system functioning.
Longevity
 “The one that’s looked at the most is mortality,” said Prof Cynthia Graham of Indiana University’s Kinsey Institute.
Indeed, a number of long-term studies have suggested having more sex could be associated with a lower risk of death. 
However Graham noted many studies looking at the health benefits of sex focus on frequency, rather than broader issues of sexual wellbeing, and do not necessarily look at different types of sex.
There is also the conundrum of whether sex drives better health, happiness and longevity, or whether it is that people in relationships who are healthier and happier have more sex and live longer.
And studies rarely consider different types of sex.
One study that followed 900 middle-aged men in Wales found those who had orgasms at least twice a week had a 50% lower risk of dying in a 10-year follow-up period than men who had them less than once a month. Experts say it is not clear whether the benefits were down to orgasm itself, and hence could be gained by masturbation.
Sleep
Graham said in general there was a lot of research that focused on the potential health benefits of partnered sex. “There’s not much on solo sex and that may be really beneficial [to health too],” she said. 
One area where this has been studied is sleep. One study of 778 people in Australia revealed both men and women reported sleep benefits with orgasms, whether in partnered or solo sex.
Graham added she had also studied the area.
“Masturbation seems to really help, for example, sleep problems in women who are perimenopausal or menopausal,” she said.
