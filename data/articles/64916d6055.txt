After more than two decades, the US has lost its measles-free status, following two years of record case numbers and deaths.
The Pan American Health Organization (PAHO), a specialised service of the United Nations, determined the US has had uninterrupted transmission of the same strain of measles for more than 12 months.
Dr Jarbas Barbosa, director of PAHO, announced that Mexico and Bolivia have also lost their measles elimination status, attributing the resurgence in the region to persistent "gaps in vaccination".
The US Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) said it was committed to "regaining measles elimination status" by expanding access to vaccines.
The US has seen 5,400 cases of measles this year alone, with 10 measles-related deaths, the most the country has seen since the 1990s.
The measles virus - with its signature blotchy red rash - used to infect millions of children a year before a vaccine was invented. The extremely contagious virus can be fatal, and some patients can experience long-term complications including encephalitis and immune amnesia, which resets the immune system, leaving it with only limited ability to respond to new infections.
The US recommends two measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) shots for children between ages one and six, which is 97% effective in protecting against the illness.
PAHO, a group within the World Health Organization, revoked the US's measles-elimination after determining that the virus had circulated nonstop for more than a year.
"These figures are a serious warning for our region," PAHO's director Barbosa said at a news conference on Tuesday. "Measles is resurging globally amid persistent gaps in vaccination."
He added that Canada, which lost its status of having eliminated measles last year after seeing several large outbreaks, has made strides this year to interrupt transmission.
Daniel Salas, the executive manager of immunisations at PAHO, said that the US and others who lost elimination status would be required to submit corrective action plans outlining how they would attempt to improve vaccination rates.
US Health Secretary Robert F Kennedy Jr Kennedy has attempted to make a series of sweeping changes to US vaccine recommendations and policies. The vaccine sceptic has also frequently cast doubt on the safety of MMR shot, as vaccination rates have declined across the US in recent years.
Kennedy has argued that the rise in measles cases started before he took the helm at HHS and that neighbouring countries have also seen more infections.
Responding to Tuesday's announcement, the CDC said the agency and its partners were "committed to stopping measles outbreaks, protecting Americans, and regaining measles elimination status".
"Together, we will encourage MMR vaccination, expand access to vaccines, and help communities close gaps in protection," the agency said.
CDC Director Erica Schwartz said the measles elimination designation was not what protected Americans.
"Vaccination does," she said. "We know where transmission is occurring and we've invested $100 million in state and local health departments to reach those communities."
In a post on social media, Kennedy said: "I continue to recommend MMR vaccination as the most effective way to prevent measles."
"We are working to close vaccination gaps and rebuild public trust," he also said. "We will answer families' questions honestly about vaccine benefits and risks and work with trusted health care providers and community leaders to prevent measles, limit its spread, and support timely treatment."
The UK, Spain and Austria were among European nations that lost their measles-free status earlier this year, along with countries including Russia, Azerbaijan and Uzbekistan.
Nuwan Gunawardhana, an assistant professor of medicine with Columbia University Irving Medical Center, said the US losing its elimination status was "a huge leap backwards".
The US was the first country to eliminate measles in the Americas thanks to vaccination campaigns, noted William Moss, a paediatric infectious disease specialist at the Johns Hopkins Bloomberg School of Public Health.
Dr Gunawardhana said it took massive efforts from public health officials and clinicians to build trust with patients to increase uptake of the MMR vaccine, leading to decades in the US with few infections and deaths.
But that trust has been steadily eroding since the Covid-19 pandemic, when Dr Gunawardhana said patients were confronted with misinformation about the safety of vaccines and lost even more trust in public health systems.
The vaccination rate for MMR across the US has fallen to 92.4%, below the 95% level that provides herd immunity, which limits the spread of measles and protects those who cannot get vaccinated, including newborns.
An outbreak in Pennsylvania has infected over 1,400 people - likely a large underestimate - and killed seven people, according to state officials.
Kennedy, the health secretary, has sparred with Pennsylvania officials over how it is counting the measles deaths, with CDC declining for now to record some of the deaths in its database.
Last year, the health secretary fired all the members of a top vaccine advisory panel and replaced them with his own appointees, some of whom were vaccine critics.
The panel recommended a number of vaccine changes - including no longer requiring the Hepatitis B vaccine for all newborns - but those recommendations were blocked by a federal judge.
President Donald Trump this year also cast doubt on the safety of the MMR vaccine, issuing an executive order for the shot to be split into three separate immunisations for measles, mumps and rubella, a move public health experts say is costly and could sow confusion about an already safe vaccine.
Doctors and public health officials say the misinformation from Kennedy and Trump has played a major role in falling vaccination rates.
"It's confusing patients. It's confusing families," Dr Gunawardhana said. "We need a clear message from the top."
The federal government's vaccine manoeuvres have left public health officials and states to fill in the gaps, offering their own separate recommendations upholding decades-long practices.
