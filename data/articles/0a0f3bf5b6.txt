Hospitals will be investigated for regularly breaching their legal duty to tell patients and their relatives when mistakes have happened.
The public service ombudsman for England is launching an investigation into “repeated failures” by NHS trusts to discharge their “duty of candour” to admit when treatment errors have led to patients dying or suffering harm.
Trusts’ lack of openness about mistakes is adding to the distress felt by those who have been harmed and undermining the “precarious trust between citizen and state”, Paula Sussex warned.
England’s 205 NHS care provider trusts were put under a statutory “duty of candour” in 2014 after the Mid Staffordshire care scandal to try to ensure that staff were honest about lapses in care.
However, critics say trusts’ widespread failure to discharge that obligation means patients and families are denied the truth, and that hospitals do not learn from their mistakes. Patient safety campaigners claim trusts’ breaches of the duty are part of a widespread NHS “cover-up culture”.
Sussex decided to investigate after looking into a number of cases in which patients had been harmed or died as a result of blunders that trusts did not disclose or report to the Care Quality Commission (CQC), as they are obliged to do.
“Patients and their families deserve to be met with honesty and transparency, especially when things go wrong. Too many families are left having to fight for answers and accountability,” she said.
In June, Sussex investigated a case in which a 70-year-old grandmother died avoidably after a series of failures in the care she experienced from Kettering general hospital NHS trust.
Staff were worried about her treatment, with one voicing unease that “her heart could stop at any point”. But neither the unnamed woman nor her family were ever told of these concerns and they were not recorded in her notes.
Her daughter told the ombudsman that “by raising concerns I gave them multiple opportunities to have a transparent conversation with us. But they never told us how serious it was or that she was dying.”
She said: “In the end we were given barely any time to prepare for her death and could not contact other relatives in time to come and say goodbye.
“It was a horrible experience and the lack of candour from the trust made the grief and trauma so much worse.”
Sussex will look into why the NHS is “inconsistent” in its adherence to the duty and make recommendations on how to improve in a report next year. Fear among staff of being blamed for making mistakes and their pressurised work environments may be factors, she said.
Duty of candour obliges trusts to be honest about any “notifiable safety incident” that has led to a patient dying or suffering severe or moderate or prolonged psychological harm.
“It’s unacceptable after over 10 years that trusts still do not always comply with the statutory duty of candour. The provisions in the regulations are clear but the reality is that implementation is sporadic at best,” said Guy Forster, the president of the Association of Personal Injury Lawyers.
“Without openness, lessons are not learned. Failures in care are repeated time and again, and more patients are needlessly harmed. Our members report that fear of repercussions from trust leadership often creates a culture in which staff are reluctant to speak out.”
Blackpool Teaching Hospitals NHS trust is facing a payout of over £3m to Hazel Gregory, its former head of safeguarding. The Health Service Journal reported last week that Gregory sued for unfair dismissal when she was sacked after raising concerns that it was concealing information about deaths, mistreatment of patients and sexual assaults. The judge at the employment tribunal that heard her case struck out the trust’s defence.
Sussex said an NHS campaign could alert both patients and staff to the existence of the duty of candour, to help overcome patients’ lack of knowledge of it, in the same way it has promoted awareness and take-up of Martha’s rule.
NHS England said that it is working to ensure that trusts do adhere to the duty of candour.
“We welcome this investigation by the ombudsman as we continue to transform our culture and improve patient safety across the NHS,” a spokesperson said.
“The NHS is strengthening its duty of candour, including through our Patient Safety Incident Response Framework, making compassionate engagement and involvement of patients and families a core principle after harm or safety incidents occur.”
