An NHS Grampian surgeon attempted to take over a knee operation while he was recovering from a medical procedure and under the influence of drugs, the BBC has learned.
The doctor, who had to be driven to Dr Gray's hospital in Elgin, allegedly tried to step in because a colleague felt unable to complete the surgery.
In a separate incident, it is alleged a surgeon had a physical altercation with a registrar who it is claimed suggested they were about to replace the wrong hip of a patient who was lying unconscious on the operating table.
NHS Grampian said both matters were addressed at the time through the appropriate governance and employment processes, and immediate action had been taken.
The allegations, first reported in The Times, external newspaper, have emerged less than two weeks after the watchdog Healthcare Improvement Scotland (HIS) published a damning report identifying serious shortcomings with NHS Grampian's leadership.
The BBC has verified the first allegation involving the surgeon who it has been reported was recovering from an endoscopy - a procedure where a tube with a camera attached is inserted into the body. Patients are often given a sedative for this.
He was ready to take on the knee operation after being driven from his home to the hospital, because he felt he could not drive himself.
A senior member of NHS Grampian's management team learned what was happening and removed him from the operating theatre.
The BBC has asked NHS Grampian whether the patient had been informed of the incident.
The allegations, including the claim about a separate physical altercation, were brought to the attention of former MSP Douglas Ross by a whistleblower last year.
Ross then raised both incidents with NHS Grampian chief executive, Laura Skaife-Knight, at the end of October by email.
The following week, Skaife-Knight replied that the health board "cannot respond to the specific issues" due to duty of care to individual employees.
Ross told her the response was "unacceptable" and repeatedly asked whether NHS Grampian had been "100% open, honest and transparent" with the press and public - which Skaife-Knight declines to answer.
NHS Grampian's chief officer of acute services, Geraldine Fraser, said the health board takes concerns about patient and staff safety "extremely seriously".
She said: "The matters referred to were addressed at the time through the appropriate governance and employment processes, with immediate and appropriate action taken.
"The organisation sought external support as part of its response and there has been continuing oversight of the service since, including to address workforce challenges relating to this service.
"It would be inappropriate to comment further on any employment matters about individual staff."
Health board built 'defensive wall'
Speaking to the BBC, Ross said it was one of the most serious cases he had received about care in the NHS - and said Skaife-Knight had "stonewalled" him.
He said: "I was surprised, it looked like they were just building up a defensive wall. They didn't want to get to the root of the problem, they just didn't want this problem being exposed.
"I think it's come out now because when we saw the recent report into NHS Grampian and then consultants coming forward saying 'look we've been trying to warn you about this', I felt in a similar position.
"It seemed like politicians trying to represent their constituents were being treated in the exact same way as consultants trying to raise concerns â and that's not acceptable."
The HIS report about NHS Grampian, published on 12 November, found that failures to learn from mistakes had caused "repeated and avoidable harm" to patients.
A whistleblower - Dr Mohammed Habib - then leaked a 2022 review which found 19 avoidable deaths among 45 patients in liver and pancreatic surgery at Aberdeen Royal Infirmary (ARI).
Scotland has had four health secretaries since this review was carried out.
The Scottish government has deployed an oversight team to help NHS Grampian make improvements, and ordered an independent review into hepatobiliary surgery at the health board.
Health Secretary Angela Constance, who was appointed to the role this year, has denied a cover-up, telling parliament this week that staff "should not carry the blame for leadership failures".
Dr Habib said he reported his concerns to the government, including letters to First Minister John Swinney.
BBC Scotland News was told that Swinney's office did not make him personally aware of the letters, with the first minister ordering a review into how his correspondence is handled.
Who knew about the 2022 review?
The review showing avoidable deaths at the ARI was flagged to Scotland's chief medical officer Prof Sir Gregor Smith in October 2022 - a time when Scotland was preparing for NHS winter pressures exacerbated by the Covid-19 pandemic.
Smith told Holyrood's health committee that it was unusual for him to be told about such a report, and he found it of sufficient concern to call a meeting with senior NHS members.
He said surgery at the hospital was paused "very quickly" and he thought the action plan proposed by the hospital's medical director was "appropriate".
Smith said Humza Yousaf, who was health secretary at the time, was informed.
It is unclear whether the situation was escalated to then First Minister Nicola Sturgeon. The report, which was written by Edinburgh University's Prof Stephen Wigmore, was not made public.
Smith said it was not his role to decide whether the review should be made public or whether the first minister was informed.
He said his role was between October 2022 and May 2023, when the all-clear to restart surgery was given.
A spokesperson for Humza Yousaf said he would answer questions about what he was briefed on and his follow-up actions at the upcoming independent review.
