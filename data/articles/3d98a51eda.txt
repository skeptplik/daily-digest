This is a spoiler-free review of The Altruists (though can you really spoil real life?). The eight-episode series is now streaming on Netflix.
2022 was a banner year for TV shows based on true stories about tech CEOs behaving badly, fraudsters, and sometimes both: The Dropout, WeCrashed, Inventing Anna, Super Pumped. It was also the same year that the crypto exchange FTX and the crypto hedge fund Alameda Research filed for bankruptcy, revealing that the two companies founded by Sam Bankman-Fried, “the J.P. Morgan of crypto,” and his math whiz business partner/on-off girlfriend Caroline Ellison were not just closely affiliated; Alameda was funding trades with FTX customers’ money to the tune of $8 billion.
2026 is another standout year for similar entertainment: You Can See Everything, The Social Reckoning, Musk, Artificial. Once again, FTX is in the mix, this time via The Altruists, an eight-episode Netflix series that traces the rise and fall of “Scam Bankman-Fraud” and Ellison, dubbed the Bonnie and Clyde of crypto. While it’s at times maybe more sentimental than it needed to be in humanizing the two, The Altruists doesn’t hold back on its assessment of Bankman-Fried and Ellison as two unpleasant weirdos who knew how to draw the worst out of each other.
Created by Graham Moore, who won the adapted screenplay Oscar in 2015 for The Imitation Game, and based on reporting by New York Magazine, The Altruists begins with the end. Audio news clips about FTX and Alameda’s bankruptcy scandal play over to Anthony Boyle’s Bankman-Fried seconds from being arrested, screening a call from Ellison (an unrecognizable Julia Garner). Before he decides whether to answer, it cuts back to 2016 as Bankman-Fried practices human reactions in the mirror during his early days as a money-making savant at the quant trading firm Jane Street, where he met Ellison.
The Altruists jumps across timelines throughout, and it’s largely effective. In bopping between the contrasting rosy early days and the tension-wracked lead-up to the FTX collapse, the parallel edits serve as a useful tool in watching just how much of their souls Bankman-Fried and Ellison sold to keep their companies afloat. They were up to some risky, sketchy stuff from the beginning, but it was in the pursuit of following the ethos of effective altruism, a philosophical movement popular in tech spaces that, as The Altruists describes it, boils down to the idea of making as much money as possible to give away to charitable causes. But as the director for the Center of Effective Altruism told New York Magazine in 2022: “There has not been a bad actor in history who hasn’t been able to couch their conduct behind some meaningful and generally positive ideology.” (The front of that issue featured a portrait of Bankman-Fried along with the coverline “The Virtue Was the Con.”)
Like its approach to explaining EA, The Altruists maintains a fairly narrow scope ostensibly out of necessity; how easy it would be to go down the rabbit hole of explaining every connection, decision, and detail. It mostly ignores the outside media curiosity to focus on the relationship between Bankman-Fried and Ellison. What the series loses as a result is the bigger picture of just how massive FTX was at its height — though it’s always floating in the background, telegraphed through the sheer amount of wealth shown off on yachts and swanky houses in the Bahamas.
Bankman-Fried was hailed as a visionary, the “next Warren Buffet” who would democratize money, as he cruised through meetings while playing League of Legends. He was invited to the Met Gala and built relationships with politicians and celebrities. The Altruists, on the other hand, depicts him less flatteringly: He was a socially awkward dork whose best ideas seem to have come from other people. The show isn’t terribly kind to Ellison either: She was a straight-shooter who was into writing smutty Harry Potter fan fiction, LARPing, and polyamory. Boyle and Garner are both excellent as Bankman-Fried and Ellison, each doing an affected character voice that is perfectly grating. But Garner — who played a different scammer, Anna Delvey, in Netflix’s Inventing Anna — is the highlight, sinking into her role to the point the prosthetics started to seem like natural extensions of her face.
The script’s one major flaw is something that doesn’t really have an alternative, more elegant workaround. Despite the attempt to simplify a complicated story, The Altruists still needs to explain finance and economic concepts to a Netflix audience. It spells these things out by putting two of its (very smart!) characters into conversations where one brings up a concept, i.e. a prisoner’s dilemma, the other person rebukes them saying they “know what it is,” and then the first person continues to explain it anyway. Again, it’s a hard problem to get around if jargon-spouting, a la Industry, isn’t the goal. Even if it’s laid out in broader strokes, The Altruists wants the audience to grasp just who these people are and what they’ve done.
