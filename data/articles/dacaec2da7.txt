Three days in the dark: How prepared are we for the lights to go out?
Authorities are urging households to prepare for 72 hours without power, water or internet access as cyberattacks, infrastructure failures and extreme weather expose France and other countries to national security vulnerabilities. The message is of practical resilience, not alarm: modest preparations can help keep people safe when essential services falter – and recent security breaches are sparking a wider reckoning across Europe, including France's presidential campaigns.
For years, France’s government-advised kit d’urgence – six litres of bottled water per person, non-perishable food, a wind-up radio, cash, a torch and identity documents – has sat silently on the Interior Ministry’s website, more likely to be consulted alongside extreme weather warnings than during the usual autumnal cadence of Paris in the run-up to Christmas.
But longstanding efforts by the French authorities to embed the idea of national, 72-hour self-sufficiency – first formalised back in November 2025 in public civic-security guide “Tous Responsables” as an update to earlier risk-prevention initiatives – are garnering attention from elsewhere, with other European countries prioritising similar initiatives.
“There are legitimate reasons for concern. The threat is very real, and we are seeing increasingly concrete examples of it, whether through cyberattacks or physical acts of sabotage targeting critical infrastructure,” said François Mattens, lecturer in defence and security strategies at Sorbonne University and Sciences Po.
In October, the UK Cabinet Office launched its “Be Ready” campaign, urging households in England and Wales to prepare to cope for 72 hours without power, water or internet. Weeks earlier, Ireland circulated its “Be Emergency Ready” booklets to 2.2 million homes by post, echoing the European Commission’s earlier Preparedness Union Strategy. What once appeared a regional civil-defence approach is increasingly becoming mainstream core-security policy on a national level.
Fearmongering social media frenzy?
While the 72-hour benchmark may seem alarmist, emergency planners insist the opposite. They say the three-day period offers a practical operating window – an interval during which households may need to manage before roads are cleared, substations repaired, telecoms restored or supply chains re-routed. Their view is that this is not Cold War bunker-nostalgia or doomsday prepper survivalism, but a pragmatic admission that modern, hyper-efficient societies have become so reliant on electricity, software and just-in-time supply chains that they are vulnerable to a very slim margin for error.
Bernard Benhamou, Secretary General at the Institute for Digital Sovereignty in Paris, shared that the notion of a “kill switch” (the threat a foreign actor could cut national services) – albeit once a figment of science fiction – is “now fully present in the mind of all policymakers in Europe”.
“It is the first time ever that a technological problem or issue was mentioned by all the candidates for the presidential election in France,” he said.
“It's on us to vote for the right kind of government that's going to be able to navigate through these challenges.”
In the past two weeks alone, a series of events have catapulted the subject into public consciousness. A global Microsoft outage brought to near-standstill the services relied upon by businesses, schools and public bodies, with the impact rippling to global effect, prompting the Japanese government to rely on telephone communication and making the UK’s National Health Service revert to paper records to maintain essential care and appointments. A separate failure across US aviation forced a nationwide ground stop – only the second in US history, and the first prolonged air-traffic shutdown since the 9/11 attacks. Meanwhile, in the banking sector, JP Morgan Chase experienced a disruption to transaction processing which the bank attributed to a bug in internal hardware systems – and crucially, not a cyberattack.
“It is somewhat unfortunate that there has been a confluence of unlinked outages and high-profile disclosures of AI-discovered vulnerabilities in widely deployed technologies,” said Rafe Pilling, senior director of Threat Intelligence at the Sophos Counter Threat Unit at cybersecurity firm Sophos.
He said that while the issue is not necessarily new, it is becoming all the more pertinent on a modern, cross-border level. He drew on the 2024 incident of CrowdStrike, when a faulty software update by the US cybersecurity firm caused tech outages across multiple global sectors, disrupting banks and media, among others.
But he warned against listening too closely to social media sound bites or misassociating recent events with “imminent Armageddon”.
Mattens agreed: “Sometimes this amplification is deliberately orchestrated by hostile actors seeking to destabilise our societies. But it is also inherent in the business model of social media platforms: fear, outrage and strong emotions tend to generate engagement, which in turn generates revenue.”
Digitally dependent refrigerators
France’s digital dependence is especially visible. Carte Vitale health reimbursements, SNCF railway ticketing, and the Linky electricity smart meters deployed across 95 percent of households all depend on continuous connectivity. ANSSI, France’s national cybersecurity agency, has repeatedly warned that digital dependency creates cascading risks across critical infrastructure – and recent events have made this abstract policy debate feel more immediate. In 2025, the agency recorded 1,366 confirmed malicious incidents out of 3,586 treated security events.
Last month, French authorities said they traced an attack on supermarket refrigeration systems to an Iranian cyber operation, with hackers allegedly raising temperatures from -1°C to over 50°C, affecting nearly 700 French food distribution sites and ruining entire inventories of perishable food. Similar attacks were reported elsewhere in Europe, with at least 11 countries affected.

“The French refrigerator cyber incident shows how the cyber world can directly impact the real world,” said Kyle Chan, fellow at US-based policy think tank the Brookings Institution.
The subsequent attack on a French blood bank revealed the fragility caused by over-reliance on sole system providers, highlighting a need for digital diversification across key industries, Benhamou noted. “This is a ‘hybrid war’, as they say now. It's a new kind of war… The fact is that architectural security has to be completely renewed and things from the bottom up.”
Firm French foundations
Mattens maintains that France has a strong instutional foundation, but believes the next step involves embedding preparedness into schools, workplaces and local communities. “Resilience is not just about infrastructure, emergency plans or government capabilities. It’s also a question of mindset.”
France’s institutional foundation includes the ORSEC framework, a decentralised civil protection system that is activated by departmental prefects when a crisis overwhelms local capacity to help and support vulnerable residents.
When the system was triggered in response to storm Ciarán in November 2023 – an event that left 1.2 million households without electricity and killed five – Enedis, the public grid operator that supplies electricity to 95 percent of continental France, restored power to 95 percent of customers within five days, and the firm has since shared that typically, for 90 percent of its customers affected by major storms, it is able to restore electricity within 48 hours.
“France has companies and infrastructure operators with impressive recovery capabilities,” Mattens said.
Look across France’s eastern border and preparedness takes a more literal form. Swiss law is built around the principle that the government must be equipped to provide every resident with a protected shelter near their home. Approximately 370,000 public and private shelters are distributed across the famously neutral country that is not a member of the EU, and in cases where municipalities lack sufficient capacity, locals are required to build, equip and maintain them. Elsewhere in Europe, Sweden recommends preparing for at least one week of self-sufficiency.
Politics by other means
But Paris-based Gaël Musquet, a meteorologist and ethical hacker who has played an instrumental role in the United Nations’ (UN) Caribe Wave initiative – an annual exercise in tsunami preparedness across the Caribbeann including Guadeloupe – believes France has some way to go in terms of accepting the “risk culture” that might be more widely present in other countries.
He underlined how recent cyber events highlight the interconnected nature of modern society: “It is not fearmongering. Each of these events is a recall… we must be prepared of any kind of occurrence that can shut down the various networks that coordinate critical infrastructure – our food, water, safety and healthcare.”
“Russia and other governments want to involve and disturb the democratic process,” he said.
“When you protect the democracy, we protect also our industry, we protect our economy... So we must address sovereignty.”
Drawing on how the recent Nvidia scandal displays where modern society, politics, tech and power intersect, Benhamou turned to the words of artificial intelligence researcher Kate Crawford: “AI is politics by other means”.
And herein lies the political opportunity.
“Europe has a huge opportunity to develop and to invest in open-source software, open hardware, open protocols to develop our own solutions to be able to be sovereign… so we can resist, be independent, and have the engineers and technicians that can answer as quickly as possible if we are attacked,” said Musquet. He added that France has the opportunity to address the issue of technological sovereignty over the course of the next presidential term.
He pointed to how the leader of the far-left France Unbowed party, Jean-Luc Mélenchon, has embedded into his campaign for the 2027 presidency the notion of French resilience by including proposals to address different types of national threats. Musquet also noted how right-leaning newcomer candidate and Cannes Mayor David Lisnard had readied the coastal city for the threat of tsunamis – with Nice the only other French city to do so.
“Politicians in this debate must respect the Article 3 of the Universal Declaration of Human Rights. Each human has the right to be respected in security – security of their life. It is a right that our state, our government holds due to us.”
The way in which presidential candidates address issues of sovereignty and external threat will surely play into France’s changing political landscape. But the overall message is one of practical readiness, not panic. Citizens should prepare to know what they might need, how to get information and who nearby might need help. In a society built around provisions that are expected to serve continuously, a little preparation could make 72 hours in the dark easier to manage.
