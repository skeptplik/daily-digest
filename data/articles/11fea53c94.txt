The progress AI has made in mathematics this year is nothing short of extraordinary. In January, I was impressed to see relatively minor open problems tackled with the help of AI. Now, AI is being used to solve the hardest problems in maths. The obvious next question is, if AI can do this, is there anything it can’t do? That is the inference that the AI companies want us to make, but, in reality, the answer is much more nuanced.
We see this messaging in claims that AI can “solve X”, where X is any field of human endeavour you care to name. Biology is a big one. “Cancer, Alzheimer’s, heart disease, mental illness – these will be relics of the past in, I think, less than a decade,” said Anthropic CEO Dario Amodei while promoting the company’s recent initial public offering. “I think one day maybe we can cure all disease with the help of AI,” said Demis Hassabis, CEO of Google DeepMind, last year, adding that this could be “within the next decade or so”.
This phenomenon isn’t new: I call it “physicist brain”, after the propensity for physicists to wade into areas outside their expertise and declare simple solutions. More broadly, it is called Nobel disease, after the many Nobel prizewinners who have gone on to make, shall we say, less evidenced-backed claims. In general, there is a tendency to think that if an intelligent person is good at one thing, they are good at many other things that require intelligence. What is different now is that trillion-dollar companies and large swathes of the global economy now depend on this being true of artificial intelligence.
To be clear, I think we must now admit that AI is better at solving open mathematical problems than any human, and that this has happened faster than anyone ever expected. When I first saw the news in September that the Navier-Stokes problem had been solved by OpenAI, I felt stunned. We had entered a new world. With the announcement of the Riemann hypothesis proof this week, I felt sick and had to go and have a lie-down. These were intellectual Everests I have marvelled at since my teenage years, conquered by a machine far sooner than anyone expected. You may have read my recent piece forecasting the future in 2096 – an early draft had a line, later removed, about three Millennium Prize Problems being solved by AI in the 2030s. We’ve had two this year alone.
But maths isn’t biology. Yes, we have seen advances like AlphaFold, Deepmind’s AI that can predict the structure of proteins, but this is biology at its most mathematical, a computational puzzle. True advances in biology and medicine require studying cells, animals and people, all of which can be frustratingly illogical and uncooperative. Amodei admitted as much in 2024, writing that “the main challenges with directly applying intelligence to biology are data, the speed of the physical world, and intrinsic complexity”. You can imagine mitigations here – AI-directed robotic labs, for example – but it doesn’t seem like we should expect massive speed leaps in capability comparable to those seen in mathematics. There isn’t an easy way to confirm that a biological discovery is correct, in the manner that the computer code Lean allows for mathematics.
Worse still are claims that AI will “solve climate change”. For Sam Altman, CEO of OpenAI, this came as a throwaway comment in a 2024 essay: “Although it will happen incrementally, astounding triumphs – fixing the climate, establishing a space colony, and the discovery of all of physics – will eventually become commonplace.”
But we already know the solution to climate change: reduce greenhouse gas emissions to net zero and the warming will stop. The blockers to doing so, as demonstrated by the recent failure of the COP31 climate summit, are political, not technological. Even if AI were to produce a blueprint for a perfectly functioning nuclear fusion reactor tomorrow, we would still have to build thousands of them across the globe in the next decade or so to avoid the most catastrophic warming effects. In the past decade, we have built 60 nuclear fission reactors, a technology we understand far better than an as-yet-mythical fusion power plant. Again, you can see how AI might offer some improvements, but not enough to counteract our fossil fuel use.
It is important for me to have some humility here, because one thing AI’s breakthroughs in mathematics this year has underlined for me is the need to change my mind when the evidence changes. I started that process with the early signs of AI progress in maths, but didn’t update my beliefs fast enough – though, to be fair to myself, neither did almost everyone else, including leading mathematicians such as Terence Tao, who, just weeks ago, said he didn’t expect the Riemann hypothesis to be solved by AI until 2050.
For that reason, I am on the lookout for early signs that AI success in biology is shifting. That could include an AI-designed drug proving useful in people – for example, Rentosertib, a leading candidate, is now undergoing a phase III trial as a treatment for idiopathic pulmonary fibrosis, a type of lung disease. The trial is expected to conclude in 2029, so we’re talking years of wait for any results. If the drug succeeds, that doesn’t necessarily provide a way for any other AI drug candidates to succeed, and the painfully slow process of molecule selection, animal testing and human trials is still hard to speed up.
If I’m forced to make a prediction, I can fairly confidently state that we will see yet more advances in AI maths in 2027. I also expect that we will see some completely unexpected results in other fields as well. But solving biology? Ending climate change? Defeating disease? These still feel, for now, like science fiction.
Further AI reading
- AI maths has become a spectator sport, thanks to a website dedicated to tracking agents attempting to solve the P vs NP problem, one of the remaining huge open problems in mathematics (see my explanation of it here). I don’t expect this approach to work – the models being used here are consumer ones, rather than the internal frontier models the AI firms have used for the big maths results so far.
- As if to illustrate this, both OpenAI and Anthropic have released, with little fanfare, solutions to the VP vs VNP problem. This is a lesser version of the P vs NP problem, and doesn’t necessarily help us get any closer to solving its big brother.
- And speaking of internal models, Axios reports that both Anthropic and OpenAI employees are concerned that their internal models are so powerful that they can’t safely be released. Take this with a big pinch of salt – Anthropic said the same about its Mythos model due to its cybersecurity abilities, then later released its Fable model, which is essentially just Mythos with some added guardrails. And don’t forget that, way back in the mists of 2019, OpenAI said that GPT-2 would be too dangerous to release. Today, you can run far more capable models on a smartphone, and no one finds that dangerous.
- Jensen Huang is back as Nvidia CEO after a mere 11 days away following a scandal about his admitted affair with an alleged Chinese spy. For a reminder of why Nvidia is so important to the AI industry, see my previous column.
- A UN conference has failed to agree to a ban on autonomous weapons, as my colleague Matthew Sparkes predicted last week.
