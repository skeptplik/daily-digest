The Australian Transport Safety Bureau is investigating flight crews’ “mental models” after a Qantas plane aborted its landing at Sydney airport on Wednesday after noticing another aircraft crossing the runway while on its descent.
The ATSB said Qantas flight 426 from Melbourne was due to land just before 10.15am on Wednesday, with the Boeing 737 jet told to expect a late landing clearance due to aircraft crossing the runway.
As the Qantas plane continued to approach the airport, and before it received a clearance to land, the crew reported seeing an Air New Zealand plane crossing the runway.
The Qantas crew decided to conduct a go-around, aborting the landing before trying again.
The ATSB said it would investigate what it called an “aircraft separation issue”. The agency will gather evidence, including interviewing flight crew, looking at flight data and monitoring air traffic control audio and any available CCTV into the incident.
“In particular, the investigation will seek to ascertain what information was provided to the crews of both aircraft, and what mental models those crews held,” the agency said in a statement.
“The ATSB notes that, during the occurrence, important safety defences were in place and operated, such as the flight crew’s go-around decision.”
A preliminary report should be complete in about eight weeks, the ATSB said.
Qantas did not immediately reply to a request for comment.
“Our pilots were following air traffic control instructions and elected to conduct a go-around as the other aircraft was crossing the runway,” the airline told the Sydney Morning Herald.
Sydney airport has seen a series of near-miss incidents this year, including when a Jetstar plane narrowly avoided a collision with a Qatar Airways plane on the tarmac in August.
In another incident, a Virgin plane was forced to abort takeoff when a Qantas plane began to cross the runway in front of it.
Angus Mitchell, the ATSB chief commissioner, released preliminary investigation reports in September that found staffing levels at Sydney airport had “emerged as a recurring area of inquiry”, with a review finding runway incursions and near misses at 20% above national averages there.
“This should be viewed in the context of Sydney airport being the most complex in the country,” Mitchell said at the time.
The ATSB stressed it “has yet to determine whether staff resourcing factors directly contributed to any occurrence, or whether they were simply underlying conditions that were recognised and being effectively managed by Airservices, Australia’s air traffic services provider”.
The ATSB added Friday its examination of the recent incident would seek to look at safety defences in place “and to establish or rule out any underlying safety issues and contributing factors common across the other occurrences at Sydney airport currently under investigation”.
