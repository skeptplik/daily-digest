We all know what happens to the One Ring in The Lord of the Rings. It's forged in the fires of Mount Doom, used by Sauron to dominate Middle-earth, and eventually destroyed in the very same flames thousands of years later by a Hobbit named Frodo Baggins. But what did it actually take to create one of the most powerful artifacts in all of fantasy? First published in 1954, The Fellowship of the Ring retconned the fun ring of invisibility that Bilbo Baggins took from Gollum in The Hobbit and transformed it into the Dark Lord’s greatest weapon. But its actual creation has remained largely a mystery. Now, 72 years later, The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power on Prime Video has finally dramatized the forging itself, and the process is even more spiritually devastating for its creator than anyone could have imagined.
"A theme throughout the earlier seasons of this show is the idea that creation requires sacrifice," series co-creator Patrick McKay tells Polygon. "One of Sauron's challenges this season is that he needs to learn what that means. He needs to actually be willing to sacrifice something. But that also means that maybe there's a part of himself he can't get back after that."
Spoilers follow for The Lord of the Rings: The Rings of Power season 3, episode 7.
How Sauron finally forges the One Ring
Rings of Power season 3 began with Sauron (Charlie Vickers) trying and failing to forge the One Ring. The solution he needs comes from two unlikely sources: Galadriel's long-lost husband Celeborn (Jamie Campbell Bower) and a Balrog (Simon Pegg), one of the ancient demonic beings that served Morgoth during the First Age.
After torturing and manipulating Celeborn, Sauron learned in episode 6 that he needs silver and gold from Valinor to create the Ring. Conveniently, he still has Celebrimbor’s hammer, which was constructed by the legendary Elven craftsman Fëanor and used to create the Silmarils.
In episode 7, the Dark Lord travels to Khazad-dûm and speaks to the Balrog, who begrudgingly reveals that Sauron has to “become one” with the mysterious Flame of Udûn. The demonic creature, also known as Durin’s Bane, warns there’s an even greater cost he must pay.
"It's a personal journey as well as a practical one," Vickers says.
When Sauron asks the Balrog how he can survive the endless fire, the Balrog's long answer (only revealed to the audience later on as Sauron forges the One Ring) is cryptic and terrifying.
                        To know the mystery of the flame of Udun, the dark fire that shall one day eclipse even the Secret Fire, thou must become one with it. Thou must submit to its embrace. Reduced in infinite pain to purest being until thy will shall become the only will that is or ever was. Thou shalt descend. Thou shalt be expurgated. Not because the fire shows you mercy but because you no longer need it. Cast off the last light of The One and become only thyself. Thou and thou forever.
"Expurgated" is a particularly striking word here that implies a kind of cleansing through the removal of something unwanted. It’s a term typically used to refer to removing the offensive parts from a piece of writing, yet here it’s the opposite: erasing the faintest flicker of God’s light from Sauron’s existence.
At the Crack of Doom, deep inside the iconic volcano that defines the land of Mordor, Sauron offers up his hammer, causing the lava to recede before molten rock begins pouring down from the cavern ceiling. He plunges the hammer into the falling lava, repeatedly exposing himself to its heat. His hands burn as molten gold and silver drip across his arms and trickle down onto the anvil below. Once enough has pooled, he tosses a few shards of mithril into the mixture and watches it melt down. Then he begins hammering the anvil. Every strike sends a flaming shockwave through the cavern that reverberates back to the anvil like an echo. Gradually, the scattered pieces of metal coalesce into the shape of a ring.
It's an extraordinary moment, one McKay describes as the show's greatest technical undertaking to date.
"It is the most visually complex and ambitious sequence we've ever done in the show between score, sound, set design, visual effects, and anchoring all of that, Charlie's performance," McKay says.
But the darkest part of it all happens after the forging is complete.
What did Sauron sacrifice to create the One Ring?
In Tolkien's mythology, the Secret Fire, also known as the Flame Imperishable, is the divine creative power of Eru Ilúvatar, the supreme creator of the universe. Even Morgoth, Sauron's former master, could never possess it. Therefore, he could only corrupt life, never create it. In The Fellowship of the Ring, Gandalf refers to the Balrog he fights at the Bridge of Khazad-dûm as “flame of Udûn,” and Rings of Power takes some liberties here to establish it not as a moniker for the Balrog but as essentially the source of all evil in Tolkien’s universe.
The Balrog’s instructions suggest that Sauron has to purge himself of whatever connection remains between himself and his former divinity. Remember that Sauron, Gandalf, and the Balrogs are all Maiar, immortal angelic spirits created directly by Eru Ilúvatar, the God of this world. The language of being "emptied" makes the forging sound much like a ritual of spiritual annihilation.
When Sauron holds the One Ring for the first time, he undergoes a transformation similar to what we’ve seen previously this season when a man puts on a Ring of Power and transforms into a Nazgnagôl. Wracked with pain, dark energy surges through his body. He collapses, seemingly dead, only for the Ring to move of its own accord and slip onto his finger. The Balrog then proclaims his ascension as the Dark Lord, invoking ancient servants of Morgoth, including Ancalagon and Ungoliant. Sauron rises and casts an incantation with Black Speech to inscribe the Ring with its iconic little poem: "One Ring to rule them all, and in the darkness bind them."
The implication is that Sauron has poured so much of his own power and will into the Ring that it has become an extension of his being. Tolkien himself made this connection explicit, but The Rings of Power gives it a much more tactile interpretation. The Balrog says Sauron won’t burn or die, but he will be emptied and reduced to a being of pure will. This process does seem to “kill” Sauron’s physical body, and his concentrated willpower controls the Ring. The sequence seems to present Sauron's transformation as an attempt to replace the divine power of creation with the absolute authority of his own will. Has he essentially transcended the very nature of his existence?
The process comes at an enormous cost. By concentrating so much of his essence into a single object, Sauron has made himself extraordinarily powerful, but he has also created the weakness that will eventually destroy him.
The One Ring changes everything for Middle-earth
Across Middle-earth, all three elven Ring-bearers — Círdan (Ben Daniels), Gil-galad (Benjamin Walker), and Galadriel (Morfydd Clark) — immediately sense the creation of the One Ring and remove their rings. That’s in line with Tolkien’s canon, since they’re able to sense Sauron reaching out in an attempt to control them through the rings.
McKay compares the One Ring's role in the escalating war between Sauron and the elves to a nuclear weapon that could decisively alter the balance of power.
"For Sauron, the Ring is like the atom bomb that he wants to have the edge to win the war," he says.
The forging has been a long time coming, both for Sauron and for the show's creators. McKay says he and co-showrunner J.D. Payne always intended season 3 to revolve around the One Ring, an idea that dates back to their earliest plans for the series nine years ago.
Yet the episode also makes clear that Sauron's triumph contains the seeds of his eventual defeat. In seeking absolute control over Middle-earth, he had to invest so much of his own being into something that can be physically taken from him and ultimately destroyed. At that moment, however, he is perhaps too arrogant to even consider the possibility.
The Balrog promised that Sauron wouldn't die in the fire. Instead, he would be emptied. Thousands of years later, when the One Ring melts in the fires of Mount Doom, Sauron finally learns exactly how much of himself he gave up to create it.
