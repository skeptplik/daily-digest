U.S. Supreme Court set to hear 3 major Trump agenda immigration cases
Top court just added major asylum case to its docket, with third-country deportation arguments set for Dec. 9
The U.S. Supreme Court late last week agreed to take on a case central to the Trump administration's immigration agenda concerning the conditions under which migrants can request asylum.
While the announcement ended up being buried in a Friday afternoon news cycle that saw the unforeseen ground stop of U.S. air travel, it means that the top court will now hear at least three major immigration-related cases in its session that runs through June 2027.
The Trump administration's "retinterpretation of certain detention and deportation policies" has led to a "particularly high number of immigration disputes" compared to previous sessions," according to SCOTUSblog, which tracks the work of the top court. The blog, in an October post, also highlighted a handful of other cases that, while narrower in scope in terms of their ramifications, could also be described as immigration-related.
The top court in the 2025-2026 session rejected a Trump administration bid to end birthright citizenship going forward, but it made an impactful ruling that has paved the way for the federal government to deport large numbers of Haitian Americans and other residents who have been in the U.S. for years after fleeing natural disasters or turmoil in their home countries.
Overall, these new cases could lead to Supreme Court rulings that further reshape the American immigration and asylum system, or at least provide clarity in response to actions, some very controversial, that have been undertaken by President Donald Trump's second administration.
Here is a look at the three major cases, so far, of the 2026-2027 docket:
Executive authority to limit asylum
Generally speaking, U.S. laws have recognized through the Immigration and Nationality Act (INA) and other legislation the rights of migrants to make an asylum claim when arriving at a border entry point, or even if they are caught trying to cross into the country between entry points. In most years, denials have been greater than asylum claims being granted, though the percentage has fluctuated.
In his final year in office, former president Joe Biden sought to stem the tide of surging arrivals at the southern border and encourage appointment-based claims with what was described as an executive order meant to be temporary, which limited asylum eligibility based on the daily number of encounters at the border.
On the very first day of his second presidential term in 2025, Trump signed an executive order deemed necessary due to an "invasion" at the southern border.
Trump's order has essentially amounted to a blanket asylum ban for migrants caught crossing between border points. The prohibition has also applied to migrants who arrive at official border crossings and lack certain medical or criminal history information, a requirement that advocates say is often impossible to meet for people fleeing violence.
It was blocked first by a federal judge and then in April by the U.S. Court of Appeals in D.C. The panel ruled 2-1 that the INA doesn't "allow the president to remove [migrants] under summary removal procedures of his own making."
In a partial dissent, Judge Justin Walker wrote that the administration can broadly deny asylum applications, but that the INA and other laws forbid sending people back to countries where they would be persecuted.

Whether that has been practised in the asylum claims the government does consider is arguable. Denial rates in some cases have climbed to over 90 per cent, according to the Transactional Records Access Clearinghouse at Syracuse University. Denial rates that high have not been seen for decades, if ever.
Democratic House Rep. Rob Menendez of New Jersey, posting Friday after it was learned the Supreme Court would take on the case, said "fixing our broken immigration system means upholding our values as a nation of immigrants" while "protecting the right to seek asylum."
Third-country deportations
The Supreme Court justices will gather to hear oral arguments on Dec. 9 regarding a controversial plank of the Trump administration's deportation policies, also related to executive orders the president signed on his first day back in the Oval Office in 2025.
The U.S. traditionally has followed a principle of non-refoulement. That is, not returning persons to countries where it could be reasonably expected they'd face persecution or torture due to race, religion, sexuality, political views or other factors.
People deported from the U.S., all nationals of third countries, under an agreement to accept such deportees, arrive at the Juan Santamaria International Airport, near Alajuela, Costa Rica, on April 11. (Mayela Lopez/Reuters)
Flowing through the Trump executive orders, however, the administration has sought workarounds through third-country deportations. According to a website run by two human rights organizations, the Trump administration has spent more than $95 million US to deport over 28,000 people to 31 different countries and has struck agreements to potentially send people to four additional countries.
In many cases, people are being sent to countries to which they have no connection. In addition, some of the countries that have struck agreements with the U.S. have had questionable track records in terms of upholding human rights and freedoms.

"These migrants are being transferred to countries or territories with which they have no ties, which can be a traumatizing and isolating experience that compounds harms already suffered which triggered their fleeing to the U.S. in the first place," the UN's Human Rights Council said in a statement earlier this year condemning the practice.
The administration has said that it has struck deals with foreign governments where the safety of the deportees has been assured. But the New York Times reported last week after an extensive investigation that it had it identified 110 persons who were deported to a third country but then put on a plane back to their home country of origin, even after U.S. courts had established they had reasonable fears in leaving those countries to seek asylum.
Access to bond hearings
Under past administrations, most non-citizens without a criminal record who were arrested away from border points had an opportunity to request a bond hearing. In many cases, that allowed them to work and remain free while their cases, which would ultimately determine if they would be deported, wound their through immigration court.
The Trump administration has ended that practice, citing an interpretation of existing 1996 legislation. After conflicting rulings in federal courts across the U.S., the Supreme Court will take up the issue after the American Civil Liberties Union filed a lawsuit on behalf of Ricardo Aparecido Barbosa da Cunha.
