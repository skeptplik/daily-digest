Gene editing is already saving lives, with hundreds of trials happening around the world. But there is an upstart in the wings: epigenome editing, which turns genes on or off instead of altering them. The first human trials of epigenome editing are producing promising results, and it could prove to be an extremely powerful tool.
“What’s great is that we now have a demonstration of epigenome editing in the clinic showing that it’s effective at what it’s supposed to do,” Charles Gersbach at Duke University in North Carolina told the Engineering the Epigenome conference in Paris earlier in November.
Epigenome editing has tremendous potential for treating a wide range of conditions. It could also help in a less direct way, said Gersbach, by helping biologists understand our genome and thus work out the root causes of common conditions.
“We don’t really know what the cause is of most of our common, most debilitating diseases,” said Gersbach. “It is not surprising that we don’t know what causes Alzheimer’s or neuropsychiatric conditions or autoimmune disease when we don’t know what the vast majority of our genome does.”
Gersbach is a cofounder of Tune Therapeutics, one of several companies testing epigenome editing as a treatment for the hepatitis B virus that infects the liver. Globally, around 240 million people were infected with hepatitis B as of 2024, and the virus could eventually kill many of them by causing liver failure or cancer.
Vaccines can prevent hepatitis B, but we don’t have good treatments. The problem is that our immune system can’t completely get rid of hepatitis B because the virus can hide indefinitely, in the form of circular loops of viral DNA inside liver cells. Viral DNA also integrates into the genome of liver cells, which is why the virus can cause cancer. “Each cell could have dozens of copies of this viral genome,” said Gersbach.
Using standard CRISPR gene editing to destroy the viral DNA would be risky. Making lots of DNA cuts at the same time increases the risk of the wrong ends being joined together, which could cause chromosomal abnormalities and even cancer.
But what if the viral DNA could be permanently deactivated without having to cut it? That’s what epigenetic editing can do.
Cells naturally make so-called epigenetic changes to their DNA to control gene activity. For instance, as cells specialise into muscle or nerve cells, some genes need to be turned on, and others turned off.
There are many kinds of epigenetic changes, but they can all be thought of as labels that tell cells whether a particular piece of DNA should be active. For instance, attaching methyl tags to DNA – a process called methylation – can shut down genes without altering the underlying sequence.
Standard CRISPR gene editing relies on a protein called Cas9, which has two key components: a targeting part that finds a specific piece of DNA, and the “scissors” that cut DNA at that spot. Replace the scissors with an enzyme that adds methyl tags, say, and the modified CRISPR Cas9 can be used to make epigenetic changes. In other words, a gene editor can be modified into an epigenome editor.
Early attempts at this worked, but the gene-silencing effect lasted only a few days, said Angelo Lombardo at San Raffaele University in Italy. “That was a big problem, at least for therapy applications,” said Lombardo. “You want an epigenetic editor that can impose stable gene silencing.”
His team succeeded in making one by linking parts of three different enzymes to a de-scissored CRISPR Cas9 protein. Animal studies have shown that these kinds of editors can permanently shut down gene activity, with the effect passed on when cells divide, just like most natural methylation.
It’s working in people, too, said Gersbach. In the Tune trial, researchers have been measuring levels of an RNA molecule produced only when the hepatitis B genome is active. “At high doses, or with multi-dosing, we’re getting 97 to 99 per cent repression,” he said.
The effect also persists after just a single treatment, and there have been no serious safety issues. It remains to be seen whether this will prevent liver damage and cancer, but for the wider field, what matters is that it is working in the sense of shutting down genes inside the human body.
“Epigenetic editing is on par even with CRISPR gene knockout. It is maintained, and it is reversible,” said Marianne Rots at the University Medical Center Groningen in the Netherlands.
In other words, epigenetic editing can do everything standard CRISPR Cas9 gene editing can do but without the risks of cutting DNA. What it can’t do is correct disease-causing mutations, unlike more advanced forms of CRISPR gene editing, such as base editing and prime editing.
But controlling gene activity alone could help treat a huge range of conditions, including inherited ones. Gersbach points out that the first approved CRISPR Cas9 treatment, for sickle cell disease, works by destroying the “off switch” for the gene for fetal hemoglobin, not by correcting mutations. Epigenome editors should be able to achieve the same result.
Another treatment being tested in people lowers cholesterol by using CRISPR base editing to wreck the “on switch” for a gene called PSK9. Lombardo’s team has developed an epigenome editor that achieves the same result. In monkeys, it reduced PSK9 activity by 90 per cent, resulting in a 70 per cent fall in cholesterol. In theory at least, the epigenome-editing version should be safer.
But epigenome editing does have drawbacks. As several speakers at the conference described, the complexities of how cells control gene activity mean an epigenome editor that works beautifully for shutting down one gene may fail to turn off another gene, or may work in some cell types but not in others. “It depends on the context,” says Stylianos Tsagkris at the European Molecular Biology Laboratory (EMBL) in Rome.
Tsagkris has developed a way of targeting thousands of sites in a single study to assess how well different epigenome editors work. The ultimate aim is to predict which epigenome editors will work in which circumstances, but there’s a long way to go, he says.
Another issue for the field is that, while existing epigenetic editors work well for some genes, at least, they could do with improvement. For instance, editors that consist of a de-scissored CRISPR Cas9 fused to other enzymes are very large proteins, making them hard to deliver, said RJ Jiao at Rice University in Houston, Texas. Some can be harmful to cells, too.
So his team has used an AI system called AlphaFold to design an epigenome editor where the de-scissored Cas9 isn’t fused to enzymes, but instead binds to existing ones in a cell to achieve the same results. This approach was inspired by the way many natural proteins form complexes, Jiao told the conference.
Our ability to design new proteins like this is rapidly advancing, he said. “The same approach can likely be applied to many other epigenome-modifying proteins.”
Then there’s delivery – getting epigenome editors into the organs where they are needed. At the moment, only the liver is easily targetable, hence the hepatitis B trials. But this delivery problem isn’t specific to epigenome editing, and many groups and companies are making progress towards targeting other organs.
The big one is the brain. Turning genes on or off there could help treat everything from mental illnesses and neurodegenerative disorders such as Alzheimer’s to even multiple sclerosis. “Ninety per cent of the interesting targets are in the brain, so when we can target that, the field will really take off,” says Jamie Hackett at EMBL.
