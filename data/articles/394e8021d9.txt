Few things could generate more buzz for a small restaurant than a visit from Jensen Huang. In recent years, the former Nvidia CEO became known for dining out at street markets and hole-in-the-wall noodle joints across Asia, drawing huge crowds of fans and photographers wherever he went.
But after Huang resigned last week amid revelations of multiple extramarital affairs, some of the food vendors he helped put on the map are wondering whether their most famous customer is still good for business. In Taiwan, where Huang was born and his dining habits have long been a spectator sport, many say they aren't ready to turn against their hometown hero.
Tu Chang-chun, the owner of Ice World, a shaved-ice shop in Taipei, describes Huang as friendly, the kind of person who puts others around him at ease. His impression of the billionaire hasn’t changed since he learned of his affair with Alice Huang, a businesswoman accused of spying for China.
“It’s not as if he killed someone or set something on fire. Why blow it all out of proportion?” Tu said on Monday evening, standing beneath the yellow lights of his stall at Linjiang Street Night Market. “Now, a lot of foreigners walk past, see my sign, and laugh. People just love kicking someone when they’re down.”
At the entrance of his shop, Tu continues to proudly display a photo of Huang posing with him and his wife. “I think he’s such a warm and approachable person,” Tu added. “Why are people treating him this way?”
Tu says that Huang has visited his shop three times since 2023, and he always orders the same thing: mango shaved ice with tangyuan (sticky rice balls). The dessert, a blend of tropical fruit and condensed milk, is popular in Taipei’s bustling night markets, especially among first-time visitors to the island.
“When he came in for shaved ice, he would spend half an hour eating a single bowl,” recalled Tu. “Whenever someone came up to take a photo with him, he got right up. He’s not pretentious.”
At a tofu pudding shop near Ningxia Night Market in western Taipei, photos of Huang remain on display alongside those of other prominent technology figures who have visited, including Morris Chang, the founder of Taiwan Semiconductor Manufacturing Company (TSMC). The store manager, Hsieh, defended the shop’s decision to keep them up.
“I don’t think this changes the fact that he’s a very successful businessman,” Hsieh said of Huang’s affairs with Alice Huang and other women. “Having an affair is a private matter.”
Hsieh, who agreed to be identified only by his surname for privacy reasons, said that Huang had visited the shop four times over the past few years, and on each occasion he seemed friendly and down-to-earth.
In Taiwan, Huang’s culinary indulgences extended well beyond street food. He frequented some of Taipei’s most exclusive restaurants, where he hosted technology executives for what became known in the local press as “Trillion-Dollar Banquets,” a reference to the attendees’ combined net worth of more than 1 trillion New Taiwan dollars.
The dinners often drew crowds of journalists and photographers hoping to catch a glimpse of Huang and his guests. The intense media attention helps explain why so many photographs and videos of Huang with Alice Huang were captured in Taiwan. Restaurant owners, meanwhile, welcomed the publicity, often proudly displaying photographs of themselves with Huang inside their establishments.
But not every food vendor has rallied to Huang’s defense. Some have removed advertisements or photos featuring him, while others have tried to capitalize on the scandal to generate publicity. A night market vendor in Taipei told Taiwanese media that he was considering taking down advertisements associated with Huang out of concern that they could hurt his business.
The Taiwanese tea chain 19 Tea House launched a new drink this week, giving it a name that pokes fun at Huang’s alleged “womanizing” ways. According to the chain, the drink combines osmanthus-infused honey with fresh lemon, offering a blend of fragrant floral notes and sweet-and-tangy citrus flavors.
Support for Huang in Taiwan has also extended far beyond the restaurants he frequented. On Monday evening, Taipei 101, the island’s iconic skyscraper just blocks from one of Huang’s favorite night markets, lit up with messages of support for the former Nvidia CEO, including “Taiwan ♡ Jensen,” “Family ♡ Friendship,” “Thrive Together,” and “Taiwan AI Build Future Together.”
Tech industry figures who supported the initiative told Taiwan’s state-run Central News Agency that the illumination campaign was intended to “help restore Jensen Huang’s reputation,” urging the public not to “kick him when he's down, but instead offer warmth and support during the autumn and winter.”
