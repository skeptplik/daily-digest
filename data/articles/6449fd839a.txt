Jensen Huang received a “hero’s welcome” from employees on Monday after Nvidia’s board of directors reinstated him as CEO, according to people who were present. An outspoken faction of Nvidia’s workforce had demanded his return and greeted him with chants, raucous applause, and hugs at the company’s headquarters.
But other employees were quietly stewing. Several current and former Nvidia staffers had viewed Huang’s abrupt exit earlier this month as a rare opportunity to change the “one team” corporate culture of unfettered unity and minimal bureaucracy that he had cultivated over the past three decades, according to interviews with the workers, as well as internal correspondence and social media posts reviewed by WIRED. Most spoke on the condition of anonymity because they feared backlash from colleagues.
Some employees were alarmed by Huang’s alleged extramarital affairs, including one with a subordinate and another with a suspected Chinese intelligence asset. But their frustrations extended beyond the CEO’s personal conduct. Current and former workers describe a culture at Nvidia in which allegedly abusive behavior was tolerated and challenging leadership was perceived as discouraged.
“Everyone agrees that mistakes were made, the question is what we are ready to forgive,” one employee wrote on an internal platform before Huang’s return. “I don’t know how comfortable I’ll be working at a place where such affairs are not taken extremely seriously.”
Another added, “If Jensen comes back, I’m not doing any HR trainings ever again.”
Nvidia did not immediately respond to a request for comment.
When Huang returned to Nvidia’s offices in California on Monday, he told employees who greeted him that he would do a better job with his second chance at CEO. (As of Monday afternoon, he had not yet sent a companywide note about his return, as he was still getting his laptop set back up.)
US president Donald Trump said he played a crucial role in convincing Nvidia's board to bring back Huang. A person close to Trump calls him an irreplaceable CEO and says they expect him to exercise better judgment going forward.
Nvidia’s board announced it will continue an independent investigation into whether Huang had been a target of foreign intelligence services and that it will double down on plans to step up corporate governance.
It’s difficult to gauge how widespread dissatisfaction with Huang’s leadership may be at Nvidia. The company’s emphasis on unity has historically made it difficult for people to openly question his leadership or choices, workers say. “There's only one team at Nvidia,” the chipmaker’s official website declares.
A public petition campaign known as Stand With Jensen garnered signatures from more than 10,000 Nvidia purported employees, according to its website. Nvidia has roughly 49,000 employees and 18,000 contractors overall, according to a person familiar with its workforce.
But inside Nvidia, one employee told colleagues that “some portion” of the signatures appeared to be “illegitimate,” noting that the names included “Satan” and media personality Mia Khalifa, who does not work at the chipmaker. The employee wrote that they found 75 suspicious names in an hour of checking. WIRED was unable to independently verify the findings. The petition’s organizers did not respond to requests for comment.
Huang’s supporters have praised how he made the company feel like a “big super family.” They credited him for minimizing bureaucracy, making himself available to staff, and supporting employees during difficult periods, including the Covid pandemic. A former employee who attended numerous meetings with Huang and other senior executives says the CEO was particularly adept at preventing bureaucratic infighting. He could detect corporate “politics and nonsense before it was even born,” says the worker.
There are also powerful financial reasons for Nvidia employees to feel loyal to Huang. His bets on chips and software for artificial intelligence have transformed Nvidia into one of the world’s most valuable companies and made many longtime employees wealthy in the process.
But for some employees, the revelations about Huang’s behavior have forced them to question that loyalty. Jason Sequeira, a longtime engineering leader at Nvidia, wrote on LinkedIn last week that he ultimately couldn’t decide whether he wanted Huang back as he weighed arguments from both sides. “Some believe his contributions to Nvidia and the industry are so extraordinary that he deserves another chance,” Sequeira wrote. “Others argue that making exceptions for powerful leaders sets a dangerous precedent. … I don’t have a clear answer.”
Several current and former workers say that Huang’s management style could come off as overly harsh at times, including, for example, toward longtime colleagues he viewed as having grown complacent as Nvidia’s market value surged. Two former employees say that he regularly raised his voice at workers, sometimes in ways they found belittling.
Other employees were more concerned about whether Nvidia’s board would hold Huang accountable for allegedly misleading the directors about his affairs. “That’s something we can’t have,” one employee says.
Another source of frustration for Nvidia workers has been the growing influence of Huang’s two children and son-in-law, who all hold senior titles at the chipmaker. In March, his daughter, Madison Huang, scolded a colleague on a call with about 100 other employees, according to a participant who didn’t believe the anger was warranted. “I’m disappointed you asked that. Are you fucking stupid?” the employee recalls Madison saying in response to a question about workshops on OpenClaw technology scheduled for an upcoming Nvidia conference. “Your mom can build a claw.”
Madison and her brother, Spencer Huang, didn’t immediately respond to requests for comment.
Praveen Mhapsekar, a senior system software engineer who separated with Nvidia last week after disputes with his managers and HR, says he and colleagues stopped feeling comfortable complaining about practices that concerned them because of the trouble they believe it brought on themselves.
“This organization requires greater discipline and structured processes,” he says. One of the world’s most valuable companies “cannot be managed like a private startup operating out of a garage, where personal influence takes precedence over established procedures.”
Huang’s return doesn’t eliminate the prospect of Nvidia evolving to quell frustrations. But with internal issues more public than ever before, maybe the days of one big family all cheering along together are mostly gone.
