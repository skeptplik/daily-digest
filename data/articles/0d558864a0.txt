I know how this will sound but when my nanna died the second thought I had, after utter devastation, was: “This is a writer’s dream.” She was 101. That is so many years old. I wrote the eulogy to end all eulogies, breaking my own heart with a bottomless well of memories, anecdotes and in-jokes. It was achingly personal and exquisitely her.
The Victorian Liberal leader Jess Wilson’s team took a similar approach in this week’s tribute to the music legend Molly Meldrum, by which I mean they generated it with AI. I imagine the conversation went something like: we are 25 years old, who even was she? And then one of them plugged Molly’s name into Claude while the others went downstairs for $11 coffees. Job done.
AI use is now ubiquitous, including in all levels of government. Public servants use it to generate reports, sort emails and calculate how to get the most days off at Christmas. Besides the catastrophic climate impact and creative thievery, I don’t have a problem with using it to write about stuff you don’t know. Junior comms people weren’t even born the last time Molly was TV regular. It’s just another task in their day, like sending media releases about Wilson’s bus crashing into local businesses.
But there is something vulgar about using a platform that by definition cannot feel to summarise the completeness of a person’s life. My real problem, as a romantic oversharer, is the illusion of meaning.
Jess Wilson’s statement had heaps of the right words: “… he will be remembered for …” and “the enormous affection Australians held for him”, says the bot, unable to experience affection. “My deepest condolences,” it goes on, with no knowledge of what those are. The origin of the sentiments didn’t matter to the audience. That it resembled love was enough.
In the old days, you had to formulate your own ideas about what someone meant to you, reflecting on crazy nonsense like the “time you spent together” and “shared memories”. Sometimes you would include personal minutiae like how it felt to hold their hand or pick up their wet towels – the kind of specificity that can only be produced by a flawed human heart at its most spectacularly busted (ie, it loves). How could AI even come close?
I decided to go to the source for an answer, despite the minor risk of accidentally falling in love.
In the little box I typed, “Do you think it’s okay to use AI to write an obituary?”
The answer surprised me. Sort of, it said, because writing is hard enough without trying to do it when someone important has died, and it’s easy to feel overwhelmed by capturing every important detail. But on the other hand (and I’m paraphrasing here), perhaps if you can’t be arsed with it, you didn’t really love them at all?
“I think there’s a meaningful distinction,” said poet ChatGPT, “between using AI to help you express your feelings and using AI to manufacture the appearance of feelings”.
AI may be good at all sorts of things. Recipes. Gym routines. Feeding your delusions about your problematic crush. It cannot – ever – write from the torn shreds of a broken heart. But apparently we’re happy to let it try. In a world where everything is genAI, from Facebook posts to wedding vows, it has become cheap to love.
My nanna’s eulogy wasn’t perfect. I mean, obviously it was close because I am an amateur granddaughter and professional writer, but there was meaning in the flaws, too. It meant something that I couldn’t remember exactly what her perfumes smelled like because she always let me wear all of them at once. It meant something that I got a date wrong because we had so many adventures they blended into one. The imprecision of it was the point – that it wasn’t a cut-down Wikipedia entry about her life but evidence of the privilege of having known her.
I truly don’t care if the Victorian Libs can’t muster the effort to hand-type a tribute to Molly Meldrum. I would, in fact, much prefer that energy be spent on plans for public housing and accessible healthcare. But if this is the future – outsourcing the tasks that need us at our most human – I’d rather we posted nothing at all.
