Fantasy television has given us some of the most imaginative stories ever told, transporting viewers to magical kingdoms, mysterious alternate realities, and worlds filled with creatures that shouldn't exist. Shows like Game of Thrones and Rings of Power dominate the conversation, pushing some of the genre's best storytelling to the forefront, but there’s something to be said of the smaller productions and shorter stories.
Miniseries offer a unique opportunity to explore extraordinary settings and compelling characters without stretching their stories across multiple, seemingly endless seasons. From beloved literary adaptations to wonderfully strange animated adventures, these eight fantasy miniseries prove that you don't need hundreds of episodes to build an unforgettable world.
8 Hogfather (2006)
While its name might sound a little too ridiculous for most, Terry Pratchett's Hogfather offers a rather eccentric introduction to the mythology of Discworld, arguably one of fantasy literature's most imaginative settings. The two-part miniseries focuses on Susan Sto Helit (Michelle Dockery), the granddaughter of Death himself in her investigation into the disappearance of the Hogfather — the Discworld equivalent of Santa Claus.
While Susan hunts for answers, Death (Ian Richardson) reluctantly takes over the sleigh to keep the holiday spirit intact, even as a sinister conspiracy works to erase humanity's capacity for belief. Hogfather balances clashes between guild assassins and oblivious wizards with Pratchett's trademark wit hiding a moving story about imagination and the quiet necessity of magic underneath all the festive absurdity.
7 The 10th Kingdom (2000)
Fairy tales have inspired countless fantasy adventures, but few television productions have had as much fun upending classic folklore as the 2000 miniseries The 10th Kingdom. The story kicks off when reluctant New Yorker Tony Lewis (John Larroquette) and his daughter Virginia (Kimberly Williams) fall through a magical portal into the Nine Kingdoms, a sprawling parallel world governed by the descendants of Snow White, Cinderella, and Little Red Riding Hood. As one might expect, they quickly discover that life following "happily ever after" is messy, corrupt, and deeply dangerous.
Caught in the crossfire of the Evil Queen's (Dianne Wiest) ruthless plot to seize power, Tony and Virginia go on the run with a golden retriever who happens to be the rightful Prince Wendell (Daniel Lapaine) and Wolf (Scott Cohen), a hopelessly smitten half-wolf fighting his own carnivorous urges. Driven by an oversized cast of colorful oddballs and a self-aware sense of humor, The 10th Kingdom transforms familiar fairy tale tropes into a delightfully unpredictable adventure for the small screen.
6 The City & The City (2018)
Blending police procedural grit with surreal science fiction, BBC Two’s critically acclaimed two-part adaptation of China Miéville’s The City & The City is hard not to recommend. Inspector Tyador Borlú (David Morrissey) investigates the brutal murder of a foreign student found dumped in a grimy European city called Besźel, a place that occupies the exact same physical space as Ul Qoma, its ultra-modern twin metropolis. The two distinct populations are strictly conditioned to "unsee" one another and their overlapping architecture, effectively living in separate parallel realities despite sharing the same streets.
As Borlú’s investigation crosses dangerous and forbidden borders, he soon uncovers a conspiracy that challenges everything he understands about his world. Anchored by Morrissey's methodical performance, the miniseries delivers an intellectually fascinating mystery you’ll have to see to believe.
5 Watchmen (2019)
Sure, it might lean more into the sci-fi and superhero categories, but HBO's Watchmen is still a surprisingly worthwhile hit for fantasy buffs. Set in an alternate version of America decades after the events of Alan Moore and Dave Gibbons' groundbreaking graphic novel of the same name, the nine-episode miniseries follows Angela Abar (Regina King), a Tulsa police detective who operates under the masked identity Sister Night.
As she investigates a conspiracy involving white supremacists, the police, and some very powerful individuals, the series gradually reveals a much stranger story involving godlike beings, extraordinary abilities, and the nature of time itself. King is phenomenal, and Damon Lindelof delivers one of television's most ambitious superhero stories, one filled with grit and emotion. Despite its fan backlash, Watchmen is a terrific show that deserves to be experienced in its full entirety.
4 Neverwhere (1996)
Originally conceived as a 1996 BBC Two six-part miniseries before Neil Gaiman adapted it into a bestselling novel, Neverwhere takes an ordinary London commuter and drops him into one of the most imaginative urban fantasy settings ever designed for television. The story follows Richard Mayhew (Gary Bakewell), a mild-mannered Scottish securities analyst whose impulsive decision to help an injured young woman named Door (Laura Fraser) causes him to slip through the cracks and effectively disappear from the world above.
Unable to return to his former life, Richard finds himself navigating London Below, a bizarre underground society populated by a slew of eccentric inhabitants who exist beyond the awareness of ordinary people. His journey alongside Door becomes an increasingly dangerous adventure filled with strange encounters and unexpected revelations. Although its modest production values show their age, Neverwhere remains an imaginative piece of fantasy television for any fan of the genre.
3 Gormenghast (2000)
Gormenghast isn’t like normal fantasy tales, dropping the familiar dragons and magic in favor of a decaying Gothic castle where centuries-old rituals dictate all aspects of everyday life. Based on Mervyn Peake's celebrated novels, this four-part miniseries begins with the birth of Titus Groan, heir to the ancient castle's ruling family. His arrival coincides with the escape of Steerpike, an ambitious kitchen servant determined to climb the castle's social hierarchy by manipulating its eccentric inhabitants.
Across four decadent episodes, Peake's enormous gothic castle becomes the stage for a wonderfully bizarre tale of ruthless ambition and festering political intrigue. Driven by its suffocating atmosphere, eccentric cast of characters, and surreal visual design, Gormenghast feels unlike almost anything else in the fantasy genre.
