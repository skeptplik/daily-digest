EU capitals are divided on several fronts ahead of key talks on the bloc's next long-term budget: overall size, political priorities, Ukraine financing and new EU taxes. Tense domestic politics make compromise harder, with only leaders expected to break the deadlock.
National deficits, political manoeuvring and the cost of supporting Ukraine are making the EU's budget negotiations increasingly difficult. Member states face the delicate task of balancing competing demands while keeping negotiations on track.
On Saturday, European Council President António Costa made a bold move to break the deadlock over the bloc's next seven-year budget, known as the Multiannual Financial Framework (MFF), ahead of a special European Council summit starting on Thursday.
Costa's proposal cuts the overall volume further, touches cohesion funding for the first time, introduces a new digital levy and partly rolls over the joint debt under the EU's post-pandemic recovery fund. Member states responded with the usual bashing.
At a meeting of EU ambassadors, known as COREPER, on Monday, "many states said that it is not a basis for compromise and that we are getting further away," said an EU diplomat who, like others quoted in this article, spoke with Euronews on condition of anonymity.
"Yesterday's COREPER discussion showed this file needs to be dealt with at the leaders' level," a second EU diplomat told Euronews. "It was pretty bad, four hours of discussion with no practical outcome."
Public spats have become a fixture of EU budget talks, blurring the lines between negotiating tactics and actual positions as member states dig in their heels.
Overall size
German Chancellor Friedrich Merz called Costa's proposal a "first step", a relatively mild reaction compared with that of the German ambassador, who was far more vocal at COREPER and rejected it as a basis for discussion.
Berlin and the other so-called "frugal countries", net contributors such as the Netherlands and Sweden, want a further cut to the overall volume.
In current prices, Costa trimmed the budget by €204bn compared with the Commission's original proposal. In real terms, the cut is a more modest €181bn.
Roughly €10bn of that comes from delaying repayment of NextGenerationEU, which the frugals see not as a reduction but as kicking the can down the road.
Despite the visceral opposition of countries like the Netherlands and Germany to more joint debt, the proposed delay drew little pushback, as it is seen as the lesser evil. Still, another €30bn in real terms is necessary to reach the €200bn threshold.
But how much cutting is enough? The frugals have no common position and prefer a vague call for "several hundred billion euros". In practice, that may only be settled at the last minute through national rebates.
Political priorities
An Élysée Palace source was quick to dismiss Costa's proposal as "very disappointing", and said it "does not constitute a basis for an agreement" primarily because it failed to guarantee income support for farmers.
France's stance baffled other capitals. Paris has already won considerable concessions, including the digital levy and an untouched Common Agricultural Policy envelope.
The frugals prefer to call themselves the "modernisers". They argue the EU budget should focus on emerging priorities such as defence, migration and innovation, which have instead borne the brunt of the cuts.
Costa did touch cohesion funding for the first time, but the frugals regard the cut as largely symbolic, a result of opposition from a 17-strong "Friends of Cohesion" group led by Italy and Romania.
For the frugals, a rebalancing of spending is needed to reflect the new geopolitical reality.
"Central and Eastern European countries are still not able to prioritise between traditional policies and investment in resilience and security. It means others will do it for them. It will strengthen the South's systematic efforts for prioritising cohesion over regions close to Russia," a third EU diplomat told Euronews.
Ukraine support
Merz has linked the budget talks to financial support for Kyiv. Slovakia, Czechia, Hungary and Bulgaria, which want to opt out of the upcoming top-up of the Ukraine Support Loan, are also staunch defenders of cohesion policy.
The move prompted a fierce backlash from the four leaders. Slovakia's Prime Minister Robert Fico branded Merz's tactic "blackmail" and threatened to use Slovakia's veto to block the budget.
"The fact that MFF and Ukraine are in parallel is almost obvious, even if many don't like it. The important thing is that one does not put the other at risk, but in reality, it will," a fourth diplomat told Euronews.
A possible compromise would let the countries that want out do so for the military part, worth €25bn, while still backing the civilian part, the remaining €20bn.
"The European Commission is doing a lot of creative thinking on the financing of Ukraine. There is a sense of optimism," the second diplomat said.
EU-wide taxes
A key novelty in Costa's proposal is an EU-wide levy on digital services, to replace an extremely controversial corporate tax.
The digital levy did not receive strong opposition from member states, except Ireland, home to most of the European headquarters of US tech companies. The main argument against it is the risk of trade retaliation from Washington.
The Commission first proposed a digital services tax in 2018, and France, Italy and Spain have national versions in place. But EU countries are wary that the digital economy has changed significantly since then, so the proposal needs to be refined.
Several other EU-wide taxes to finance the budget, known as own resources, still face significant opposition. That is notably true of the Emissions Trading System (ETS), strongly opposed by the likes of Poland and Bulgaria.
Again, willingness to accept new own resources that cost some countries more than average may only be secured in the final stretch, when leaders push for country-specific concessions elsewhere.
Complicated context
The tension is not confined to Brussels. With interest rates rising, energy prices high and deficits ballooning under the weight of public spending and support for Ukraine, many member states feel the budget heat at home.
In Belgium, the government's survival is at stake after coalition talks on the 2027 budget collapsed. In the Netherlands, the minority government faces a serious political crisis over a tax package tied to the national budget.
Romania's government collapsed in May, and the country is running a deficit close to 8%. In France, budget disputes have been a major driver of instability for two years.
Overall, 10 of 27 member states are in an excessive deficit procedure, and the Commission estimates the number could rise to 16 after next year, based on projected spending.
Upcoming national elections in key countries such as France, Italy and Poland, along with the rise of extreme forces on both ends of the political spectrum in regional elections in countries like Germany, are hardening governments' stances.
"Political and economic context is very difficult, and that makes it very difficult for different member states to compromise," the fourth EU diplomat added.
