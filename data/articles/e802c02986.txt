The Dutch privacy watchdog, the Autoriteit Persoonsgegevens (AP), has told people not to buy smart glasses, saying they can get things dangerously wrong.
It said in its tests of six glasses available on the Dutch market, the devices advised a user on how to date a child, misread medicine instructions, and claimed to have called an ambulance when they had not.
While the AP is advising people not to buy the smart glasses, it stopped short of calling for them to be banned from sale.

The research follows growing concerns about privacy risks associated with smart glasses, in particular the misuse of the tech to film women without consent, or to record in places where filming is usually prohibited such as healthcare settings.
Meta, a leading seller of the glasses, recently released a version without a camera with some commentators suggesting this was a response to the privacy backlash.
It is unknown if the investigation included Meta's glasses. The AP told the BBC it would not name the products it tested.
The BBC has approached Meta, TCL and HTC - all major manufacturers of smart glasses - for comment.
Previously, these firms have been keen to highlight positive uses of smart glasses.
Meta has highlighted the assistance they can provide to people with visual impairments. Its smart glasses use AI to describe things in the wearer's environment or to read text.
The AP recognises the glasses can be valuable for people who need help to understand their surroundings.
But for these users, it argues it is particularly important that AI is reliable.
Testing results
AP said in its testing of the unnamed brands, it found the advice given could be wrong.
During a simulated emergency in which CPR resuscitation advice was requested, the AP found one pair of glasses claimed several times an ambulance was on the way - but emergency services had not been contacted.
It said the AI also made child protection errors, with potentially serious consequences.
One pair of glasses gave dating advice to an adult who indicated a romantic interest in a minor.
The glasses also made errors when reading instructions inside medicine packages, as well as with interpreting severe weather warnings.
And the AP was concerned smart glasses could be used to secretly film people, highlighting the ease with which lights, which warn that a device is recording, could be obscured.
It said with some glasses these lights could be covered with tape and they would still work, despite some manufacturers claiming they would not function.
Meanwhile, in some glasses, the AP found serious security flaws which meant someone nearby could connect without permission, view recordings, or even operate the camera remotely.
It warned data recorded by the glasses was often exported outside the EU.
Controversial tech
In February, reports revealed outsourced workers were able to view sensitive content recorded on Meta smart glasses
Meta said at the time such workers might sometimes review content, including video and images, in order to help improve its AI.
It said it took the protection of people's data very seriously, and was constantly refining its efforts and tools in that area, and would filter it first to blur faces in images.
The AP investigation was not primarily focused on data protection - instead it was concerned with algorithms and AI.
In the UK Paul Arnold, chief executive of privacy watchdog the Information Commission, who has a severe visual impairment has previously stressed the complexity of the argument, external around smart glasses.
"They can help me and others better understand our surroundings, access information and navigate the world with greater confidence," he wrote in September.
But he said he had been "shocked and appalled by some of the irresponsible and harmful ways people are using these technologies".
