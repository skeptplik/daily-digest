Hospitals are bracing for a “horrible spike” in flu in the coming days as winter starts to bite, the head of the NHS in England has said.
Flu and other winter viruses including Covid pose such a threat that hospital bosses should consider telling staff and visitors to wear masks, Jim Mackey said, as figures showed rates of admissions to hospital with flu were at the highest for the time of year since before Covid.
This week several health service trusts in England were forced to declare critical incidents because of winter pressures.
Mackey said at an NHS Alliance conference in London that more hospital beds were occupied by people with flu than at the same time last year. “My instinct is that this will build probably over the next five to 10 days” and start to feel like “proper winter” within a fortnight, he said.
The rate of admission to hospital for people with flu rose sharply last week from five per 100,000 to almost nine per 100,000, the highest proportion this decade for this period in November, according to UK Health Security Agency data.
This has happened even though flu is still circulating at only “moderate” levels. Levels of Covid and the respiratory syncytial virus have also increased, although both remain “low”.
Worryingly, low numbers of people – including just a third of those with a long-term health condition – have so far been vaccinated against flu, the UKHSA said, as it urged those eligible for a free jab to come forward.
The agency said some people who were at increased risk of serious illness if they got flu were as much as 48 times more likely to die if they contracted it compared with the general population.
Mackey criticised the health service for allowing “corridor care” – leaving patients often unsupervised on chairs and trolleys in corridors near the hospital’s A&E unit – to become normalised in too many places, given the risk it involved for patients’ safety.
“We are now in a world where we have to accept that the NHS has normalised something, accepted something, that should never have happened, and it’s becoming embedded in parts of the NHS,” he said. “Let’s just all accept that that should never have happened. Whether it’s from a safety point of view or, more importantly actually, from a personal dignity point of view, it’s something we all need to try and get a grip of.”
Almost two-fifths (38%) of people who seek help at the two A&Es run by Nottingham university hospitals NHS trust end up experiencing corridor care – the highest proportion in England – Health Service Journal analysis of official data last week showed. More than 5,000 patients ended up in corridors at its two hospitals between August and October.
NHS trusts that have had to declare critical incidents this week – official admission that they cannot cope with the demand for care they are facing – include those in Nottingham, Derby and Burton, Kettering and Northampton, and Nottinghamshire.
