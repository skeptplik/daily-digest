Bill Watterson walked a few different tightropes when he created Calvin and Hobbes, one of which involved Calvin's relationship with school. It would've been easy to make it simply antagonistic, with Calvin as the hero fighting back against the dark forces of educational conformity, a bit like a comic adaptation of Pink Floyd's "Another Brick in the Wall." Any number of later webcomics that were unsubtly inspired by Calvin and Hobbes went this way, at least at first.
Instead, Watterson was always careful to clarify that the biggest problem with Calvin's education is Calvin himself. His general attitude is likely familiar to anyone who spent any time as the "smart kid" in a public school: he knows everything there is to know about the things he likes and doesn't care enough to fake his way through anything else. If he was being graded on paleontology, comic books, or abstract snowman art, Calvin would be a straight-A student. Instead, he's struggling through basic math and early American history.
That leaves his teacher, Miss Wormwood, in an unfortunate position. In the few glimpses we get of the other students in Calvin's class, like Susie Derkins, Miss Wormwood doesn't have too many problem kids. It's just Moe, the class bully, who might not even be in Miss Wormwood's class, and Calvin, who's a force of pure chaos.
Like most of the rest of the Calvin and Hobbes supporting cast, we deliberately don't know that much about Miss Wormwood. Like Calvin's parents, she doesn't have a first name. The few details that Watterson provided include that she's only five years from retirement, she smokes like a chimney, and every once in a while, Calvin drives her to the point where she guzzles antacid straight out of the bottle. She's a near-universal stand-in for a particular sort of elderly American public school teacher, who've reached the end of their careers and are just marking time until they're pensioned off.
Watterson has noted several times, such as on his old official website, that Miss Wormwood's unusual name is a reference to the apprentice demon in C.S. Lewis' 1942 Christian novel The Screwtape Letters. The literary Wormwood is an understudy to Screwtape, both of whom are in the business of tempting humans into sin.
Despite her nominative origins, Miss Wormwood isn't really an antagonist in Calvin and Hobbes. (School as an institution is, to some extent, but she isn't.) She's simply someone that, in Watterson's words, really believes in the power of education. It's just too bad that the people she's trying to educate don't really appreciate it.
You get what you give
In most of the best comics that feature Miss Wormwood, she's an almost incidental presence. For example, she makes a silent appearance in one of the best Spaceman Spiff strips, shaking Calvin out of a particularly vivid daydream.
To be fair, this is the case for almost every character in Calvin and Hobbes who isn't Calvin or Hobbes. Calvin's parents both occasionally get a strip or two in the spotlight, but the focus is otherwise squarely on Calvin.
That being said, if you had to point to a single best strip for Miss Wormwood, there's really only one contender in my mind. It's a simple strip from 1992, which is part of a larger running gag about Calvin constantly challenging school as an institution.
"What assurance do I have that this education is adequately preparing me for the 21st century?" Calvin demands during class. "Am I getting the skills I'll need to effectively compete in a tough, global economy? I want a high-paying job when I get out of here! I want opportunity!"
Miss Wormwood's reaction is to get right in his face, in a bit of angry-teacher body language I remember from when I was a kid. "In that case, young man, I suggest you start working harder," she tells Calvin. "What you get out of school depends on what you put into it."
"Then forget it," Calvin mutters.
It's the one time Miss Wormwood manages something that could be seen as a victory, as usually Calvin is the one to drive her crazy. This time, she gets the last word, as well as offering a useful piece of advice to any struggling student.
