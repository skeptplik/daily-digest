Doctors have issued a warning about eye injuries linked to the rapid rise in popularity of pickleball, after a woman in her early 50s who was hit in the eye during a game developed glaucoma.
She sustained a direct hit to her left eye while playing without protective eyewear. The woman reported immediate blurry vision, severe pain and extreme light sensitivity. After emergency treatment, she was discharged from hospital and her symptoms seemed to be resolving.
Weeks later though, visual blur and pain in her left eye worsened. Three months on, she had developed structural optic nerve damage and impaired vision as a direct result of the injury. Doctors diagnosed the previously healthy woman with glaucoma.
The case study was documented in the medical journal BMJ Case Reports. Doctors said it was part of a pattern of eye injuries linked to soaring uptake of the sport worldwide.
“This case illustrates the potential for angle-recession glaucoma to develop as a complication of pickleball-related ocular trauma,” wrote doctors from UMass Chan medical school, in Worcester, Massachusetts.
It represented just one case, they said, but blunt trauma to the eye was a recognised risk factor for glaucoma, and other types of eye injuries linked with pickleball were increasingly being reported.
“Pickleball-related ocular injuries include reports of retinal tears requiring surgical repair, traumatic lens subluxation and a comprehensive series describing various patterns of ocular injury directly attributable to pickleball participation,” they wrote.
Pickleball combines elements of badminton, tennis, table tennis and chess, and can be played indoors or outdoors. The game is typically played as doubles and is especially popular among retirees. Seen as a social activity, it is also a great way to keep the body and mind active.
Like squash and tennis, the risk of other injuries – damage to achilles heels, rotator cuffs and ACL tears – is well known. But pickleball is also now a source of serious eye injuries, particularly among older players who don’t always wear protective eyewear, the doctors said.
“The unique characteristics of pickleball, including the proximity of players to the net, the high velocity of a struck ball, the speed of play, and the demographic profile of participants, may contribute to distinct injury patterns that warrant specific preventive measures,” they wrote.
Their recommendation? Protective eyewear. “With the growing body of players and advancement of technology in the sport, such as modern paddles that can generate shots exceeding 60 miles per hour, these injuries will continue to occur unless proper safety measures are implemented.”
