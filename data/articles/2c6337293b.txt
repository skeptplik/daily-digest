Many studies over the years have examined how people encounter climate science information. Some focused on the top results that Google surfaced (back when Google was still in the business of serving links), while others examined social media content or newspaper and TV news coverage. With large language models (LLMs) now forming a share of the public information diet, it’s worth looking at how they handle climate change.
But there’s no direct way for researchers to look at the information people have seen. It is served ephemerally to individual users, confined to their chat session. A study led by researchers from University College London and the United Nations Educational, Scientific, and Cultural Organization (UNESCO) tried to tackle this question from multiple angles, testing LLMs with different prompts and evaluating their output. They started with benchmarks of simple factual questions (benchmarks that might even be in models’ training data at this point) but also analyzed more complex interactions—including when users think they’re right to push back on an answer.

LLM research often faces (no-true-Scotsman-tinged) criticism for focusing on a particular model rather than some other frontier model that may have been released after the experiment concluded. In this case, the study used 33 models, including multiple versions of the major frontier and open-weight models. The flagship frontier models used were Claude Fable 5, Claude Opus 5, Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview, Gemini 3.6 Flash, and GPT-5.6 Sol. (You can find the full list in the report.)
All the models were scored for correct answers to 600 questions from several benchmark sets. This included multiple-choice textbook questions, “reading comprehension” questions based on text snippets from scientific papers, and questions about how confident climate scientists are in different conclusions drawn from IPCC reports.
That last one was the most difficult, with the flagship models answering around 78 percent correctly and the smallest open-weight models coming in at around 47 percent. For the other benchmarks, frontier models and large open-weight models generally scored around 90 percent, while small open-weight models scored in the 70s.
