It’s been a big week for the stock market and the leather jacket-wearing community.
Nvidia founder Jensen Huang, who had resigned as chief executive of the chipmaker after reports of multiple affairs, was reinstated as CEO. But as he returned to the company’s Silicon Valley headquarters surrounded by clapping employees chanting his name, one thing was amiss. Jensen was back, but the black leather jacket that had been his trademark piece for nearly two decades remained absent from its former power position. In its place? A navy cotton jacket.
In his first post-return company all-hands meeting, Huang apologised to employees, telling them he had made some “terrible mistakes”. He also said: “I’m going to be a better CEO the second time around … I take this responsibility with the greatest humility and renewed commitment.”
What better than a humble cotton jacket to emphasise Huang’s newfound morality? A leather jacket comes with in-built bravado. It walks a fine line between confident and cocky, whereas its cotton cousin is much more subtle. It suggests modesty and restraint.
The internet was quick to weigh in.
“I changed my jacket. I’m different now,” read one comment below a photo of a leather-less Huang. “Someone in fashion needs to make ‘The Jensen’ jacket right now,” read another.
The Cut described it as his “listening and learning jacket”. Vogue posited it was his way of saying “I’m sorry.”
Johnny Davis, the luxury director of Esquire magazine, wasn’t surprised to see Huang switch up his look. “A black leather jacket is not really the thing to be taking a victory lap in,” he says. Instead, Huang needed a garment “to signal some degree of contrition”.
Huang’s zipped deep blue gabardine jacket brings to mind the popular chore jacket, which is rooted in workwear. While in the leather jacket club you’ll find everyone from Marlon Brando to Brad Pitt, chore-jacket fans include the likes of Monty Don. A leather jacket creates drama. A cotton jacket is very much “doing the work” in therapy speak.
Huang’s new jacket is less flashy than his Tom Ford leather numbers, but it is still a designer jacket. It appears to be Dior’s £2,400 “zipped shirt” – not cheap but Huang is worth over £100bn. He previously wore it on a visit to Seoul in June where he paired it with white jeans. This time, he teamed it with black jeans and slip-on Hermès shoes.
Andrew Groves, director of the menswear archive at the University of Westminster, suggests Huang has attempted to “materially soften his image” with his new look.
“Leather carries associations of protection and rebellion,” Groves says. “Cotton feels more everyday and approachable. That shift registers before anyone notices it’s actually from Dior.”
Davis describes the cotton jacket as somewhere between a Harrington and a workwear jacket in “a very respectable colour” which brings to mind school uniforms and corporate dress. It says: “I’m getting back to work, but I’m still not a suit.”
With Huang facing two congressional hearings next week, we may be waiting a while before we see him back in his leathers. And with a collection of up to 40 variants, it’s unlikely his leather-jacket era is permanently over. Perhaps, like Colette Kress, who served as Nvidia’s interim chief executive for just 11 days, the cotton jacket is very much just in a temporary position, too.
