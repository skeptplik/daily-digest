Japan is expected to say in revisions to its security-related documents by the year-end that it will “begin considering and reach a conclusion” on the controversial issue of whether to acquire nuclear-powered submarines, sources have said.
The government will finalize the language in the documents after examining proposals by an expert panel and the outcome of discussions with the ruling Liberal Democratic Party, the sources said Friday.
Nuclear-powered submarines can stay underwater longer and travel faster than diesel-powered ones. They can be equipped with vertical launch systems for long-range missiles and are thus expected to be used to provide a counterstrike capability, or the ability to strike enemy bases.
Nuclear submarines “would allow crew members to engage in operations at a higher level of safety,” Defense Minister Shinjiro Koizumi has said.
However, the costs of building nuclear submarines are said to reach trillions of yen. Another challenge would be recruiting and training engineers and crew with specialized knowledge of the vessels.
Furthermore, some question whether the introduction of nuclear-powered submarines is compatible with the Atomic Energy Basic Act, which limits nuclear power research and use to peaceful goals.
Within the government and the LDP, support for the introduction is rising amid an increasingly severe security environment surrounding Japan.
The LDP’s ruling coalition partner, the Japan Innovation Party, has called for acquiring the vessels, with officials pointing to China’s growing capabilities in far-off waters.
China is expanding its military activities, including by deploying aircraft carriers in the western Pacific and building an all-nuclear submarine fleet, while Russia and North Korea continue to strengthen their military capabilities.
To counter these moves, Australia and South Korea have decided to acquire nuclear-powered submarines.
The expert panel will finalize its proposals at its final meeting as early as Dec. 7. An early draft says that the government “should make a decision on whether to introduce or develop (nuclear submarines) from a comprehensive viewpoint,” including the feasibility of such an endeavor.
Japan currently operates a fleet of 23 diesel-electric attack submarines, but has long avoided the more expensive and politically fraught option of nuclear-powered vessels.
