Chinese telecommunications equipment maker Huawei was convicted on Tuesday in a sprawling US criminal case on charges that it stole trade secrets from US companies and deceived banks about its business in Iran.
Huawei, the world’s leading telecom equipment manufacturer, was found guilty by a federal jury in Brooklyn of 10 of the 12 criminal counts it faced, including bank fraud, conspiracy to steal trade secrets and conspiracy to defraud the US. It was found not guilty of racketeering conspiracy, a charge alleging it had acted as a criminal enterprise.
US authorities had targeted Huawei for years, saying the company posed national security risks. It has previously urged countries not to use Huawei infrastructure for the rollout of new 5G mobile networks.
The US attorney general, Todd Blanche, said in a statement after the verdict: “No person or company is above the law”, adding: “For decades, Huawei and three of its subsidiaries conducted their business in the United States on the basis of lies and theft.”
The criminal case began in 2018, and the following year the US government placed Huawei on a trade blacklist that restricted its access to US technology.
Experts have said the case has nonetheless not undermined Huawei’s business, as the company diversified and emerged as ‌a key player in China’s push for artificial ‌intelligence. The company has more than 200,000 employees and operates in approximately 170 countries.
The trial lasted more than two months, with prosecutors calling more than 60 witnesses and the defense calling 15.
In a statement, Huawei said the charges on which it was convicted lack legal merit and said it would “take all necessary legal actions to defend our rights and interests”.
The case emerged in December 2018 when Meng Wanzhou, Huawei’s chief financial officer and daughter of the company’s founder, was detained in Canada on a US warrant, prompting a diplomatic crisis involving the US, China and Canada.
Meng had been secretly indicted on charges of misleading an HSBC ‌banker about Huawei’s business in Iran, so the company could move dollars in and out of Iran in violation of US sanctions.
She fought extradition to the US for nearly three years, before entering a deferred prosecution agreement and flying home to China to a hero’s welcome. The charges against her were dropped.
The 2018 indictment alleged that Huawei misled HSBC, Standard Chartered and BNP Paribas about its business dealings in Iran.
Prosecutors added new charges in 2020, including that Huawei conspired to steal trade secrets from US technology companies over two decades.
During the trial, prosecutors tried to prove Huawei stole source code from Cisco Systems for its routers, stole wireless telecommunications company Quintel’s antenna technology, and hired a professor to gain access to a circuit board developed by the semiconductor company CNEX.
Jurors watched a video that, according to prosecutors, showed a Huawei employee slipping a piece of a T-Mobile testing robot into his backpack, and heard about a Huawei engineer sneaking into a Fujitsu booth at a trade show to photograph a device.
Prosecutors gave jurors a statement attached to Meng’s deferred prosecution agreement, in which she acknowledged that the company illegally conducted business in Iran. She ‌did not testify at trial, though an HSBC banker she met with in 2013 did testify.
The defense argued that HSBC and the other banks knew about Huawei’s business in Iran and were competing for Huawei’s business. Brian Heberlig, a lawyer for Huawei, accused prosecutors during his closing argument of having “cherrypicked” a handful of disparate incidents to support their claim of a 20-year conspiracy.
He said Huawei succeeded through “hard work, innovation, and investment in research and development”.
