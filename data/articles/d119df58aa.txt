Between the salmon sperm facials, the skin boosters, and the next generation of lasers, there’s plenty to pique the interest of the beauty obsessive in South Korea. So much so that vacations are increasingly being organized around visits to the dermatologist’s office. But how much is too much when it comes to our beauty itineraries? Just ask Khloé and Kim Kardashian.
On Wednesday’s episode of her podcast, Khloé in Wonder Land, Koko and her close friend La La Anthony reminisced about their August 2025 trip to South Korea, where they enthusiastically availed themselves of the country’s advanced cosmetic treatments—with some less-than-desirable results.
“Kim and I came back deformed, you came back beautiful and the plane ride just made it so much worse,” Kardashian told Anthony, recalling how their post-treatment appearances had kept them holed up in their hotel room.
The Kardashians are, of course, well-versed in the world of aesthetics. Over the years, the sisters have been particularly candid about their experiments with everything from the vampire facial to regenerative skin treatments. But even for those with the world’s most sought-after dermatologists on speed dial, things don’t always go to plan.
While it’s unclear precisely which procedures the sisters underwent—photos shared on Kim’s Instagram at the time showed her receiving facial treatments and an IV infusion—their experience raises some pertinent questions. How much research should we be doing before traveling abroad for cosmetic treatments? How many procedures can we feasibly—and safely—fit into a vacation? And how do we know when something has actually gone wrong?
The lure of a beauty vacation is understandable. South Korea, in particular, is at the forefront of aesthetic innovation, with technologies and treatments that aren’t yet widely available in the United States. And as our collective beauty literacy has expanded, so too has our appetite for more technologically sophisticated treatments. A facial no longer necessarily means an hour of extractions and massage. But there is a distinction to be made between a beauty treatment and a medical procedure. Just because something is marketed as minimally invasive, or requires no incisions, doesn’t mean it is without risk. Nor does its ubiquity on TikTok necessarily speak to its suitability for your own skin.
“Traveling for treatment isn't the same as having unsafe treatment,” says consultant dermatologist Dr. Sidra Khan. “What travel adds is complexity: a different regulatory system, possibly a language barrier, often a compressed consultation, and being thousands of miles from your practitioner during recovery.”
“A beautiful clinic, an impressive Instagram following and a gallery of celebrity clients don’t really tell you much about the practitioner’s medical credentials or what results you might expect,” adds cosmetic surgeon and aesthetics specialist Dr. Paul Banwell. Independently verify your practitioner’s qualifications and registration with the relevant medical regulator, and establish whether the clinic is equipped to manage complications. Khan recommends arranging a virtual consultation with the treating doctor before booking anything, and asking about their experience with your particular skin tone, the products and devices they intend to use, and why. “A holiday itinerary shouldn't replace informed consent.”
For London-based aesthetics and NHS doctor Dr. Alicia Gonzalez-Fernandez, the marketing of medical procedures as vacation packages is itself a red flag. “On a handful of occasions, I have encountered patients that have come into A&E straight from the airport,” she says of her experiences working in emergency medicine. She cautions against practitioners who pressure patients into additional procedures or fail to adequately discuss complications and aftercare. “We are not in the business of making people self-conscious about things that are not a problem for them.”
Then there’s the phenomenon of “treatment stacking.” While certain procedures can complement one another, squeezing several into a short period of time can increase the likelihood of complications. “Many of these treatments work by creating a controlled injury that the skin then repairs, and doing several close together doesn't give it time to recover,” explains aesthetics doctor Dr. Priya Verma. “That raises the risk of prolonged inflammation, infection and pigmentation changes, particularly in darker skin tones.”
Multiple procedures can also make it harder to establish which treatment is responsible for an adverse reaction, notes aesthetics doctor Dr. Ahmed El Muntasar. “Ultimately, I’d much rather a patient’s treatment schedule was dictated by how their skin is recovering than by the date of their flight home.”
Consider, too, how conducive the rest of your vacation itinerary is to your skin’s recovery. As Verma notes, the low humidity in airplane cabins can exacerbate dryness and sensitivity in already compromised skin, while sun exposure can increase the risk of post-inflammatory hyperpigmentation. There is no universal rule about how soon you can fly after a non-surgical cosmetic procedure, says Khan, but you should allow ample time for recovery and an in-person review before your departure. (With surgical procedures, flying also carries an increased risk of blood clots, making a conversation with your surgeon about your travel plans particularly important.)
There's also the question of downtime. An unfortunate-looking reflection in the hotel bathroom mirror doesn’t necessarily mean a treatment has been “botched.” Verma makes an important distinction: “Downtime is the expected redness, swelling or flaking that settles over a few days. Dissatisfaction is when the skin has healed but you're not happy with the result. A complication is a medical problem that needs treating.”
Knowing what to expect—and for how long—is something to establish with your practitioner well before the treatment itself. The trajectory of your recovery is key: Increasing pain, spreading redness, heat, discharge, or fever warrant prompt medical attention.
There are, however, symptoms that warrant immediate medical attention. Following dermal filler injections, for example, the FDA advises seeking immediate medical care for unusual pain, changes to your vision, white, gray, or blue discoloration around the injection site, or signs of a stroke. These may indicate that the blood supply has been compromised.
“If there are red-flag symptoms, don't just get on a plane because you'd rather deal with it at home,” stresses Khan. “Some complications are time-critical, so get seen locally.”
In non-emergency situations, contact your original practitioner for an in-person review and document your recovery with photographs taken in consistent lighting. El Muntasar recommends obtaining your full treatment records, including the names, quantities, and batch numbers of any injectables, or the device and settings used for laser treatments. Seek an independent medical opinion from someone experienced in managing complications if you remain concerned.
As distressing as it can be to feel unhappy with your appearance, resist the temptation to immediately book another aesthetic procedure. “Swelling can make certain areas appear overfilled or disproportionate, and adding more filler to try to balance things out can leave a patient with an entirely different problem once that swelling subsides,” explains Banwell.
“More treatment isn’t always the antidote to treatment,” says Khan. Anti-wrinkle injections can take around two weeks to reach their full effect, filler can take several weeks to settle, and collagen remodeling following laser treatments or microneedling continues for months. The same caution applies to overzealous at-home skincare. “Your freshly lasered face is not the moment to try the acids and retinoids you picked up at duty-free.”
And while you might have meticulously mapped out your Seoul shopping itinerary, restaurant reservations, and dermatology appointments, have you given quite as much thought to what happens when you get home? Establishing a plan for follow-up care, obtaining a record of your procedures, and checking whether your insurance covers complications should all factor into the planning process. El Muntasar recommends getting your aftercare plan in writing, including details of who you can contact outside of clinic hours and who is responsible for any additional medical expenses should you need to extend your stay.
By all means, indulge your K-beauty curiosity. Just make sure you’re approaching your dermatological appointments with a little more discernment than your haul at Olive Young.
