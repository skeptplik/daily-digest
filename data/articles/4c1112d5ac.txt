In the face of planetary collapse, design education and practice need to change to show young designers that it’s possible for them to make a difference, writes Céline Semaan.
The last time I taught a design class, at the School of Visual Arts (SVA) in New York, I remember sitting in my car after the two-hour workshop feeling quite honestly distraught. How had these young designers become so convinced of their own powerlessness?
During class, we discussed the political, ecological and economic infrastructures that make design possible: mineral extraction, exploited labour and the colonial systems sustaining much of what we produce and consume.
Having assumed these conversations had become an expected part of design education, I was taken aback by how little the students cared. Instead, they were far more concerned with securing and keeping jobs in an increasingly precarious industry.
Perhaps the profession needs to reconsider its relationship with obedience
“How do you expect us to make any difference if this is how the industry works?” one asked. Another just wanted to know how to earn a living, without upsetting their employers.
These were excellent questions, particularly as designers now compete with artificial intelligence capable of executing instructions at extraordinary speed. If design is reduced to following briefs and producing outputs, what space is there for a designer to effect any structural change?
Perhaps the profession needs to reconsider its relationship with obedience. Designers are trained to solve problems, yet rarely encouraged to question who defined them, whose interests they serve, or whether they should exist at all. They are commissioned to make products more sustainable without questioning production volumes, or improve services without examining the policies that make them inaccessible.
I often say design is a series of well-informed decisions. Every object, building and service emerges from decisions about resources, labor, budgets and the conditions of life.
Designers participate in these decisions, although they rarely control all of them. So where does their power begin and end?
Imagine a design studio commissioned by a city to address extreme heat in a low-income neighborhood. The brief requests solar-powered cooling shelters and shaded public spaces, with a fixed budget and deadline.
During research, the designers discover that the proposed shelters would be too far from elderly residents most vulnerable to heat, while existing community centers could accommodate cooling facilities at a lower cost. What should they do? Follow the brief, collect their fees and pretend their hands are tied? Refuse the commission, leaving another studio to complete the work, perhaps feeling rebellious for a moment?
There is a third possibility. They could show the city why its proposal would fail to reach its intended beneficiaries and present an alternative within the same budget. Working with residents and community organizations, they could identify accessible locations, compare costs and demonstrate how the alternative better meets the city’s objectives.
The difficult part is to make these commitments part of everyday practice, rather than optional gestures dependent on a sympathetic client
Here we see a tangible expansion of design as a civic responsibility. Designers can use their expertise to bring communities into decisions that affect their lives, improving outcomes while sharing decision-making power.
And if the city insists on its original proposal? The client controls the budget. A junior designer acting alone may have little negotiating power.
But a studio can incorporate community research into its contracts before work begins, negotiate a paid investigation phase and require the client to review evidence and alternatives before approving the design. Of course, this doesn’t guarantee agreement, but it makes critical inquiry a recognised part of the commission.
Design is a practice of applied imagination: understanding interconnected systems to intentionally shape the conditions of life. In this example, designers would examine the relationships between urban planning, public health, housing, energy and community governance. Their value lies partly in identifying what the original commission overlooked and developing viable possibilities with the people affected.
Thanks to the work of people like Victor Papanek, Tony Fry, Ezio Manzini and Arturo Escobar, by now it’s well understood that design has the capacity to shape the world but remains complicit in ecological and social destruction. The challenge today is to translate that knowledge into professional authority, economic leverage and meaningful participation in the decisions that will determine our collective futures.
Likewise, advocates of participatory design have long argued for involving people affected by design decisions. The difficult part is to make these commitments part of everyday practice, rather than optional gestures dependent on a sympathetic client.
Meeting these challenges requires rethinking design education. Designers need to learn to investigate the historical conditions behind a problem, understand ecological and economic relationships, and work alongside communities to develop alternatives.
Design can shape community-owned infrastructure, food systems and forms of local governance as well as products and buildings. These are tangible fields of work requiring collaboration and shared authority.
We should not ask young designers to compensate for structural failures through personal bravery alone
Of course, clients may refuse evidence, and insecure workers cannot always risk confrontation. Already there is too much pressure on Gen Z to save the world; we should not ask young designers to compensate for structural failures through personal bravery alone.
Studios, educators and commissioners must create paid roles and decision-making processes that give designers genuine influence. What if, for example, investigating the risks and assumptions behind a brief became an essential professional service, one that designers were commissioned and compensated to provide?
I keep thinking about those students at SVA, afraid of being considered difficult. We have taught them how to execute decisions without adequately preparing them to participate in making them. If designers are to take responsibility for their role in shaping the systems that govern our lives, they need a say in the decisions that produce those systems.
In the near future, success would mean residents gaining influence over where public money goes, with designers helping turn their priorities into lasting changes in public planning. This is design as a civic responsibility, and a meaningful distinction from machines optimized to execute instructions. To design possible futures, we must grow the agency designers hold, and the collective power they can help communities to build.
Céline Semaan is a Lebanese-Canadian designer, artist, writer, educator and speaker based in New York City, whose work focuses on climate issues and social justice. She is the founder of Slow Factory, a non-profit education and research platform that teaches about regenerative design. Her writing has appeared in The Cut, Vogue, Elle, Refinery29 and New York Magazine, among others. Her first book, A Woman Is a School, was published in 2024, and her upcoming book, Designing Possible Futures, is due for publication in 2027.
