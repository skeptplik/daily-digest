I knew I’d need many skills during the opening hours of Grand Theft Auto VI, but effective time management was not one of them. From playing the previous five titles in the series (and their many spin-offs), I understood I’d have to master Rockstar’s idiosyncratic approach to car handling. True enough, the vehicles do drive like airbeds tied on top of supermarket trolleys. I also knew I’d need to get to grips with the somewhat tricky aiming and shooting if I was to commit any sort of respectable crime spree.
But for the first few hours of playing this staggeringly large and bewildering game, what concerned me most was lead character Jason’s personal routine. Each day, he needs to get up, choose an outfit, complete a workout, feed his pet snake Dolores, and perhaps do some DIY, so that when his girlfriend Lucia gets out of jail she’ll fall for him again – rather than fall through his apartment’s rotten staircase. Meanwhile, his drug-smuggling boss phones every five minutes with new parcels for Jason to pick up or drop off, and his pals Cal and Donnie get into all sorts of cocaine-related hijinks they expect Jason to extricate them from. There are also predatory neighbours to do favours for, and groceries to buy. I’ve seen so many people on social media posting about how they took several days off work to play this game, only to find themselves busier and more productive than ever. On his radio show last week, James O’Brien derided himself for completing, in Jason’s apartment, a simple DIY task that he’s put off for months in his own home. I know where he’s coming from.
Timekeeping has never been my strong point yet I’ve sorted it out for Jason. Each morning I spend as him, I get up, put on my gym outfit, lift weights until I’ve completed my daily target, drink a protein shake and then run errands. I’ve mentally linked the tasks together so I don’t forget anything. The fridge where I get my shakes is right next to Dolores’s tank, so I remember to feed her a dead mouse. (Jason keeps these in a food container, and I have no idea what I’m supposed to do when I run out.) I divide the days up, so that I do jobs for my boss first, then household chores and then I put out whatever coke-addled dumpster fire Cal and Donnie have started. Then I’m home to meet Lucia, when she eventually gets out of jail to do community service.
It also points to an important truth about video games; they are almost always about tidying up as efficiently as possible. We talk about “clearing the screen” of enemies, we tick off checklists, we “grind” levels to earn XP – in the same way that we mentally switch off at work, knowing that mindless repetitive tasks lead to a monthly pay packet. We may have this half-remembered fantasy of GTA as something utterly chaotic and unbound, but remember all the times we had to hang out with Roman in GTA IV to keep him happy? Or poor Franklin’s car-towing missions in GTA V? We even ran a frickin’ ice-cream business in GTA: Vice City.
The difference with GTA VI is that, through its visual acuity and likable characters, it has made routine pleasurable. It’s fun to browse the grocery store, it’s nice to lift weights in the morning sun, the sky all pink and orange over the sparkling sea. The world of the game is so detailed and tactile it gives us pleasing feedback whatever we try. Whether we’re blasting redneck drug dealers in the everglades, or fixing tiles to the roof, everything has its own feel, its own sounds, its own rhythm. The art of game design is about tricking us into doing things we already do, but in surprising new contexts. GTA VI is a lesson in how simple pleasure really is. Beyond the shoot-outs and the car chases, you’re tidying, sorting, counting, saving – but this time you’re doing it with another character who you love.
And that is the secret sauce, right there. I’m terrible at routines, but when I’m Jason, I want to get it right for Lucia (and vice-versa), just as I try to get it right in real life because people I love are relying on me. I like to shoot stuff, too – in GTA, I mean – but the context is different now. Lucia and I have a house by the ocean, we have amusing neighbours, we built a coffee table together. The depth of connection the game manages to create shows us how much Rockstar has grown up in the last 13 years. The designers of this game know that when it comes down to it, the most powerful motivator for completing tasks and staying alive is not really money or time; it’s who you get to spend them with.
What to play
It is so lovely how modern developers are rediscovering not just the aesthetics of the PlayStation era, with their glitchy low-res texture maps, but also its somewhat lost sub-genres and conventions. I’ve only just downloaded the indie-action RPG, Queen’s Domain, but its ruined gothic buildings, muddy, overgrown landscapes and colossal monsters wandering the place looking for a fight is already bringing back memories of adventures such as King’s Field and Legacy of Kain.
You play as Prince Beruim, cast out of his realm and washed ashore on the Isle of Neasied. From here, you engage in tense, stalking real-time battles, unlock dungeons and explore the densely detailed horrorscapes, while the synth soundtrack pounds. It’s like being in your own brutal low-budget fantasy movie, but with a deep, challenging combat system and a levelling-up structure that doesn’t feel needlessly complex or exhausting. The perfect game for wintery hibernation.

Question Block
As we edge towards December, Gemma has emailed in with a timely question:
“I need to buy my 17-year-old nephew a Christmas present that he doesn’t immediately discard. I know he plays lots of video games and is interested in computers, but I don’t have a clue about either, and his parents buy him all the accessories he needs. Can you help? If not, he’s getting a Greggs gift card.”
How about a video game book? Publishers such as Thames & Hudson, Lost in Cult, Bitmap Books and Lock Books produce lavish, fascinating books about games. I love Videogame Architecture: Building Virtual Spaces, the recently rereleased Itchy, Tasty, which is about the Resident Evil series, and The Arcade 1971–1999, an exquisite history of arcade games. There’s also Super Nintendo by our games editor, Keza MacDonald.
Or what about a cool gadget? The Trimui Brick Pro (£100) is a retro gaming handheld that will play thousands of classic games but is also highly customisable – you can even turn it into a portable synth. If you have a bit more to spend, Arcade1Up makes amazing recreations of classic arcade cabinets such as Pac-Man and Mortal Kombat for around £500. Or try the Micro:bit V2, a cool, teeny, programmable computer with an LED display and motion sensors.
Might he enjoy a physical game? Twilight Sword is an acclaimed new board game heavily inspired by Japanese role-playing video games. Or there’s a new Cyberpunk 2077 trading card game, based on the very popular sci-fi adventure.
Failing all this, there’s always Lego. We love the retro PlayStation model or the Pokémon Smart Play series, which adds electronic sounds and interactions to classic Pokémon characters. And before you ask, no, he isn’t too old for Lego. None of us are.
