A survey by more than 200 specialists found that Madeira's marine reserves are working – but some species are still in peril.
Madeira's waters sit at an ecological crossroads in the North-East Atlantic. Migrating whales, seabirds and turtles pass through, and the archipelago is home to cold-water corals and one of the world's rarest seals. Yet large stretches of the sea around the Portuguese islands have never been properly studied.
The Madeira Ocean Expedition, which ran throughout October, set out to fill those gaps. Its findings will feed into decisions on how to manage and expand Madeira's marine protected areas, as the region works towards protecting 30 per cent of its sea by 2030, with at least 10 per cent fully protected.
"We cannot protect without understanding how the biodiversity functions and works," says João Canning-Clode, director of the MARE-Madeira research centre.
A health check at sea: 500 dives in Madeira's ocean
Madeira's regional government organised the expedition with the Oceano Azul Foundation, National Geographic Pristine Seas, the regional research agency ARDITI and Oceanário de Lisboa. Teams worked from the historic tall ship Santa Maria Manuela and a fleet of smaller boats around Madeira, Porto Santo and the Desertas Islands.
"In an expedition like this, we want to take a snapshot about the health of the marine environment," says Emanuel Gonçalves, chief scientist at the Oceano Azul Foundation.
Scientists completed nearly 500 dives, lowered more than 300 baited cameras, filtered seawater for traces of DNA, mapped the shoreline with drones and sampled for microplastics.
With no research vessel of its own yet, MARE-Madeira also hired a fishing boat. "The fishermen could see for the first time live images of some of the biodiversity that they are actually fishing," Canning-Clode says. "On the other hand, the scientific community can see firsthand how difficult their life is."
Signs that Madeira's ocean protections work
Early results suggest Madeira's reserves are making a difference. Limpets – grazing sea snails harvested as a local delicacy – were bigger and more abundant inside protected areas, a sign that limits on collecting them are working, and the largest groupers were recorded there too.
"The good news is that we have the science to show us that when we fully protect the ocean, the ocean responds," Gonçalves says.
The Mediterranean monk seal is another case in point. Hunted for centuries, it had almost vanished from Madeira by the late 1980s, when only six to eight animals were thought to remain. The Desertas Islands became a nature reserve in 1990, partly to protect it. Today the species "is a beacon of hope", Gonçalves says. "The population is increasing in numbers. Now that increase is very slow because they are very slow breeders."
The expedition also achieved the first-ever satellite tagging of a Blainville's beaked whale in the North-East Atlantic. Early tracking data show the animal favouring waters south of the Desertas, close to the fully protected area.
But one finding stands out for the wrong reason. Fewer than 10 sharks were recorded across the entire expedition. The organisers call this scarcity concerning and in line with wider worries about sharks in the Atlantic.
Into the deep: The difficulties of exploring Madeira's ocean
By area, Madeira is mostly deep sea. "It's an ocean state, a deep ocean state," says Diva Amon, a marine scientist on the Oceano Azul Foundation's board. "And the deep sea is a very difficult place to study."
Drift cameras developed by researchers in the Azores and lowered from a fishing boat reached 1,060 metres. They revealed sponge fields and large black corals that may be several hundred years old – alongside lost fishing lines and a glass bottle.
Closer to the surface, the habitat-mapping team's highlight was a dense black coral garden off Porto Santo, known as Ariel's Garden after the team's pink underwater robot. "Our torpedo is pink, so by majority vote it's called Ariel, after the little mermaid from the Disney film," says João Monteiro, a researcher at MARE-Madeira and the University of Madeira.
A second expedition in 2027 will explore the deepest waters. Amon hopes it will reveal "places that are as iconic as the Grand Canyon or the Okavango Delta".
For Paul Rose, head of expeditions at National Geographic Pristine Seas, Madeira's location matters. "People think about ocean protection, they think about the Pacific... It all seems a bit remote," he says. "But if they see huge, beautiful, functioning, proven marine protected areas in Europe, it's closer to home."
Just over 10 per cent of the global ocean now lies within protected areas, according to IUCN and UNEP-WCMC – a third of the way to the 2030 goal. "We've really got to protect 30 per cent or we're in big trouble," Rose says.
