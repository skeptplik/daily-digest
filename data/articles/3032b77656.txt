Many fields of science have struggled under the second Trump administration, with funding cuts and delays plaguing research in fields such as public health and climate science. But some topics have found firmer footing, including artificial intelligence and quantum science.
These technologies, along with supercomputing, “are going to be the three legs of the stool that is going to transform how science and engineering is done,” says Darío Gil, undersecretary for science at the U.S. Department of Energy.
In November 2025, President Donald Trump issued an executive order to launch the Genesis Mission, a $5 billion whole-of-government effort to advance science via artificial intelligence. The initiative began with the DOE but has since expanded to more than 15 federal agencies.
In June, the president issued an executive order that aims to accelerate the development of tools such as quantum computers, quantum sensors and quantum networks. And in July, the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy issued a 123-page report describing its vision for a “golden age” of federal science, in which agencies align investments in research and development with administration priorities.
Federal agencies spent some $4.2 billion on AI R&D in 2025, according to an arm of the White House Office of Science and Technology Policy that quantifies such investments.
Many scientists are enthusiastic about the opportunities for AI and quantum research. But some caution that a measured approach is needed. “My strongest opinion … is that I want to see all fields of science supported,” says Brad Marston, a physicist at Brown University and president of the American Physical Society. “Priorities have to be chosen, but getting a good balance is important, because you never know where some advance will come from.”
Through Genesis, the DOE plans to use its troves of data as well as experimental and computing facilities to produce a common set of AI tools and a shared ecosystem for science. “That’s conceptually a very powerful idea,” says astronomer Rachel Mandelbaum of Carnegie Mellon University in Pittsburgh. “And if it pans out as intended, it could drive great advances in science and technology.”
This year, DOE funded nearly 300 projects through Genesis — including one led by Mandelbaum. Her team’s effort aims to unify immense datasets from astronomical facilities around the world, such as the Vera C. Rubin Observatory, the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument and the Zwicky Transient Facility. That would enable AI models to be trained on the data, which could help uncover hidden astronomical insights.
Other projects include efforts to accelerate nuclear reactor development, create simulations of the U.S. electric grid and study nuclear fusion. And federal agencies including the National Science Foundation and the National Institutes of Health have launched their own Genesis efforts, centered around the mission’s National Science and Technology Challenges, which include topics in health, biology, energy, space, national security and more.
Many scientists were already wielding AI in their work. But Genesis “really did create an inflection point,” says John Sarrao, director of SLAC National Accelerator Laboratory in Menlo Park, Calif. SLAC is now involved with 20 Genesis projects and leads two.
Compared with AI, quantum technologies are less mature. With quantum computing, “we’re still at mile one or two or three of a marathon,” says Celia Merzbacher, executive director of the Quantum Economic Development Consortium, a coalition of public and private institutions that works to bolster quantum tech in the United States. Nevertheless, researchers hope the two technologies can amplify one another. AI could help design and develop quantum computers, for example, while quantum computing shows promise for efficiently tackling certain machine learning problems.
Accordingly, several Genesis projects involve quantum error correction, quantum sensors and quantum materials. And the DOE’s Quantum Genesis initiative aims to develop scientifically useful, error-corrected quantum computers by 2028.
AI and quantum were not always hot topics. “Both of those areas … were kind of backwaters until recent advances,” Marston says. Before the development of deep neural networks, for example, AI was mostly a curiosity. “It points to the importance of having a balanced approach to science funding,” he says. What starts as an obscure scientific specialty may suddenly explode into wide relevance.
In the context of limited resources, prioritizing one area of research necessitates deprioritizing another. While some funding for the DOE’s portion of Genesis came from the One Big Beautiful Bill passed in 2025, much of the rest came from the DOE’s budget.
As excited as Mandelbaum is about Genesis, she is concerned about funding for non-AI related work. A cosmology grant her group received from the DOE didn’t align with Genesis mission priorities and was reduced this year, she says. “If the reality is that some of this [funding] is coming out of the baseline research budget in my field, then I think that it would be beneficial for the DOE to engage the scientific community about those trade-offs,” Mandelbaum says.
In response to these concerns, Gil says that the money going to Genesis from the DOE’s Office of Science — about $400 million so far — is small compared to the office’s total FY2026 budget of $8.4 billion.
Sarrao says that it’s important to build on the momentum of Genesis while still supporting the DOE’s core research. His impression is that things are trending in the right direction. “Are there really exciting opportunities? Yes, 100 percent,” Sarrao says. “Are there risks if you get the balance wrong? Equally well, 100 percent.”
