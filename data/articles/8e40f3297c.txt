Ahead of Spain's snap parliamentary elections on Sunday, allegations of electoral fraud are spreading widely on social media. From claims that undocumented migrants are being illegally registered to vote to accusations that postal ballots and vote counts could be manipulated, internet users are accusing Pedro Sánchez's government of attempting to rig the election. But these allegations misrepresent how Spain's electoral system works.
One word has repeatedly cropped up on Spanish social media in the run-up to Sunday's vote: pucherazo, a Spanish term for election rigging.
Internet users have accused outgoing Prime Minister Pedro Sánchez's Socialist government of attempting to manipulate the outcome of the elections to stay in power, with one of the most widespread narratives concerning immigration.
Undocumented migrants being illegally registered to vote?
A social media post that has garnered hundreds of thousands of views claims that residents of Alcorcón, a municipality on the outskirts of Madrid, discovered that strangers had been "fraudulently registered" as living at their addresses.
According to an article published by La Gaceta, these individuals were allegedly undocumented Moroccan migrants. The media outlet is owned by a foundation chaired by Santiago Abascal, the leader of the far-right Vox party.
The article claimed that the registrations were part of a ploy by the Socialists to stay in power, alleging that dozens of residents had discovered the deception. The claims were widely shared on Facebook and TikTok.
However, Alcorcón's municipal authorities denied allegations of mass fraudulent registrations. Despite referring to dozens of residents, the article cited only two specific cases, both of which local authorities said had since been resolved.
More importantly, the allegations overlook a fundamental distinction between being registered as a resident in Spain and being registered as a voter. Being listed on Spain's municipal register, known as the padrón municipal, does not automatically confer voting rights. Spanish nationality is required to vote in parliamentary elections.
Therefore, even if an undocumented migrant were fraudulently registered at somebody else's address, this would not entitle them to vote in Sunday's election.
Postal voting targeted by fraud allegations
Another major target of suspicion is Spain's postal voting system, with social media users suggesting it could be used to manipulate the election results. Even Vox leader Santiago Abascal has urged Spaniards not to vote by post, fuelling the mistrust.
However, Spain has several safeguards in place to prevent postal voting fraud, as experts confirmed to news agency AFP.
Voters are required to verify their identity twice: first when applying to vote by post and again when submitting the envelope containing their ballot.
These envelopes are then securely stored by the postal service until they are delivered to the relevant polling stations on election day.
Does Indra control the counting of votes?
Other allegations circulating online concern Indra, a Spanish technology company partly owned by the Spanish state that plays a role in the electoral process.
Social media users have suggested that the government's stake in the company could enable Sánchez to manipulate the vote count. However, these allegations misrepresent the company's responsibilities.
Indra does not count ballots. In Spain, votes are counted manually at individual polling stations, in a public process that can be observed by representatives of political parties.
Indra's role is to process and communicate the preliminary results on election night. The official results are subsequently verified by electoral authorities.
The company is therefore responsible for communicating the figures, rather than determining them.
While none of these viral allegations provides evidence of a rigged election, their circulation is fuelling distrust in Spain's electoral process before polling stations have even opened.
