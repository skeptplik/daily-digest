With a new computing power centre, international collaborations and a robotics lab, the small South Caucasus country seeks to turn its technological talent into greater economic influence.
The ancient Silk Road carried goods, knowledge and ideas between East and West. In Armenia, tech industry leaders are re-imagining it in the modern day: a network linking countries through the infrastructure needed to develop artificial intelligence.
"What we are starting to rebuild is the ancient Silk Road," said Rev Lebaredian, vice president of NVIDIA responsible for physical AI simulation, speaking at the DigiTec Technology Conference in Yerevan. "It is coming back, but in this new form, as a kind of AI Silk Road," he said.
He argued that Armenia and Kazakhstan can become its main pillars, with countries in the region contributing at different levels of the AI economy: energy, microprocessors, computing infrastructure, models and applications.
The vision goes beyond constructing data centres. It is about connecting markets, researchers, businesses and capital across borders, and creating a role for smaller economies in an industry dominated by much larger powers.
"We must work hard to create as much interdependence between us as possible," Lebaredian said. "That will help everyone’s prosperity and strengthen our security."
The emerging corridor is starting to take physical shape.
Firebird, a US-based AI infrastructure company with Armenian co-founders, opened a computing centre in Armenia in August and is building another one in Kazakhstan.
With more than 6,100 NVIDIA Blackwell processors, the Armenian facility provides the computing power businesses need to train and run artificial intelligence systems. NVIDIA itself is among its clients, while Perplexity also collaborates with it, linking the small country with international AI players.
A small country looking beyond its borders
This ambition runs through the DigiTec Conference, held from 20 to 22 November at Yerevan’s Meridian Expo Center. The organisers announced 125 speakers, including representatives of NVIDIA, OpenAI, Dell Technologies and Mozilla, bringing international technology companies together with governments and entrepreneurs from the region.
For Armenia’s technology community, the event is also a space to showcase the position the country wants to secure.
Armen Kocharyan, president of the Union of Advanced Technology Enterprises UATE, which organises DigiTec, described it as a "live platform" that connects governments, investors and businesses from Europe, the Gulf, Central Asia and North America".
"A real hub never stops at borders. [...] We promote all five layers of artificial intelligence together, connecting people, technology, capital and markets in real time," Kocharyan said.
"We are creating a live platform" that brings together governments, investors and businesses from Europe, the Gulf, Central Asia and North America so they can develop technology, invest and expand their activities."
For a country of around three million people, these connections are essential. Armenia’s domestic market cannot by itself support the growth of an AI industry.
The opportunity lies in specialisation and cooperation with neighbours and partners, members of the Armenian technology community stress.
7% of national GDP
The political interest is also clear: the DigiTec programme includes a discussion between Armenian Prime Minister Nikol Pashinyan and Nvidia's Lebaredian on turning the vision of an AI hub into reality.
The country is building on an already developed technology sector.
Arto Minasyan, co-founder of AI companies Krisp and 10Web and an investor in new technology ventures, said the sector makes up 7% of the Armenian economy, and described an ecosystem with its own unicorns, investment funds and research labs.
"The dream of building a technologically advanced Armenia pushed me to found the company and move from academic research into entrepreneurship," said Minasyan.
"It pushed my co-founder David to come back from Silicon Valley and start the company. It pushed many researchers from leading universities to come to Armenia and join forces to build here," he added.
This international network is a key element of the strategy. The Armenian diaspora provides connections through which know-how, information and business opportunities can flow.
The advice to build a supercomputer
The first tentative steps towards attracting Nvidia to Armenia also had a personal motivation. Lebaredian described how, after he set up Nvidia's office in Moscow in 2004, he began dreaming of something similar for Armenia, waiting for the right conditions to emerge.
The Armenian office opened in 2022. Today it employs more than 100 people, he says.
But attracting an international technology company was only one part of the ambition. The bigger question was how Armenian researchers and entrepreneurs would get the tools needed to develop technology themselves.
Lebaredian recounted a meeting at Nvidia's California headquarters in 2019, when Pashinyan asked the company’s co-founder Jensen Huang what Armenia should do to attract a company like Nvidia.
According to Lebaredian, the answer was to build an AI supercomputer for its people.
He said that initial efforts to secure state funding were halted by the pandemic and the war. The commercial project later took shape, as partners, capital and clients came together, with DigiTec 2024 serving as a meeting point.
This journey matters, because Armenia’s proposal relies on more than access to imported chips. The country has a tradition in computing dating back to the Soviet era, as well as expertise in semiconductor design and in the software used to develop them.
Armenia’s Minister of High-Tech Industry and Artificial Intelligence, David Tadevosyan, focused on the same challenge: how to turn successes in software and technology services into domestic research, engineering and production of internationally competitive products.
He highlighted human capital, modern infrastructure and cooperation between science and business as the necessary conditions.
The minister said the government had approved, around two weeks before DigiTec, an educational programme in physical artificial intelligence, involving international-level experts.
The aim, he explained, is for technological development to boost the productivity and competitiveness of the entire economy.
Firebird: The "AI factory" and expansion into Kazakhstan
Firebird’s facility in Hrazdan is the most visible expression of this strategy. An AI factory provides the computing power businesses need to train and run artificial intelligence systems, without having to build their own data centres.
Speaking to Euronews, Firebird co-founder and CEO Razmig Hovaghimian described a project that from the outset was driven by an ambition to go beyond the country’s borders.
"Why build it only in Armenia? Why not build it with Armenia as a starting point, expand it globally and attract talent and investment here?" he said.
An Armenian originally from Egypt with experience in creating and selling tech businesses, he saw in AI an opportunity to tap the country’s human capital.
Implementing it, however, required more than good engineers: funding, licences to export US technology and investors’ trust in a market they were not used to associating with such large-scale infrastructure.
"We knew we had the talent. We just had to convince the rest of the world that this is the right place to build and grow," he said.
According to Hovaghimian, the first phase has around 6,200 GPUs, while demand was for about 25,000.
"Everyone was saying that perhaps we would have one customer who would take all the capacity. We have 15," he said, adding that 12 of them are Armenian companies.
The customers he named include Nvidia and Perplexity, as well as Krisp and Activeloop. The presence of domestic firms shows that the facility can also serve as infrastructure for the local ecosystem, beyond serving international clients.
The second phase is already under construction, he said, with the aim of bringing the infrastructure in Armenia to around 70,000 GPUs in 2027.
"Today we are at 6,200. We are already building the second phase. Next year we will be at around 70,000," he told Euronews.
The expansion into Kazakhstan fits the same logic: developing infrastructure in markets that have talent and business potential but need access to AI computing power.
Hovaghimian put Firebird’s broader international plan at around two to two and a half gigawatts, with additional markets under exploration.
Firebird’s ambition, he stressed, is to give more countries the ability to develop their own technology.
"Not simply to become passive consumers of AI, but to take an active part in creating it."
An entire country as an AI laboratory
For Lebaredian, computing infrastructure is the starting point for the next step, "physical artificial intelligence" that involves robots, autonomous vehicles and systems that operate in the real world after being trained and tested in simulations.
He sees applications from agriculture and manufacturing to health and transport, sectors that account for a large part of the global economy.
In Armenia, a new robotics and physical intelligence lab links universities, the state and businesses to develop researchers and practical applications.
"To turn the whole country into our laboratory and our industries into a lab," he proposed, referring to mines, agriculture and high-tech production such as drones.
In his view, Armenia’s small size can make cooperation easier and speed up the move from research to deployment.
That is where the success of the new Silk Road will be decided: in the country’s ability to turn computing power into home-grown businesses and technologies.
