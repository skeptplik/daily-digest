A long-running class action lawsuit alleging that Sony was engaged in monopolistic behaviour and unfair price-fixing on PlayStation games has been dismissed by the UK competition authorities, following a trial earlier this year.
The case, which was brought in 2022 by consumer campaigner Alex Neill, alleged that Sony was engaged in exclusionary behaviour by creating a "closed ecosystem" that rendered digital users an "entirely captive class", enabling it to enact “excessive and unfair” charges on downloads from the PlayStation Store. It claimed that digital game content would have been approximately 20% cheaper if Sony's practices were not in place.
The claim covered 12.2 million users and said they were in line for a payment of £162 each, with a total cost of around £2 billion ($2.6 billion).
The claim was dismissed in its entirety by the Competition Appeal Tribunal in a judgement issued today, which found that consumers had sufficient alternatives to Sony's digital store in the form of physical copies - which the firm recently announced would be discontinued - and alternative console platforms.
The tribunal also concluded that Sony's ecosystem had not been created as an open platform, and to make it capable of supporting third-party stores would require "significant cost and effort" that would likely result in reduced security and performance.
The judgement held that "there was strong evidence of a willingness of consumers to switch consoles following an increase in games prices," citing Sony research which "forecast commercial harm to Sony if it allowed PlayStation game prices to be uncompetitive with Xbox."
It said that the claim's assertion that players were trapped in a closed ecosystem was "not credible as it was based almost entirely on theoretical assumptions, which did not hold good in important respects in the real world. The contention that the competitive price would be 20% lower than the actual retail price was not made out."
The tribunal had previously found against Apple in a similar case in 2025, judging that the firm had abused its dominant position by forcing users through the App Store and its 30% commission rate. Apple has subsequently relaxed its policies to enable alternative web stores, which has driven a boom in third-party stores from providers like Xsolla and AppCharge.
