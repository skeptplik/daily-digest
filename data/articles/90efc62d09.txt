A 24-hour strike by resident doctors in Northern Ireland is due to end at 07:00 GMT on Saturday.
But consultants and specialist doctors will begin a separate 24-hour strike on Monday, as part of the same pay dispute.
The British Medical Association (BMA) rejected a recommended 3.5% pay rise and wants to restore the value of doctors' pay to 2008 levels.
Health Minister, Robbie Butler, said he had accepted the recommended rise but could not implement it without sufficient funding for his department.
Friday's strike action by resident doctors affected non-emergency services, resulting in the cancellation of many planned outpatient appointments.
Urgent surgery and most GP services continued as normal because senior doctors remained on duty.
How will patients be affected on Monday?
Monday's walkout will involve consultants and specialty, associate specialist and specialist grade (SAS) doctors and will also take place over 24 hours from 07:00 on Monday.
Routine hospital and elective services are expected to be cancelled during the second strike.
But emergency care will continue under Christmas Day cover - which means full emergency cover will remain in place.
The Western and South Eastern health trusts have asked patients with appointments on Monday to attend as planned, unless their trust tells them otherwise.
The South Eastern Trust said the urgent care centre at Lagan Valley Hospital would be closed on Monday and that postponed appointments and procedures would be rescheduled as soon as possible.
Butler said more than 2,200 appointments were cancelled during similar strikes in June and warned the latest action would have "real consequences for patients, waiting lists and service delivery".
Why are doctors striking?
The BMA says years of below-inflation pay rises have made it harder for Northern Ireland's health service to recruit and retain doctors.
Dr Seamus Crumley, an anaesthetics registrar who works in intensive care at the Ulster Hospital, joined Friday's picket line in Belfast.
He told BBC News NI he had taken a pay cut when he moved from Scotland last year, despite securing a more senior role.
"The medical workforce here is very, very vulnerable to losing doctors to literally anywhere else in the UK, but also to the Republic of Ireland," he said.
Dr Steven Montgomery, who chairs the BMA's Northern Ireland Resident Doctors Committee, said his colleagues were facing burnout and emotional distress because of pressure at work.
He said the union was "not looking for this all to be paid in one lump sum" and wanted the minister to set out a plan to restore pay over time.
Dr OisÃn Fearon, a resident doctor in Belfast, told BBC News NI he had considered moving to Australia, but family and local ties had kept him in Northern Ireland.
He said he had begun the process of registering to train in the Republic of Ireland and was keeping that option open.
What has the health minister said?
Butler said he had met the BMA to explain the financial pressures facing his department, external and intended to implement the recommended award as soon as possible.
He said cumulative pay rises over the previous three years averaged 30% for resident doctors, 25% for SAS doctors and 24% for consultants.
Butler said the recommended rise was higher than that for other healthcare staff and warned that going beyond 3.5% would have "significant repercussions for nurses, teachers, police officers and indeed the entire public sector workforce".
Separately, the Northern Ireland Public Service Alliance (Nipsa) released the results of a recent ballot of its health and social care members.
It said 94% of its health and social care members backed strike action, while 97% backed action short of a strike, over growing pressures on the health and social care sector.
The union said it would consider its next steps and warned that coordinated industrial action could spread across the health service in the coming weeks.
