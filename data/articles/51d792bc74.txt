As It Happens
She says Russell Brand sexually assaulted her when she was 16. She's waiving her anonymity to speak out
Mia Latter is calling for changes to the U.K.'s age-of-consent laws
WARNING: This article contains details of abuse. It may affect those who have experienced sexual violence or know someone affected by it.
Mia Latter has decided to waive her legal right to anonymity and speak publicly about her teenage relationship with disgraced actor Russell Brand.
The 37-year-old British woman says Brand sexually assaulted her while they were dating when she was 16 and he was 30.
Victims of alleged sexual offences in the U.K. are afforded lifelong anonymity. But in the wake of Brand's high-profile rape trial, Latter says she felt compelled to go on record to advocate for changes to the country's age-of-consent laws.
"That would never have happened if my parents had been able to stop the relationship dead in its tracks to begin with," Latter told As It Happens host Nil Köksal. "They had nowhere to go. The police wouldn't have been able to help them."
In the U.K., people who are 16 and older can consent to sexual activity with an adult of any age who is not in a position of authority over them.
Latter is working with the Close the Consent Gap campaign, which advocates a "staggered" age of consent in which it would be an offence for a person over 20 to engage in sexual activity with a person aged 16–17, unless the age difference is no more than three years.
Brand, 51, was convicted this month on one count of rape and three counts of sexual assault and sentenced to 10-and-a-half years in prison. He continues to deny all allegations against him.
Latter's allegations, which were not part of the trial, are under police investigation.
'I told him my age'
Latter, who now lives in California, says she was shopping at Leicester Square in London when she first met Brand. She recognized him from his appearances on MTV.
"I told him my age before we ever went on a date, and he didn't have a problem with it for one moment," she said.
"My mum went to Brand and asked him not to see me. And she wanted to look him in the eye and talk to him about this. And he just was quite defiant and looked at her and just said, 'And what?' Because he knew there was nothing she could do."
Brand has been convicted of rape and sexual assault. He's maintained his innocence behind bars. (Leon Neal/Getty Images)
The U.K.'s Ministry of Justice says the Law Commission has been tasked with reviewing the country's consent laws.
“Adults abusing imbalances of power, influence or vulnerability to exploit young people is unacceptable and we will always look at what more can be done to protect young people," a ministry spokesperson said.
"Our immediate focus remains on tackling exploitation, supporting victims and ensuring perpetrators are brought to justice."
In Canada, the age of consent is 16, but a 16- or 17-year-old cannot consent to sexual activity if they are dependent on their partner, their partner is in a position of power over them or the relationship is deemed "exploitative."
'Love bombing'
When Latter and Brand first started seeing each other, she says he was very romantic.
"Lots of poetry and, you know, over-the-top declarations of his intentions and how special I was and all of this stuff," she said.
She says she now recognizes this as "love bombing," an abuse tactic that Western University's Learning Network for gender-based violence defines as "a way of exercising control through overwhelming displays of affection, gifts, and praise."
They dated for three months and toward the end of that period, she says he sexually assaulted her. She described the assault as an oral rape.
London's Metropolitan Police Service confirmed it launched an investigation into Latter's allegations in January, but says the Crown Prosecution Service advised that "further information was needed before a charging decision could be made."
“We understand the delay has been distressing for this victim-survivor," the police said in an email. "However, our focus has been on putting forward the best possible evidence.”
A Crown Prosecution Service spokesperson said it will "continue to work closely with investigators to provide advice and make sure cases progress as quickly as possible.”
CBC reached out to Brand for comment through his lawyers, but did not receive a response.
Why she decided to go on record
Latter previously spoke about her experience under the pseudodyn "Alice" in a 2023 Sunday Times investigation and the subsequent Times/Channel 4 podcast, Dispatches.
During Brand's pre-trial hearing, defence lawyer Ian Winter KC called Dispatches "a work of fiction" and questioned whether the women featured in the documentary actually exist.
That's why Latter decided to go on record.
"It suddenly clicked in my head," Latter said. "I was like, well, I'm a real person and Russell knows who I am.'"
Winter's law firm, Cloth Fair Chambers, did not respond to a request for comment.
The lawyer has since reported himself to his professional regulator after he had a public confrontation with a Sunday Times reporter following Brand's conviction.

Since she first revealed her identity on Saturday in the Times, Latter says she's been on the receiving end of cruel and defamatory comments from Brand's supporters.
But she says she has no regrets.
"I've just been reading the stories that women are sending to me — and that parents are sending to me, and teachers and social workers and foster carers — about the children that are between the ages of 16 and 18 in their lives that they can't protect," she said.
"That's what's really pushing me forward, and it's also reinforcing to me that I did the right thing in going public. Because this is clearly a much bigger issue than even I could have imagined."
