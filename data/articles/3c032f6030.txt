The fetishization of mass murderers has been around since long before TikTok.
Bonnie and Clyde were folk heroes. Serial killer Ted Bundy was adored by a legion of female fans. And the mother of Columbine shooter Dylan Klebold reported receiving love letters to her son in the mail written by young girls.
More recently, this phenomenon has manifested in a network known as the True Crime Community, or TCC, a group of mainly young people who lionize mass shooters like Klebold. (This group is distinct from true crime enthusiasts who like to read books or listen to podcasts about unsolved crimes.)
While this community has been around for years, in 2026, it moved en masse to TikTok. There, supercharged by the video-sharing platform's powerful black-box algorithm, the community’s violent, nihilistic worldview expanded its reach dramatically, serving up graphic content to users who had never been exposed before.
Tracking the scale and real-world impact of the network has been difficult, because the coded language, symbols, and memes used within the group are constantly changing to avoid detection by platforms and law enforcement. On TikTok some of the group members most interested in violence began referring to the group as the True Cringe Community. When TikTok blocked that term, they began referring to themselves as the True Larp Community.
But now, for the first time, the Network Contagion Research Institute (NCRI), a Princeton, New Jersey–based nonprofit that tracks online extremism, has collated data that shows that attacks and attempted attacks linked to the TCC have exploded by 200 percent over the last two years. The data, shared exclusively with WIRED ahead of publication, shows that the number of annual attacks or attempted attacks have gone from 13 reported in all of 2024 to 30 by mid-October this year.
The footprint of these attacks also expanded globally. Attacks and attempted attacks linked to the TCC were registered in 13 different countries in 2026, with two of every three incidents happening outside the US. And NCRI’s researchers predict that without intervention, the situation is about to get much worse.
“This is heading towards an explosion,” Joel Finkelstein, chief science officer with NCRI, tells WIRED. “That's what the data is suggesting, and the data is unambiguous. It's heading next year to a point where this is going to be happening more than once a week. That's really alarming.”
The TCC is part of a broader online nihilistic violent extremism movement and often overlaps with the extremist crime network known as “The Com,” which incorporates groups like 764 and No Lives Matter,
The TCC’s members, a large proportion of which are teenagers, share content online that references, glorifies, mimics, and simulates school shooters, mass shooters, and serial killers from all over the world. This is done through flattering videos posted on social media, taking pictures replicating the hand signals attackers used during their killing sprees, or wearing the T-shirts worn by mass shooters. The members also use AI to turn videos of mass shootings into cartoons or anime in order to avoid detection by online platforms’ trust and safety teams.
Beyond posting fan fiction about their favorite school shooters, this community also encourages others to carry out their own attacks. If successful, those attackers in turn become part of TCC’s ever-expanding lore and mythology, becoming part of a self-reinforcing cycle.
While writing its report, NCRI researchers documented numerous other attacks linked to the group, including the September 18 shooting at a high school in the Philippines and an October 1 attack at a secondary school in Torreón, Mexico. There was also a shooting on October 12 at a middle school in France and a knife attack on October 13 in South Africa that have also been linked to the TCC community.
While TCC was found on a lot of platforms online, for the first 10 years of its existence, much of its activity was centered on Tumblr. But in January, the site implemented a ban wave that drove much of the community to TikTok, where the platform’s unique algorithm put TCC-related content in front of a much broader audience, according to a report from the Institute of Strategic Dialogue published earlier this month.
“Algorithmically driven engagement has supercharged its spread,” the report’s authors wrote. “Unlike Tumblr, where the fandom is largely isolated, TikTok actively works to expose users to new content through its algorithm, resulting in TCC videos being served to a much larger audience.”
Finkelstein says that while TikTok does remove some of the accounts his team flags to the platform, in general the company is not doing enough to combat the problem.
“[TikTok’s] capacity to manage this is very spotty,” Finkelstein said. “This is an uncontained open wound. Whatever measures they think they're making to manage this are obviously inadequate to deal with the damage that the community is unleashing. If you took TikTok offline for a few weeks, my guess is these attacks would slow down because there's not a permission structure in place that's encouraging vulnerable youth to go out and murder people in order to be famous with their peers because they're so depressed and living in their mom's basement and spending 10 hours a day on social media.”
TikTok, whose US operations are now owned primarily by a group of investors including Oracle, controlled by GOP megadonor and Donald Trump ally Larry Ellison, said it had rules in place to proactively remove TCC-linked content.
“We enforce strict rules against content that promotes violence or glorifies perpetrators of mass violence, and we had already banned a large number of the accounts in this report,” a TikTok spokesperson tells WIRED.
WIRED shared the report with several other researchers who closely track TCC activity online, and all broadly concluded that NCRI’s assessment was accurate. One independent researcher, Ry Terran, pointed out that accurately tracking this movement is difficult given the dense nature of the language they use around attacks.
“When examining perpetrators' social media, I see that they frequently frame threats as a joke, a LARP, or AI-generated,” Terran tells WIRED. “Social media companies should not take these claims at face value, but rather report any credible threats. Without appropriate interventions, TCC-inspired attacks will likely continue to increase.”
Another European-based researcher, who requested anonymity due to fears of retaliation, tells WIRED, “We're panicking here in Europe.” The researcher collaborates with European law enforcement agencies and says they are concerned about the increasing number of attacks taking place globally.
Finkelstein says that US lawmakers and law enforcement need to do more in order to combat this problem. “The FBI seems to want to deal with it by arresting kids. None of this makes any sense,” he says. “We've got to step back and see the scale of this. We should be turning to our law enforcement agencies and our national security apparatus that has the ear of the president and say something's got to be done about this.”
The FBI declined to comment, and the White House did not respond to a request for comment. The Department of Homeland Security referred WIRED to the FBI.
“The Justice Department remains vigilant against the threat of such violent extremist networks that operate within the United States and around the globe,” Wyn Hornbuckle, a spokesman for the Department of Justice, tells WIRED.
