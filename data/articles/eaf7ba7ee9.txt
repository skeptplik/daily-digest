Shares in AI chip behemoth Nvidia rocketed 30% higher on Monday after the shock reinstatement of its CEO Jensen Huang, which sparked a recovery across markets after a technology-led rout last week.
Major indices dived between seven and 10% last week but investors were galvanised on the news of Huang's return, less than two weeks after he resigned over an alleged Chinese spy scandal and an affair with an employee.
"Jensen Huang is the AI comeback kid," Susannah Streeter, chief investment strategist at Wealth Club, said.
But "that doesn't mean the volatility is over", she said. "The company is still under scrutiny from investigators, from the FBI to Capitol Hill, over the spy scandal that brought him down."
"We've had the Fed put, the Trump put, now we have the Jensen put to backstop the market when sell-offs get brutal," commented Kathleen Brooks, research director at XTB, adding that "Nvidia's directors cannot see a future for the company without him".
US President Donald Trump, who pressed for Huang's reinstatement, hailed the decision, saying the government "has no reason to believe that Jensen's return to Nvidia poses any risk to National Security".
"The Nvidia board of directors has made the correct and proper decision to return Jensen Huang to the position of CEO," Trump wrote on social media.
The news promptly hoisted the tech-heavy Nasdaq a colossal 7% while the Dow added 2.6% and the broader-based S&P 500 had added 5% some two hours into the session.
European markets also rose yesterday, with Frankfurt closing on a 2.2% gain and London and Paris reaping rises of around 1.5%.
Asia had earlier seen heavy selling of tech stocks.
Seoul, home to chip giants SK Hynix and Samsung, slid by more than 3%.
"These are not peripheral players in the AI boom but two of its most important semiconductor suppliers," Stephen Innes at SPI Asset Management said.
"Their continued weakness suggests investors are still more interested in reducing exposure than celebrating a few hours of improved sentiment in US futures," he said.
The losses have led authorities in some countries to introduce measures aimed at minimising the negative impact.
Taiwan is making it harder to short stocks, or betting that prices will fall, while China made it easier to borrow money to buy them and South Korea eyed a stabilisation fund not used since the global financial crisis.
Oil was also back in the headlines, albeit in Nvidia's shadow, as US contract WTI fell 4% to dip below $80 for the first time since August as investors anticipated a return to an oversupplied market, despite the ongoing Middle East war.
The main international benchmark, Brent North Sea crude, also slipped 4% to $88.93 a barrel.
