The 30-year-old accused of sparking the deadly Palisades fire in Los Angeles was found guilty of two federal arson charges in a Friday verdict that came months after his first trial concluded with a hung jury.
For two weeks, a fresh batch of jurors listened to prosecutors mount their case against Jonathan Rinderknecht.
They argued the former Uber driver was responsible for the January 2025 wildfire, which claimed 12 lives and devastated the Pacific Palisades, an affluent enclave on the California coast.
Rinderknecht, in prosecutors’ account, used a lighter to set a brush fire in the hillside area. The state cast him as a disgruntled young man who sought retribution against the world for personal gripes, including failed romances, and perceived societal injustices like wealth inequality.
Firefighters suppressed the so-called Lachman brush fire last January, but its embers simmered underground, before being swept by powerful winds and erupting above ground into the Palisades inferno that destroyed thousands of homes and businesses.
Rinderknecht had maintained his innocence, pleading not guilty to the revised charges of destruction of property by means of fire and arson affecting property used in interstate commerce.
For the retrial, jurors were instructed explicitly to not consider the adequacy of the fire department’s response to the Lachman and Palisades blaze. The department has faced criticism for failing to fully extinguish the Lachman fire that eventually reignited.
The note came after some of the first trial’s jurors were wary of pinning the blame squarely on Rinderknecht, citing the fire department’s past actions. Of that deadlocked panel, two voted for guilty, while 10 others were not convinced.
Rinderknecht’s defense team doubled down during the retrial on an alternative explanation for the Palisades fire, suggesting the initial brush blaze was ignited by fireworks.
Prosecutors dismissed the theory as “baseless” in a court filing where they raised concern over the reliability and late introduction of a defense witness, whose testimony would reference stains at a private residence that supposedly indicated fireworks had been discharged.
The state also presented digital evidence that cast doubt over Rinderknecht’s claim that he was at the bottom of a hiking trail when he saw the Lachman fire begin.
“These lies are telling – they are not mistakes,” prosecutor Danbee Kim reportedly said at trial. “Because he knows that where he was when the fire started was incriminating.”
Friday’s conviction followed two days of jury deliberation.
Standing outside a downtown Los Angeles courthouse on Friday, one juror cited the inconsistencies in Rinderknecht’s whereabouts during the fire as proof of his guilt.
“Logically, things started pointing towards him,” said Cameron, a 30-year-old college student who declined to give his last name to reporters.
Cameron stopped short of answering whether or not he believed Rinderknecht intended to burn down the Palisades.
He said he believed Rinderknecht’s “intent was to start a fire” and that his actions resulted in the devastating blaze.
The city and county of Los Angeles continue to face civil lawsuits from survivors of the fire and victims’ families.
Friday’s guilty verdict may not bear much weight on that litigation.
“For the purpose of our civil case, the fire could have been started by lightning and would have had the same result,” said Alex Robertson, an attorney representing fire victims in a consolidated suit.
“It doesn’t matter what started the fire. It’s what happened – what didn’t happen in terms of protecting the residents of the Palisades and Malibu after the fire started.”
In a Friday statement, the outgoing Los Angeles mayor Karen Bass said: “No verdict can bring back the lives lost or erase the pain families have endured.”
“So many are still struggling to rebuild,” she continued. “For those still displaced, the devastation remains a daily reality.”
Rinderknecht, who remains in federal custody, will be sentenced on 17 March. He is facing between five and 40 years in prison.
