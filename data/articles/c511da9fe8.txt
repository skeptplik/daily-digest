In short:
Lani Pallister says she wants to make more history by winning a third consecutive 800m world title.
Flynn Southam says Australia is in a golden age of swimming.
What's next?
The Short Course World Championships get underway on December 1 in Beijing.
Australia's swimmers are bullishly approaching the 2026 World Short Course Championships as they gear up for a massive lead-in to the 2028 LA Olympics.
The 23-strong Australian team heads to Beijing on Thursday, ahead of the short course championships getting underway on December 1.
Lani Pallister will headline the Australian team, looking to win a third consecutive women's 800-metre title, an event she also holds the world record in, beating legendary American Katie Ledecky's mark by a colossal 3.42 seconds in October last year.
"Any time I get behind the blocks I want to make history; I want to swim times that haven't been seen before," she said.
"I was super stoked last year with my 800. Breaking Katie's world record by three seconds was, I feel, the big jumping point for my career, particularly in the last 12 months."
For a generation, Ledecky has been the benchmark against which all other female distance swimmers are measured — and for good reason.
The American legend has proven almost unbeatable over the 800m freestyle, claiming the last four Olympic gold medals in the distance.
And yet, at the Pan Pacific Championships earlier this year, Pallister stunned the living legend to land a remarkable psychological blow as the Olympics approaches.
"I am the kind of person who likes getting behind the blocks and racing everyone," Pallister said.
"I don't want the result to be just about beating Katie; I want it to be [that] I swam a world-class time that only two other people in history have beaten.
"That's a really important thing for me, stamping my legacy as a distance swimmer and not just someone that's racing someone else in the pool."
Pallister acknowledges that Ledecky's mantle as the greatest women's distance swimmer of all time is unlikely to be shaken in the short or even medium term, whether she claims victory in LA or not.
But that does not mean that the Sunshine Coast swimmer is overawed by her rival.
"Katie's held that title [greatest of all time] for the last 14 years. I think she's done absolutely tremendous things for women's distance swimming and women's sport in general," Pallister said.
"Just to be part of that story and part of that race in two years' time will be unreal, but also knowing that I'm doing it for me and because I want to swim and I want to be successful.
"To be able to have the opportunity to race in two years' time and be in Brisbane in six years' time and challenge for Olympic gold medals and be close to world records for long course and short course is all I ever dreamed of."
Pallister is a noted workaholic when it comes to racing — the day after the trials for the world short course championships she flew from Sydney to Brisbane to compete in an ocean swim hosted by her surf lifesaving club, Alex Heads. She won it, too.
As such, the 24-year-old will endure an abundance of racing over the next 12 months, which is the backbone of her strategy for success.
"Racing's the best form of training," Pallister said of her plans over the next year.
"So I'm the type of person who's happy to live out of a suitcase and travel and see friends and people overseas, but more importantly get the race practice in."
Flynn Southam has also embraced the chance to race hard and often over the past 12 months, enjoying huge success in Glasgow at the Commonwealth Games.
"I think it kind of stamped my authority," Southam said of his four golds and one silver in Glasgow.
"Not just as potential, but, 'He's here and he's arrived.'
"Our job is to go into the Americans' backyard and play some games with them."
Southam even went as far as to say Australia was in a "golden age" of swimming, a bold claim for a nation with such a tremendous history in the pool.
"This is the best team we've had in this Olympic cycle, and probably will be going into LA [the best] that we've ever had," the 21-year-old said.
Southam, the Commonwealth 100m freestyle champion, said the prospect of competing in LA was all-consuming, dominating his thoughts.
"It's all I can think about," he said.
"Most people can go down to the pub and watch a game of sport and switch off.
"But for me, it's all I think about; it's in my mind 24/7.
"That's what fuels my fire. I live for it. I breathe this stuff.
"The higher the challenge, the higher the pressure, the more I rise to the occasion.
"I think the man who's going to win the 100 free at the LA Olympic Games is the one who is going to be able to deal with the most pressure, the most adversity and still perform under any circumstances.
"And I believe with my whole heart that that's going to be me."
