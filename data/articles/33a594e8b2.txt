A strike that could shut down much of the nation’s busiest port complex is now hours away.
Negotiations between the Pacific Maritime Association (PMA) and the port watchmen of the International Longshore and Warehouse Union (ILWU) Local 26 hit an impasse Tuesday, days before extended talks set to expire at 12:01 a.m. Pacific time Saturday.
The dispute covers roughly 250 guards who handle gate security and cargo watching duty at the Ports of Los Angeles and Long Beach. Other longshore workers, marine clerks and foremen are expected to honor a picket line if they walk out.
According to the PMA, talks ended Tuesday with “little-to-no movement from the union,” preventing any extension from taking place beyond Saturday. Contract talks have already been extended three separate times since the watchmen voted to authorize a strike in late October.
The apparel and footwear industries are watching the negotiations unfold intently ahead of the holiday shopping season due to the high concentration of product passing through the California ports.
The American Apparel & Footwear Association (AAFA) wrote to Labor Secretary Keith Sonderling on Thursday urging the Department of Labor to help the parties reach an agreement and avoid costly disruption.
The AAFA said 36 percent of total apparel, footwear and travel goods imports by value entered the U.S. through the Los Angeles customs district in 2025, totaling $42.5 billion. For footwear alone, the district accounted for about $13.1 billion and 1.2 billion pairs. That is 49 percent of U.S. footwear imports by value and 57 percent by quantity, according to U.S. International Trade Commission data cited in the letter.
The group is among more than 110 trade associations, including the National Retail Federation (NRF), the U.S. Fashion Industry Association (USFIA) and the Retail Industry Leaders Association (RILA) that urged the parties to stay at the negotiating table.
While the port watchmen make up only about 2 percent of the ILWU workforce at the ports, the Local 26 members work at six container terminals that handle about 85 percent of cargo at Long Beach and 33 percent at Los Angeles.
September was the highest-volume month on record for both ports, which handled more than 1.06 million 20-foot equivalent units (TEUs) of imported cargo. October continued the import strength, with 37 percent of all U.S. container volume moving through L.A. and Long Beach, Flexport’s senior director of operations Jabari Watt said.
Given that the six container terminals handle roughly half of the TEUs across the San Pedro Bay ports, Watt told Sourcing Journal that more than 500,000 TEUs could be affected by a strike if dockworkers walk off the job in solidarity.
“We haven’t seen any shift in booking activity,” Watt said. “The one big concern from our end was that the ports were already kind of backed up, and we’re seeing heavy on-dock rail congestion that was really driven from the typhoons that happened in the summer in Asia. It’s gone up significantly, so you know this will just compound the issue that we’re currently going through.”
As of Wednesday, 20,351 containers were waiting for rail at the Port of Los Angeles, according to public data compiled by The Dwell. Nearly 48 percent of them, about 9,687, had waited nine days or longer. The Long Beach gateway had 21,400 containers waiting for on-dock rail on Thursday, with 3,638 (17 percent) waiting nine or more days.
“Every day that we see a stoppage is just going to compound on top of the delays that we’re seeing today,” Watt said. “It’s a lot of people involved. There’s the rail carriers, then there has to be enough truckers that can move the cargo from the on-port over to the rail terminals. Then they have to make sure there’s enough chassis. Even a three-day stoppage could really wreak havoc on the market right now.”
The one sigh of relief for shippers is that most core holiday inventory has already arrived, Watt said, echoing NRF’s indication earlier this month.
According to Watt, the immediate risk is last-minute replenishment and products tied to promotions with specific delivery windows. However, some products were already at risk because of the lengthy rail dwell, particularly for shipments moving to the Midwest.
For example, Watt said he spoke on Tuesday with an apparel company that pushed to move containers that had been dwelling for a few days because they were at risk of missing Black Friday sales.
“Those are the ones that there is some concern, and those are the sectors that are really pushing us because any of that cargo that they were expected to sell against, they need it in the DCs as soon as possible,” Watt said.
Flexport had already advised clients with critical cargo held up by rail dwell to transload it for truck delivery, Watt said. For cargo still on the water, he advised diverting to Seattle, Tacoma or Oakland if that port is part of the vessel’s string of calls. Cargo that has not departed its origin should consider the East Coast.
Watt said any disruption lasting over four or five days would probably prompt carriers to start making contingency plans and dumping cargo at other ports.
Mediterranean Shipping Company (MSC) has already diverted some Los Angeles and Long Beach cargo to Seattle. Watt also warned shippers to watch detention charges and keep records if empty containers cannot be returned.
Behind the weekend deadline is a contract dispute that has been unresolved since last summer. The original deal between both parties expired in July 2025 after more than a year of bargaining with federal mediators.
The multiple extensions pushed the negotiation deadline from Nov. 1 to Nov. 20. PMA’s offer, as described in its Wednesday statement, includes a $3,200 one-time bonus and a wage proposal that would raise average annual earnings for full-time watchmen from more than $170,000 to $198,000, although the description is disputed.
ILWU Local 26 president Luisa Gratz told Seatrade Maritime News that the PMA account is not accurate, saying that even longshore workers do not make that kind of money. Gratz said the union had counter-proposed without receiving a response.
