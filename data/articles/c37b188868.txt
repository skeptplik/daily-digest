New powers allowing defence officials to use lethal force against suspected trespassers into designated zones could have “significant” human rights implications, a Labor-chaired inquiry has warned.
The committee also raised concerns about a lack of requirements to ensure anyone detained under the new laws had access to fresh drinking water and humane conditions. It noted the broad and extensive changes could capture unwitting members of the public who stumble on to a declared defence security zone that hasn’t been publicised.
The controversial bill, which passed the Senate on Monday, was heavily criticised by civil liberty and pro-democracy groups for granting extraordinary powers to defence officials in the name of national security.
The human rights inquiry’s report, published Wednesday evening, notes a number of issues with the bill, including its proportionality and the extent of the powers without adequate safeguards.
The prime minister, Anthony Albanese, defended the laws against critics on Thursday as “nonsense” and “conspiracy theories” from both the “far left” and “far right”.
“What this legislation is about is simply making sure that our defence institutions and infrastructure are kept safe,” Albanese said in question time.
“And, quite frankly, to suggest that a minister would abuse powers in order to designate something that was not associated with defence of this country as somehow needing protection is quite frankly irresponsible, and those spreading that misinformation online should know better.”
But the parliamentary human rights report, led by committee chair and Labor MP Zaneta Mascarenhas, highlighted concerns raised over transparency, the right to free movement, free expression, privacy liberty and life.
Among the report’s harshest criticism is a question over powers given to defence officials and law enforcement use of reasonable and necessary force.
Under the laws, such officials have the power to search a person and their devices – with or without consent – and detain them if they have “reasonable suspicion” they are entering a defence zone.
Reasonable and necessary force can be used to prevent serious injury or death, or when a person is fleeing to avoid being detained or to escape from detention.
However, the committee’s report concluded the reasons to use force were broad.
“In light of the low threshold for detention, including detention without arrest or to carry out an inspection or search of a vehicle, vessel or aircraft, the scope of the measure appears broad and the extent of interference with these rights may be significant,” the report said.
It added the inspector-general of the Australian defence force could only investigate the use of force after a potential violation had occurred and would therefore “not prevent any violation of the power” in an act against international human rights law.
Detention of a person reasonably suspected of committing an offence was also too vague, the report said.
“The measures do not include requirements for officials to consider and ensure human conditions of detention,” the report said.
These included a lack of clarity over whether a person detained without arrest should have access to fresh drinking water, acceptable and humane temperatures, health care and sanitary practices.
There is also no maximum period of detention specified and it is up to an official’s discretion.
‘Impermissible’ powers
The laws give the minister the ability to make a “capability declaration” which allows declared persons to engage in a “reasonable and proportionate” activity that might otherwise be illegal under federal or state laws.
The report noted the minister isn’t required to notify “affected persons” that the declaration has been made, “meaning that affected persons may be unaware that a declaration exists or that they may be liable for offences in relation to a declaration”.
“The committee considers there is a risk that the proposed defence capability declaration framework may constitute an impermissible limit on a potentially wide range of human rights. However, as the bill has now passed, the committee makes no further comment,” the report said.
Under the laws, the minister can also declare an area of land, sea or airspace to be a “defence area” and classify it as emergency, temporary or mobile site. This would include conventional defence bases and sites – but could extend to military vehicles, buildings or structures.
A person suspected of entering or taking pictures of such sites could be jailed for up to seven years, or fined $152,880, or both.
The inquiry report said the laws did not clearly outline whether individuals would be notified of declarations, or whether defence zones could include residential or highly populated areas. It added the measures should be “accompanied by strong safeguards to assist proportionality”.
