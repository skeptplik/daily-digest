At QCon San Francisco 2026, Mouli Mukherjee, Engineering Manager for Data Infrastructure at OpenAI, argued that AI agents are most useful not for rebuilding a data platform, but for the open-ended operational work that is hard to turn into fixed playbooks. OpenAI’s platform runs at exabyte scale, with hundreds of thousands of datasets and a Spark fleet on millions of cores, and Mukherjee showed how Codex-driven agents can take on that kind of work when the right systems sit underneath them.
Mukherjee clearly distinguished between deterministic systems and agents. She stated that anything requiring consistent outputs for the same inputs, such as access control, configuration resolution, and data correctness guarantees, should remain within the realm of proper systems. Agents utilize these systems as tools and do not attempt to reimplement them. As she expressed:
We should definitely build the systems where we want guarantees and guardrails.
Agents earn their place where failure modes and remediations cannot be fully enumerated in advance, such as debugging: follow the evidence, decide what to try next, and stay inside the tools’ guarantees.
The Spark upgrade outlines steps taken to ensure safety. The team first built a shadow-run service for testing, which reused production inputs and operated on a low-priority queue to avoid impacting live operations. They also implemented a configuration override service to prevent experiments from changing global settings and developed a validation skill to compare production and shadow runs. After these safeguards were in place, agents claimed jobs, launched shadow runs, reviewed validation reports, classified errors, and transitioned jobs to the new Spark version.
Mukherjee reported that the same primitives now cover more of routine platform operations. Agents can tune job efficiency continuously by inspecting query plans, trying a config change, comparing runs, and iterating. For lower-risk incremental library releases, the team sometimes skips the shadow step and promotes directly, trading capacity for higher rollback risk. Jobs that pass shadow validation but later fail in production can auto-roll back to the previous Spark version so pipelines keep running without manual intervention. On the support side, agents can search past incident reports, suggest runbook updates, trace why a table is stale through orchestrator and scheduler logs, and help users understand access-control denials, while permissions still enforce the user’s own credentials.
Mukherjee recommended starting with the repetitive, open-ended tasks where judgment matters and failure modes are too numerous to hard-code, building the safety mechanisms first, and only then letting agents operate inside those bounds so experimentation stays repeatable, observable, and reversible.
