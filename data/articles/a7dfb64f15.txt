Fighting between Russia and Ukraine has expanded geographically, drawing concerns
In recent days, Ukraine has sunk a Russian warship docked in port in Libya, and Russian missiles have landed in Poland. The Kremlin has not commented on the missiles.
MICHEL MARTIN, HOST:
Depending on your point of view, Russia's war in Ukraine is either revving up or winding down.
STEVE INSKEEP, HOST:
This month, the Trump administration claimed it was making progress toward a negotiated peace. Also this month, the fighting has expanded geographically into Eastern Europe, Northern Europe, even the Mediterranean, as if it was a world war.
MARTIN: Joining us to talk about both these dynamics is NPR's Charles Maynes from Moscow. Charles, good morning.
CHARLES MAYNES, BYLINE: Morning, Michel.
MARTIN: So Russia and Ukraine are both striking out far beyond their borders. How concerned should we be?
MAYNES: You know, there have been long concerns that an incident along NATO's northeastern flank near a border in Russia could serve as a tripwire for turning the war in Ukraine into a wider conflict. But recent events have brought that back to our attention. Last week we saw three Russian cruise missiles land in southeastern Poland, and on Saturday, a sanctioned Russian cargo ship was hit by what is presumed to have been a Ukrainian drone in the Baltic Sea. But now we're also seeing action further afield. Ukraine destroyed a Russian naval warship docked in Libya in the Mediterranean. Russia has its own shadow role providing intel to Iran in its war with the U.S. And just this morning, South Korea said it scrambled jets to chase away Russian sorties operating near its airspace. So the spillover is real, and the map is wide.
MARTIN: So what is being done to contain it?
MAYNES: Well, you know, Russian President Vladimir Putin constantly dismisses this idea that he's seeking direct confrontation with NATO. He says it's absurd. But there have been a spate of recent incidents of bomb threats, cyberattacks, sabotage that some in Europe believe are the work of Russia's security services. Russia's goal, it seems, would be to tear at the alliance's core commitment to collective security - this idea that an attack on one is an attack on all - and it's divided NATO. How do you respond to acts that fall below this threshold of outright war? So for example, President Trump has dismissed incursions by Russia into NATO airspace as basically mistakes. Now, as to other areas where we've seen Ukraine launch attacks, like Libya or apparently the Baltic Sea, it seems to show that Ukraine is intent on striking symbols of Russian power anywhere, and that also makes some supporters of Kyiv nervous.
MARTIN: On the diplomatic front, President Trump is suggesting that a peace deal may be in the offing. Why does he say that?
MAYNES: Well, after two years, we saw the Trump administration really make no headway. They've changed tactics by expanding the talks beyond Russia and Ukraine to focus on European security as a whole. And the conversations are now fundamentally centered on what security guarantees are on offer to Ukraine, to Russia, and to Europe. But the problem seems to be there's a bit of a disconnect between what that means to each of them.
For example, Trump's envoys say Russia is, quote, "comfortable" with NATO-like guarantees for Ukraine. Russian officials have pushed back against that idea hard. But President Trump - he says this is all public posturing, that everyone is being much more flexible behind the scenes. We'll just have to see if he's right.
MARTIN: And meanwhile, the war continues.
MAYNES: It does, and it was staggering losses, particularly from the Russian side, if new findings published yesterday by various independent Russian media in exile is correct. They've been sifting through data that was hacked from Russia's statistics agency and concluded that upwards of an astonishing 700,000 Russian troops have been killed in this war, and those numbers continue to grow.
MARTIN: That is NPR's Charles Maynes in Moscow. Charles, thank you.
MAYNES: Thank you.
