According to experts, the cybersecurity incidents Europe experienced in November should not come as a surprise. But how much do we know about these attacks? And what can Europe do to improve its cybersecurity?
From cyberattacks on fridges to bomb threats at schools, November 2026 has been marked by a series of incidents that have unsettled Europe and brought the state of the bloc’s cybersecurity back into the spotlight.
In the second episode of Password, Euronews Tech Talks’ monthly miniseries on the major tech events shaping Europe, we dive into hacking attacks and technical outages with Euronews’ journalists Jonathan Benton and Roselyne Min.
What happened?
On 2 November, supermarket refrigeration systems in Belgium and several other European countries were targeted in cyberattacks, resulting in food waste and raising concerns across the continent.
Belgian Interior Minister Bernard Quintin later said Iran was “very likely” behind the attacks, while Foreign Minister Maxime Prévot stated there was not enough evidence to draw conclusions.
Regardless of who was behind the issue, it was not the first time supermarkets were targeted. Already in October, similar attacks had taken place in France.
“We don't exactly know what the hackers wanted, but if they wanted people to notice, they certainly achieved that visibility,” Min said. “If someone gets into a government database, it can take months for you to find out. But if you walk into a local supermarket and the staff is emptying the freezers, you see that impact right away,” she continued.
A cyberattack, this time directed at a political institution, targeted the European Commission, which confirmed the incident on 19 November.
“Several Commission applications were temporarily taken offline, including HR tools such as the EU Academy,” Benton explained. “Email addresses were among the data exposed, but there was no money that was stolen,” he continued.
The European Commission suspects a China-backed hacking group is behind the attack. Yet, Beijing has consistently denied supporting hacking groups accused of conducting cyberattacks on foreign actors.
The bomb threats, which paralysed flights across the continent and schools, were also a major concern for Europe. In Italy, on 11 November, around 6,000 schools were targeted, and students evacuated, spreading chaos all over the country.
“This is the main advice: that this should have been caught by spam filters. Although they [experts] have warned that this wasn't necessarily enough, that people should have been a bit more careful scrutinising and checking their emails, checking for phishing approaches, this kind of thing, to find suspicious activity,” Benton said.
Hybrid warfare of Europe unpreparedness?
Following these disruptions, the concept of hybrid warfare once again featured in public debates and newspaper headlines, with commentators linking the recent attacks to a broader strategy of destabilising Europe for political reasons.
Yet, Min prefers a more guarded approach. “I’d be cautious calling every incident hybrid warfare. (...) A government can also back hackers simply to raise money even if there’s no geopolitical tension per se with Europe,” she said, bringing the example of North Korea, where hackers are employed to steal cryptocurrencies.
Regarding Europe’s readiness to face cybersecurity threats, experts seem to agree that Europe is on the right path, but more investment and better implementation of current frameworks are needed to improve Europe's cybersecurity systems.
“Europe does not lack cybersecurity companies; we have huge growth in that. Our problem is the scalable market,” Cecilia Bonefield-Dahl, director-general of DigitalEurope, the trade association for Europe's digital technology industries, told Euronews Next.
“If Europe is all about that, making us competitive and making us safer in Europe, that is exactly how we should spend our time and our money,” she continued.
AI and quantum computing, the new threats to cybersecurity
According to the European Commission, the cyberattack it suffered was conducted with the help of artificial intelligence, a technology that is believed to be already enhancing hackers’ capabilities, helping them find vulnerabilities in their targeted systems.
Quantum computing is another technology that can further threaten cybersecurity.
“It (quantum computing) will only massively expand the capacity for the system to find vulnerabilities, but not only that, but to also break down conventional cryptography, which means normal passwords and security systems that would have taken, for example, millions of years to break, would now take hours,” Benton explained.
For these reasons, according to Joyce Hackmayer, associate fellow in the international security programme at Chatham House, Europe needs to act faster.
“The threats are familiar and so are the fixes. AI adds urgency because it is helping attackers move faster, but it can also help defenders. But the time Europe has to act is shrinking,” she told Euronews Next.
