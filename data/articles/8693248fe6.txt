Tougher pollution rules governing traffic, power generation and commerce in London mean an unexpected air-quality signal now dominates the city at certain points of the day: cooking emissions.
Pollution from commercial kitchens is now the dominant source of particulate matter pollution in London at midday and in the early evening, new analysis shows, outstripping emissions from traffic and other sources such as combustion.
The results are another sign that pollution levels in the city from traffic and power generation have fallen dramatically in recent years, says Ben Langford at the UK Centre for Ecology and Hydrology. “It’s definitely a good news story,” he says, pointing out that particulate matter emissions for road transport have fallen by more than 90 per cent in the UK since 1996. “If you went back to 1996, the cooking signal would really be in the noise compared to the traffic.”
Together with colleagues, Langford used a technique called aerosol mass spectrometry, which measures the chemical composition of air pollution particles in real time. This allowed the researchers to pinpoint the specific source of fine particulate matter – known as PM1 – and how this composition changes over the course of the day. The data was collected in London during summer and winter in 2021.
Fine particulate matter is generally measured as PM2.5, which describes particles with a diameter of 2.5 micrometres or smaller. But a large proportion of PM2.5 is PM1 – particles with a diameter smaller than one micrometre. The smallest particles are often the most harmful, as they are able to stay airborne longer and pass into human bloodstreams.
While emissions from traffic still dominate ambient pollution levels, the team found that cooking emissions emerge as the dominant source of PM1 pollution during mealtimes in London, contributing up to 83 per cent of midday emissions and 87 per cent in the early evening.
This is likely dominated by emissions from shallow and deep-frying cooking oil in commercial kitchens, the researchers say. “In terms of what the city is directly emitting in primary aerosols, cooking is definitely becoming dominant, particularly at those peak mealtime periods,” says Langford.
The picture was similar in Beijing, where the team also collected data during winter 2016 and summer 2017. There, cooking emissions contributed up to 61 per cent of midday emissions and 76 per cent of early-evening emissions.
Scientists are still working to understand whether the source of particulate emissions determines the potential harm to public health. It may be, for example, that PM1 caused by cooking emissions is less harmful to humans than PM1 caused by traffic or coal burning.
The study looks only at primary sources of emissions, which is the pollution being produced in the city itself. The air pollution residents are being exposed to could look quite different, including particulate pollution that has drifted in from the suburbs and beyond. “A lot of what you breathe in in London is not necessarily what’s emitted locally,” notes Langford.
The study also uses data collected from a very central location in London, close to lots of commercial restaurants but with few domestic residences, says Roy Harrison at the University of Birmingham, UK. “You’re not going to have the same level of wood burning, for example, as you have in the suburbs,” he points out. Research from Imperial College London suggests that 17 per cent of fine particle pollution in London comes from wood-burning stoves.
