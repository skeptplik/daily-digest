Peter Gabriel asks me to fetch my laptop: he wants to show me a video of himself playing a keyboard duet with a bonobo called Kanzi. In fairness, said footage is, as Gabriel suggests, “extraordinary”. Working with primatologist Sue Savage-Rumbaugh, who developed a means of communicating with bonobos involving sign language and a speech-synthesis keyboard, Gabriel plays droning chords on a synthesiser while Kanzi picks out complementary notes on a piano. “Look, she likes that note, which is a ninth, and then she finds the octave,” he enthuses as the video plays. “There’s a real musicality there.”
It is all deeply unlikely, but it seems the perfect illustration of how easily a conversation with Gabriel can go wildly – if fascinatingly – off-piste. I’ve only been in his company for 10 minutes, long enough to ask a solitary question about his epic new album o\i: its 13 tracks last nearly 90 minutes, the result, he suggests, of a longstanding interest in African music “which made me realise that a song could last as long as a song could last”, and of “being old enough to think ‘fuck it’, really”. For anyone who feels that an hour and a half of exquisitely arranged, beautifully produced, occasionally deeply strange art rock – its sound shapeshifting from the folk-flecked A Hard Lesson to the glowing pop hues of Till Your Mind Is Shining – is too meagre an offering, it arrives in two versions: a “dark” mix by US producer Tchad Blake and a “bright” version that’s the handiwork of Mike “Spike” Stent, famed for working with everyone from U2 and Madonna to Frank Ocean and Harry Styles.
I had mentioned the two versions, a routine enough inquiry, but it quickly turns into a discussion about the potential replacement of car stereos with immersive sound systems, which then unexpectedly shifts into an explanation of the work of Michael Levin, an American professor of bioengineering who specialises in morphogenesis. “He’s found some bio-electric instructions that can make an eye grow on the leg of a frog, for example,” says Gabriel, and has apparently “created life forms that never existed before, which really challenges evolution.” Gabriel is interested in “sonifying” some of his data and using it in a subsequent mix of the album.
From there, we move on to software engineer Blaise Agüera y Arcas, whose experiments with an esoteric 90s programming language called Brainfuck have led him to “the radical idea that some constants in maths might actually have some form of life”. This leads us, via the “re-evaluation of what consciousness is and what intelligence is”, to the belief of internet pioneer Vint Cerf that attempting to communicate with other intelligent species might prepare us for the possibility of encountering aliens, and thus to Savage-Rumbaugh and Kanzi the bonobo.
“She used to allow the bonobos to watch videos, and to choose the videos they watched,” Gabriel says, laughing, “and their favourite, perhaps obviously, was Planet of the Apes, then Harry and the Hendersons, which is a film about a sasquatch who came to live with a family. The whole thing blew me away. I think we’re the most arrogant of all apes, and there’s just so much intelligence we’ve chosen to ignore and instead see the world as something that’s entirely available for our exploitation, and I think that’s cost us, as we are now learning.”
Morphogenisis, inter-species communication, the re-evaluation of consciousness: it should go without saying that you don’t usually end up discussing these things when you ask an august rock legend about his latest album. But, as it turns out, that’s what inevitably happens when you ask Gabriel a question. We meet in a pub not far from his home in Notting Hill. He says he’s here “to sell the new album”, but almost every query about it seems to lead him somewhere completely different: science, activism, the nature of consciousness, how language is “a fairly crude tool” for communication, the controversial 1970s self-improvement courses of Werner Erhard.
You can’t fault his diversity of interests, even if that diversity of interests is why it takes so long for him to make albums. o\i is essentially the second part of a project that began with 2023’s i/o, which was his first album of new material in 21 years. Like its predecessor, it contains songs that Gabriel has spent decades tinkering with: he started work on A Hard Lesson in 1989, when he was still a huge mainstream pop star thanks to 1986’s So, an album that shifted more than 5m copies in the US alone.
Yes, he concedes, it is a bit weird to find yourself, at the age of 76, still working on songs you started writing in your 30s: “I recognise that guy, but you’re bound to make different decisions having lived a little longer.” But that’s just the way it is. His friend Laurie Anderson once suggested that Gabriel’s problem was that he was more interested in the process of making something than in finishing it. He thinks the real issue is that he’s easily distracted.
“If you have some measure of success, you end up meeting all sorts of people and having fascinating conversations, which lead to detours, and you think, ‘Well, life is for living,’” he says. “If you get on that conveyor belt of album-tour-album, there’s not time to breathe or have any kind of life, and that always seems somewhat sad to me. I think the diversions feed the music. You know, if you want a better class of shit, you’ve got to eat a more varied diet.”
Hence the digressive nature of our interview. Gabriel seems to have developed a genuinely intriguing theory about virtually everything, from theme parks to funerals: he thinks people should “plant a tree over their dead body, or the dead body of their loved ones, so you’d get woods where there wouldn’t be any signage, but if you go and sit under your dad’s tree, your phone would have the GPS position and you would start seeing his videos, hearing his voice, all the digital data would be locked into that GPS point”.
Moreover, he has an incredibly engaging way of talking about all this stuff, evincing such enthusiasm and curiosity that you’re carried along with him: on the off-chance that you wanted to pitch the idea of growing eyeballs on the legs of frogs, you could do substantially worse than get Gabriel to act as your salesman. It probably helps his cause that Gabriel has a hugely impressive track record of thinking ahead of the curve.
At the height of his fame as the frontman of Genesis, he was sharp enough to realise that the genre of progressive rock they had helped invent was becoming too grandiloquent and remote for its own good – “prancing around in fairyland” as he once put it – and that the band he had formed might be more successful without him. The latter opinion wasn’t very popular with the rest of Genesis, but nevertheless proved to be spot on.
A few years later, he was the first British artist to realise that the future of pop lay in sampling: for some time in the early 80s, if you wanted to use a sampler on your new track you had to borrow Gabriel’s, because he was the first person in the country to own one; he then set up a company to import and distribute the new technology. Twenty years later, he cottoned on very quickly to the idea that music was going to primarily exist online, co-founding a streaming platform that might have become the industry standard had it not launched at exactly the same time as a competitor called Spotify.
If he was right about everything from sampling to streaming to the latter-day career of Genesis, perhaps he’s also right about AI. In a musical world filled with people decrying its malign influence, Gabriel is one of its few high-profile boosters: he recently suggested that ultimately “everything will be done better” by AI than by humans.
He says it again today, albeit with caveats. “The application of AI and robots in warfare is really scary. There’s the sense that we’re creating a species that is way, way more intelligent than we are. If we look around, there are not many good examples of intelligent species looking after less-intelligent species, except as pets.
“You can see the worry on [computer scientist and ‘Godfather of AI’] Geoffrey Hinton’s face,” he continues. “I think we need to slow it down a bit, we need some international agreement, for which there are precedents, like in air safety or the Start treaty for nuclear weapons. We need guard rails, like a seed of wisdom in every algorithm before it’s allowed to be released that could allow for monitoring and a measure of control and an ‘off’ button. But it interests me in the moment as a whole new set of tools that could encourage people to consider themselves film-makers, painters, musicians, to explore themselves in that way.”
With or without AI, Gabriel says he thinks that’s true anyway: “Being those things is just a matter of walking around and saying, ‘This is what I am’, then committing to learning about it. You can get accelerated learning through AI – even though you haven’t learned all the skills to get there, you can quickly access some of the joy of making really nice-sounding things or have the AI eliminate some of your worst moments. So in the long run, as a creative tool: absolutely extraordinary. It’s like you’ve been painting in black and white and someone suddenly throws a colour palette at you, saying: ‘What can you do with this?’”
Contrary to most, Gabriel genuinely seems to believe that AI is capable of creating great art. “It’s just down to better algorithms. So how do we deal with this as a species? I think long-term, if you can’t defeat it, you have to join it. I think we will become cyborgs, because we’ll have these brain-computer interfaces, where you insert something in your brain that allows you then to communicate to the computer … I don’t think there’s any reason why we shouldn’t use our brains to become more AI.”
He’s keen to point out that “any of these things can be used positively or negatively”. He recently had a conversation with American technologist Mary Lou Jepsen, who was concerned about the human rights implications of the whole brain-computer-interface idea and its capacity for being used as a form of torture. But nevertheless, Gabriel seems fascinated by the idea: he has a concept for something so vague, I don’t really understand it, called “the brain show”, that will “allow you to see how the brain affects us and how it might be manipulated” and which would feature some of the songs from o\i as part of its soundtrack. “It could be a virtual thing or a stage thing or both, but how it all works, and the story, I’ve got no idea,” he shrugs. “I’m at that stage where you can feel a sort of lump in the mist and you’re trying to imagine it becoming something, which I think is the way most people approach creativity.”
In the meantime, he has umpteen other ideas floating around. He’s intrigued by the concept of a virtual performance, somewhere between Abba Voyage and U2’s “wonderful and inspiring” shows at the Sphere in Las Vegas. There’s a plan to make a film based on The Lamb Lies Down on Broadway, the 1974 concept album that preceded his exit from Genesis: you might have thought it was pretty much unfilmable, given that it features a scene in which the lead character has sex with three snake-like creatures, and another in which his recently amputated penis is snatched away by a passing raven, but Gabriel seems optimistic: he’s been working on the script with Argentinan writer Nicolás Giacobone of Digger fame.
And he wants to “dig into” another of Levin’s ideas, “that you might be able to communicate with cells and get them to behave in a certain way and sound might be a medium for it. I’m fascinated by explorations of consciousness,” he says, then goes on to tell me about experiments in the sentience of plants. “There was a cool guy at MIT who took a plant and put it in a sort of box with a computer on wheels, then moved the plant’s light source around the room and the plant was saying ‘OK’ and moving the box to follow the light around the room. I was wondering if you could do that with an oak tree. Like a sort of Jurassic Park, but with plants instead of dinosaurs. I mean, all these ideas are such great fun to explore.”
