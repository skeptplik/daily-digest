Icac finds female NSW prison staff faced ‘boys club’ culture and harassment
A NSW Independent Commission Against Corruption (Icac) inquiry has found female prison staff who faced boys club-style harassment and abuse were failed by managers, AAP reports.
Investigators uncovered a “boys club” culture which “tolerated harassment and abuse of mainly female colleagues”. Icac investigated grave misconduct allegations, including claims of disrespectful language, sexual assaults, and reprisals for reporting offensive behaviour.
The commission said that culture discouraged employees from making complaints. But it did not find Corrective Services NSW to be corrupt.
Corrective Services NSW accepted the findings and recommendations and was developing an action plan, a spokesperson said.
Conversion ‘therapy’ survivor welcomes new laws in Tasmania outlawing practice
Glen Worrell, who went through two decades of conversion practices aimed at changing his sexual orientation, hopes new laws in Tasmania mean others will never have to do the same, AAP reports.
The island state’s upper house of parliament today passed legislation to ban the practice, giving the green light for the changes to become law.
Conversion practices, often labelled as a “therapy”, are aimed at changing or suppressing someone’s sexual orientation or gender identity.
The reforms in Tasmania come after a decade-long campaign and bring the state into line with NSW, Victoria, Queensland, South Australia and the ACT. Worrell said:
I was quite elated and relieved. I did wonder if we would reach this moment at times.
I wasn’t sure if there would be amendments that would disempower the bill. But I think it’s strong. I believe it will save lives.
The laws include several nation-leading features such as the creation of a commissioner to educate, prevent and respond to any conversion practices.
Survivors can receive financial compensation and there is a mandatory responsibility for government bodies to refer conversion practices to the commissioner.
The laws make it a criminal offence if people intentionally engage in conversion practices or they cause injury, as well as if negligence towards injury is evident.
The passage of the Tasmanian laws comes on the same day that the federal government issued a formal apology to current and former serving members of the Australian Defence Force for harm experienced as a result of discriminatory policies based on individuals’ sexual orientation.
‘We still have a number of things going for us’, treasurer says as he denies Australia in ‘stagflation’
Jim Chalmers has denied Australia is experiencing “stagflation” after the release of grim inflation data today, saying “we still have a number of things going for us”.
At a broad level, stagflation refers to persistent levels of inflation with moderate to poor (in other words, stagnant) economic growth, and elevated unemployment.
During his ABC Afternoon Briefing today, it was put to Chalmers that, given Australia was experiencing rising unemployment, inflation, and interest rates, that was “kind of the recipe for stagflation”. Chalmers replied:
The reason why that term doesn’t fit our situation is because we still have a labour market, which is, by historical standards, pretty robust.
We’ve got growth, which is faster than any major advanced economy except for the US.
We’ve got wages growth above 3%. And so, even in the context of this combination of challenges and risks and uncertainties and pressures, which are in our economy – and we acknowledge that – we still have a number of things going for us as well, and that is why that description doesn’t quite fit us.
Treasurer promises government savings to help in fight against inflation
The treasurer, Jim Chalmers, says the government will have to make “difficult decisions” to respond to the surging cost of living after the release of new inflation data today.
Inflation surged to 4.9% in the year to October, from 4.3% in the 12 months to September, Australian Bureau of Statistics (ABS) data released earlier today showed, amid fears Australia could tip into recession next year.
Although the ABS said the “temporary shocks” of surging fuel costs and the end of electricity rebates drove the larger-than-expected lift in consumer prices, the Coalition has claimed government spending by Labor is fuelling inflation.
Speaking on the ABC’s Afternoon Briefing program a short time ago, Chalmers said inflation was increasing in every major advanced economy, and that the government took its responsibilities seriously.
Chalmers has been preparing to present a mid-year budget update in three weeks’ time, which he called a “mini budget”, and said the government had a three-part plan to deal with the cost of living crunch. He said:
There will be a number of new savings and that’s because we take our responsibilities very seriously in the fight against inflation.
Three parts of our plan, first of all in relation to inflation, running a tight ship in the budget, providing some cost-of-living relief but making it as responsible as possible
Obviously there are issues around how hard it is for first home buyers to crack the market, and so our tax reforms are primarily about that.
And thirdly, [the] speed limit on our economy is too slow … We have to lift [the] growth agenda – cutting compliance costs, speeding up approvals.

Australia to provide Solomon Islands with $740m under landmark agreement
Tess Ikonomou
Australia will provide Solomon Islands $740m over the decade as part of its landmark treaty with the Pacific nation.
Under the agreement, Honiara will not allow its territory to be used for a foreign military base. Developmental assistance will be provided through $670m in grants and $70m in loans.
Australia will help bridge the funding gap for Solomon Islands prime minister Matthew Wale’s national free education policy, a key pledge of his government.
About a third of children in the Solomon Islands don’t go to school due to costs, and only 19% complete year 12.
Money is expected to start flowing to Honiara in January, when Solomon Islands’ parliament returns.
Australia is racing to ensure ordinary people feel the benefits of the deal as soon as possible before expected Chinese-backed attempts to scupper the treaty get under way. The Solomon Islands is expected to face backlash from Beijing over the agreement, as Honiara chooses not to renew its five-year security pact with China when it expires next April.
The treaty with Australia also contains climate commitments.
Funding should be diverted if Closing the Gap principles breached, commissioner says
Australia’s commissioner for Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander children and young people says federal funding for child protection should be diverted from state and territory governments that breach the principles of the National Agreement on Closing the Gap.
At the National Press Club earlier today, commissioner Sue-Anne Hunter outlined concerns of a child protection system “going backwards” in Australia, and said the governments needed to be accountable for “failing systems”.
It comes after the federal minister for Indigenous Australians, Malarndirri McCarthy, withheld about $80m from then Northern Territory Remote Area Investment (NTRAI) between the commonwealth and NT, before the closure of Darwin’s largest Aboriginal homeless shelter.
Hunter said:
[Diverting funding] is a step forward about accountability and it should go to Aboriginal and Torres Strait Islander organisations, to be able to do the work that’s needed.
What I’m finding is when I look at these frameworks and everything that’s decided, is that no one is actually held accountable.
People are more likely to walk through the door of an Aboriginal community-controlled organisation than […] of a mainstream one and get the help that’s required.
