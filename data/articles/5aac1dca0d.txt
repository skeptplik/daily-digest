A CISO who traded enterprise budgets for higher ed on what “creativity within constraints” really means, and the one habit worth stealing from how he leads.
I have known Matt Martin for a few years, and the first thing I tell anyone about him has nothing to do with security. It is that he treats the work as a way to serve other people. I first knew Matt as the security leader who spends his own time flying to Ghana and Uganda to teach executives and students how to protect themselves. That instinct, more than any tool or certification, explains how he runs a security program.
Matt spent two decades running security organizations in financial services, including at LPL Financial, with an enterprise budget and any tool he needed. A few years ago he made a deliberate choice to take on higher ed instead, because he wanted to prove he could build a resilient security program under real constraints. Today he is the Chief Information Security and Privacy Officer at Western Carolina University.
I asked Matt to share his journey from a conference in Accra to a university in the mountains of North Carolina. His story is really about doing more with less, and right now, that is all of us.
Q. You have built real social impact into your career, sharing security knowledge and consultancy with underserved communities. How did that begin?
A. It started unexpectedly. After two decades in financial services I decided to go out on my own. The whole point was to support small and medium organizations, especially in developing countries, that could not afford the security consultancy they needed. I heard about the GC3B conference in Accra, Ghana, which was about building cyber resiliency in developing countries. They only accepted companies, so I applied as the LLC I had just started, and a few weeks later I received a letter from the government of Ghana inviting me.
I flew over not knowing a single person. The first day had a light schedule, so I hired a tour guide and he took me to see Elmina Castle and the Door of No Return. That one day changed my life. Seeing that side of the story of humanity as an adult, after growing up in the American South, connected so much for me about how tied together we all are. I carried that into every meeting at the conference.
Afterwards I developed a partnership with a nonprofit called Africa Digital Rights Hub, and it has since taken me back to run masterclasses for executives and students in Ghana and Uganda. Part of the reason Western Carolina appealed to me was that they wanted to help me expand that work.
Q. You had about three weeks between that invitation from the Ghanaian government and actually getting on the plane. What was it like to just drop everything and go?
A. It was a scramble. Three weeks to get all my immunizations, book everything, and fly to Ghana. Actually, my yellow fever vaccine wasn’t fully effective by the time I landed, I was a couple of days short, so I had to talk my way through the medical booth in the airport before I could even clear immigration.
Funny enough, it’s risk acceptance in real life. I knew my control wasn’t operating effectively yet, I understood the exposure, and I decided to go anyway. That’s the same type of call we make in security every day.
Q. I’ve heard you say that security and GRC have become an elite sport. Job postings demand degrees and a wall of certifications before you can apply, but attackers have none of them. You are largely self-taught. What has that taught you about who belongs in this field?
A. I started with a history degree, and some of the best security people I know do not have a security degree either. Think how this community started. In the beginning you couldn’t go get a degree in cybersecurity, it was creative people figuring it out as they went. There are brilliant people all over the world who could do this work and simply haven’t had the opportunity. That is why the teaching matters so much to me. There is a real chance to democratize security education and take down some of the barriers the industry has put up.
Q. You have a phrase for how you think about the work now, “creativity within constraints.” When you started in the new context of higher ed, what did you assume your constraints would be?
A. I assumed it would be budget. In financial services, I could get almost anything as long as it was reasonable. Budgets are definitely tight in higher ed, but it was not my number one problem. I needed to learn the environment.
A university has done things a certain way for a long time, knowledge is meant to be open and shared, and the place can feel like family. Once I really understood the environment, I could get creative and start designing security that worked with the culture instead of against it.
Q. You’ve been candid with me that you got the first few months wrong, and I think that honesty helps other leaders even more than sharing wins. What happened?
A. I went in thinking I already knew how everything should work, and I started fixing things. I probably pushed harder than I should have before I understood the place. What I should have done was go in to learn, listen more than I talked, and document what I found before I tried to solve anything. Overseeing the security program at a large company does not mean you know how to build one everywhere.
Q. So with a small team and a tight budget, how do you actually run a real program? Walk me through the mechanics.
A. It starts with risk, not compliance. If you do risk well, compliance comes almost for free, because it tells you what you need to do and in what order. So I built a real risk register. I went to the technology leaders first and asked what was broken and what was holding them back, wrote it all down, and mapped it to a framework.
Then the honest problem is capacity. I cannot hire a team of analysts to test every control or spend weeks each year on a self-assessment. Self-attestation is not enough on its own. Anyone who has run a program knows that asking whether a control works can get you different answers depending on who you ask, so I needed real evidence, not opinions. The only way to get that at scale was to embrace automation and continuous monitoring.
Q. You have now lived on both sides. What would you take from higher ed back into a big enterprise?
A. Fiscal discipline, honestly. The habit of asking “do we really need this, or is it a nice-to-have,” and whether a given feature is three million dollars better than what we already have. In large enterprises you also end up with people inserting themselves into processes to justify a role, and everything takes longer. But look, every company of every size has something it could do better. I’ve really embraced how scarcity forces you to be more efficient.
Q. How can you judge whether someone will make a good CISO?
A. This one question tells me what I need to know: Do you know how your company makes money? Can you actually tell me how your organization makes money, or in a university, how the institution succeeds? Once you understand that, you can prioritize risk in a way that matters to the business. If you cannot answer it, you are going to protect the wrong things.
Q. What kind of CISO actually thrives in a place like higher ed?
A. You have to want to get your hands dirty. If you just want to run a team of forty and just go to meetings, it will not work, because your team is smaller and you will be doing some of the work yourself. You have to be creative enough to build things that are not the industry standard. And most of all, you have to see the business-enablement side of security, not only the technical side. You are engaging with the colleges, the chancellor, even the athletics department, and your job is to enable them. If you come in 100% security or bust, you will not last long in higher ed.
Q. Matt, thank you for this. What is on your radar right now? Is there anything you are most excited to build next?
A. Giving students a real place to learn. I want more working CISOs to share with students what the career actually looks like. Where I would love to take it is a hands-on cybersecurity lab on campus, for students to get real-world practice. I would also love to keep building the connections with universities in Africa, so students can learn from each other in both directions.
Ground truth for your program
Matt’s whole approach is a posture, not a tactic. He leads as a teacher and a collaborator, and treats security as a way to help organizations succeed rather than a roadblock.
The lesson I would take to any team, at any budget: name your real constraint before you invest against the wrong one. Matt walked in certain it was budget, and it turned out to be understanding the environment. Assume it’s money and you pour your limited resources into tools, when the real problem was one money could not solve. Get the constraint right, though, and every dollar and every control is aimed at a real business objective, which is what makes a program effective at any size.
So before your next purchase or project, ask yourself two questions: What do I actually believe my top constraint is, and how do I know? And am I about to spend against it, or around it?
