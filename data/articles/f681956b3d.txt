“A handful of lime and another of sulfur in fine powder.
Place them in a vessel containing strong vinegar or the urine of a small child.
Heat it from below, until the liquid appears like blood.
Decant this properly, and use.”
Unknown, Leiden Papyrus X, c. 300 CE
In 1827, amid the dark, silent balm of an Egyptian night, a graverobber pulled a treasure from the sands. At first, the raider was frustrated; scavenging the tombs near Thebes, he had hoped to uncover a priceless heirloom, or a nugget of gold that would sparkle with lustre in the starlight. Instead, he had found only a clutch of rolled scrolls, made of papyrus and sealed against the elements.
For a moment, he considered casting the documents aside and leaving them for the desert. Then greed got the better of him. Westerners loved all things Egyptian, he reasoned. Since the French had arrived with Napoleon, the new field of “Egyptology” had swept the continent, capturing the imagination of scholars and the wealthy. It was almost as popular as the new science of “chemistry”. Surely, if he took his find to the market, he’d find some gullible fool willing to buy it.
The buyer’s name was Jean d’Anastasy, and he was anything but a fool. Born in Greece as the son of a Damascus merchant who had won and lost his fortune in the Napoleonic Wars, d’Anastasy had used his family’s connections to set himself up as a grain merchant in Alexandria, before scheming his way into being named the local Consul General for Sweden and Norway.
When d’Anastasy wasn’t trading – or rescuing enslaved girls during the Greek War of Independence against the Ottoman Empire – he ran a side-hustle, “procuring” relics from Ancient Egypt and selling them to the salons of Europe: statues to the British Museum in London; rings and jewels to the Louvre in Paris; even a pair of sphinxes that still sit on the banks of the Neva River in St Petersburg. He quickly saw the value of the papers, paid for them, and shipped them back to Europe. Some went to Stockholm, and some to Leiden University in the Netherlands.
Among the Leiden trove was one of the most remarkable finds in history: a papyrus scroll with a list of recipes, each with crystal-clear instructions written in Greek. While we have damaged cuneiform tablets and later copies of older works, the Leiden Papyrus X, written around 300 CE, is the oldest surviving alchemy book in history. It is a guide for Egyptian artisans to create wondrous riches, seemingly out of nothing. There are methods on how to polish silverware, cheap alternatives for making knock-off purple robes, and how to create gold lettering by mixing saffron and tortoise bile. These are all real instructions, and all recipes that work (even if, today, there are cheaper ways to get the same result).
Among them is the greatest secret in alchemy. It’s called the “invention of sulfur water” and is the recipe at the start of this chapter. Sulfur and lime (calcium oxide, not the fruit) are gardening supplies that you can buy in a hardware store, and if you don’t have a small child that needs a wee to hand, strong vinegar from the supermarket will work just as well. While you shouldn’t try this at home, the result, after you heat the mix for an hour, is a red-brown goop and a kitchen that reeks of rotten eggs. These are calcium polysulfides, making up lime sulfur. It’s a nasty substance – an alkali that can cause skin irritation and even blindness if you get it in your eyes. Today, we use this for pest control, but in ancient times it was how you created gold. Well, the appearance of gold. Once you’ve got your “sulfur water”, any metal object submersed – a coin, nail or paperclip – will gain a thin, yellowy film coating.
The Leiden Papyrus X isn’t just the oldest alchemy book in history. It’s a tried-and-tested guide for tricksters. We may not know who wrote the document, but we do know that if you had walked into the author’s workshop 1,700 years ago, you’d have been swindled. Or that’s how it seems. The truth, as we’ll learn, is that something else was going on that’s even more fascinating. But we’re getting ahead of ourselves.
The Leiden Papyrus X isn’t what most people think of when they picture alchemy. We think of it as a pseudoscience, a blend of religious beliefs, ancient superstition and gilded dreams, with only the occasional glimpse of something that might work underneath. If you subscribe to the traditional view, this is the best you can hope for when it comes to studying any chemistry earlier than about 1650. Before the Enlightenment, there were only wizards, witches and wishful thinking. Then suddenly, whoosh, in a flash of gunpowder, a crackle of electricity and bubbling fizz from a test tube, a new science arrived to change our world.
This book turns that idea on its head. No one person created chemistry, and even if they had, it certainly wouldn’t have been some white European in a powdered wig. Chemistry didn’t appear overnight and there is no one moment when it came into existence. It was a gradual transformation that built on millennia of knowledge. Choosing to ignore chemistry during the Age of Alchemy does a disservice to thousands of men and women, across the world, who guided us from the first campfire to the modern age.
This book is divided into three parts. In the first, we look at how early chemistry worked, from the Stone Age up to the emergence of alchemy, and how it set the basic principles for what alchemy, and later chemistry, would become. In the second, we go on a trip around the world. Practically every history of alchemy just focuses on the western traditions – mainly because they are the best studied. But we’re going to look at what was happening with chemistry in the Islamic world, China, the Indian subcontinent and even the Americas. Some of this is alchemy, and some of it isn’t, but it all plays a role in the tapestry of science. Finally, the third section returns to Europe, where we’ll look at how alchemy changed (or, to use the alchemical term, transmuted), over the course of several hundred years, into the science we know today. My journey has led to travels across six continents and taken me on adventures through deserts and jungles, tombs and tomes, and some of our most fabled cities. I have explored old roads and met the researchers busy paving new ones. The result is a story that is both a mirror to progress and a love letter to science, one that offers a glimpse into the past and shows how it all connects to our daily lives, often in surprising and roundabout ways. Chemistry is everywhere and is linked to everything. It is painfully human. It is filled with our mistakes, beliefs and hubris. It showcases our noble endeavours, destructive potential and indomitable spirit. And it allows us to appreciate how science, whether through petty rivalries, oppression and colonisation, or religious fervour, has always been political.
