Early warning signs could alert people that they're at risk of heart disease and give them time to make lifestyle changes, the researchers said.
Warning signs that someone will have a heart attack or stroke may appear up to 30 years beforehand, new research has found.
Cardiovascular diseases are the leading cause of death worldwide, with an estimated 19.8 million people dying of a heart attack, stroke or another heart problem in 2022. Most cardiovascular diseases can be prevented by tackling lifestyle and environmental risk factors such as unhealthy diets, smoking and air pollution, and early detection can help funnel people into treatment and prevent deaths.
“For the vast majority of people, heart attacks and strokes are completely preventable. But many of us don’t realise we are at risk until it’s too late,” said Bryan Williams, chief scientific and medical officer at the British Heart Foundation, which funded the study.
Researchers from the University of Edinburgh tracked nearly 114,000 people’s health over three decades beginning in the 1980s. None of them had heart disease at that time, but nearly 17,000 people eventually had a heart attack, stroke or died of a heart problem.
Among those who developed heart problems, warning signs often emerged decades beforehand, according to the study, which was published in the journal JAMA Network Open.
For example, systolic blood pressure — the pressure in the arteries when the heart pumps blood — was consistently higher in people who later developed heart problems compared with those who did not.
Blood biomarkers were another early signal. Changes in NT-proBNP — a protein fragment used to assess heart failure — were apparent 10 years beforehand.
Other early warning signs included kidney function, physical activity, diabetes and a higher body mass index (BMI), which is a measure of obesity. Several are already known risk factors for heart disease, along with high blood pressure and elevated cholesterol, but the study shows how these risk factors progress over time.
“This study adds to growing evidence that red flags for cardiovascular disease appear decades before it arrives,” Williams said.
Notably, the study did not analyse risk factors by ethnicity, and more research would be needed to determine any differences across groups.
Even so, Williams said the findings could help doctors identify patients at risk of a heart attack or stroke later in life, using simple tools like a blood pressure reading or blood test.
“Knowledge like this could empower people to make lifestyle changes or start treatment while they are still relatively young, which might reduce their risk, prevent a heart attack or stroke, and rewrite their future,” Williams said.
