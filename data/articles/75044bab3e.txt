Ask many IT teams how they would recognize ransomware, and the answer is still “when files start changing extensions.” By then, the intrusion may already include credential theft, privilege escalation, defense evasion, recovery sabotage, lateral movement and data exfiltration.
Encryption is often a late-stage action, not the beginning of the attack. In data-extortion attacks it never happens at all: the operators steal data and threaten to leak it without encrypting anything.
Recent attacks against on-premises SharePoint servers illustrate that sequence. After exploiting internet-facing systems, operators used stolen credentials and legitimate administration tools to move through victim environments before deploying ransomware.
The practical lesson is simple: defenders need to detect the behaviors that precede encryption, not only the encryptor itself.

Five high-value signals deserve immediate attention:
- Defense tampering: Attempts to stop security services, modify protection agents, load vulnerable drivers or clear event logs.
- Privilege escalation and lateral movement: Unexpected privileged accounts, remote-execution tools spreading across hosts or unusual server-to-server remote access.
- Data exfiltration: Unusual outbound transfers from systems that do not normally upload large volumes of data.
- Recovery sabotage: Deletion of shadow copies, backup catalogs or recovery options with tools such as vssadmin.exe, wbadmin.exe or bcdedit.exe.
- Pre-encryption file behavior: Rapid file enumeration, repeated read-write-rename activity and encryption-like changes across local or network locations.
How do ransomware gangs typically gain initial access to corporate networks?
Ransomware attacks rarely begin with the ransomware payload itself. Operators first need an initial foothold, and common entry paths include exploitation of vulnerable internet-facing systems, abuse of stolen credentials, and social engineering.
An unpatched externally accessible service can provide a route into the environment, while compromised credentials can allow an attacker to enter through legitimate remote-access or cloud services.
Social engineering adds another path by targeting users and the processes around identity and access.
Once attackers establish access, the initial compromise can become the starting point for credential theft, privilege escalation, defense evasion and lateral movement.
Organizations should reduce unnecessary internet exposure, prioritize patching of high-risk systems, strengthen identity and access controls, and protect email and collaboration channels.
Monitoring endpoints for suspicious post-compromise behavior provides another layer when preventive controls do not stop the initial intrusion.

What should an organization do immediately after detecting ransomware?
The first actions should limit spread, preserve evidence and create a safe path to recovery. Organizations should pre-authorize automated actions for clearly defined, high-severity conditions and test them before an incident.
- Isolate the affected workload while preserving the management channel needed for investigation.
- Stop malicious processes and quarantine confirmed malicious files.
- Protect evidence, including process trees, command lines, network connections and relevant logs.
- Identify and disable the compromised account, token or remote-access path.
- Patch the exploited vulnerability or close the exposed service after containment.
- Restore affected files or workloads from a known-good recovery point, then validate the recovered environment before reconnecting it.
- Escalate to incident-response, legal, privacy, insurance and communications stakeholders according to the organization’s response plan.
