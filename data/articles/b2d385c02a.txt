The viral ads that propelled Scott Colom's Senate campaign in Mississippi
As his narrow lead in the Mississippi Senate race expanded on Thursday, Democrat Scott Colom told NBC News that his party had all but abandoned the state before his campaign.
“First of all, let’s not give them too much credit; they haven’t been trying,” Colom said. I’ve been in Mississippi for 15 years, most of the time the Democrats conceded the state. And my frustration with that is, 38% of the population is African American; they’re very loyal Democrats. They vote 90-95% Democrat. There’s a lot of white rural Democrats across Mississippi.”
“That’s a shame, right, because morally, how could you not compete in a state where people are denying folks health care? How could you not compete in a state where folks are, you know, getting betrayed on tariffs? Farmers are getting betrayed, consumers, small businesses. How could you not compete in a state where voting rights are being attacked left and right?” he added. “When you say, ‘Listen, I can’t win those voters’, what you’re saying is ‘I don’t care enough about those voters’. And when you show voters you don’t care, they don’t show up. So what I did is I showed up, and turns out when you show up, people start to believe.”
Colom’s Senate campaign, which benefitted from very little help from the national party, was propelled, in part, by a series of viral ads that he thanked his campaign’s content creators for producing this week, as his lead in the vote count expanded.
One ad, released in mid-October, racked up more than 20 million views across social networks. It seized on a remark by the Republican incumbent, Cindy Hyde-Smith, who tried to dodge a question from a TV anchor about the high price of beef by saying: “You have so many proteins to choose from”.
The ad, which begins with that gaffe, then cuts to three Mississippi voters responding to the senator. “Pick another protein?” a man grilling burgers says. “Shit, I’d rather pick another senator instead.”
“Pick another protein?” a woman eating says next. “She done lost her damn mind. I’d rather pick another senator.”
“Pick another protein?” a woman in a grocery store says. “What the f-”.
According to Colom, the idea for the ad came from Patrick Gaspard, a former US ambassador to South Africa and adviser to president Barack Obama and it was directed and edited by Ryan Sayles. Sayles, Colom wrote this week, “should be considered the breakout-star of this cycle. He’s going to be a giant in the media consultant game. He did GAME CHANGING work with SO little.”
Another comedic ad, a Halloween parody of the film Sinners called Sinators, was written and directed by Colom’s twin brother, Andrew Colom, who also starred in it along with the candidate.
At the end of the campaign, Colom starred in a much more serious ad, showing the candidate at the Mississippi home where the civil rights activist Medgar Evers was murdered in 1963 for registering Black voters.
“I’ve been a prosecutor for 10 years and unfortunately I’ve stood in a lot of houses where something terrible happened,” Colom says in the ad. “This isn’t any house. This isn’t any tragedy. This is where Medger Evers died. He fought for this country and Normandy. He came home to Mississippi and they wouldn’t let him vote. So he organized, helping other people register.”
“He was killed for helping Mississippian vote,” Colom says at the end of the ad. “On Tuesday, use your vote and honor everyone who died for that right.”
Democrat Scott Colom expands lead in Mississippi Senate race as provisional ballots are counted
In Mississippi, Democrat Scott Colom’s lead in the race for US Senate expanded to 1,813 votes over incumbent Republican senator Cindy Hyde-Smith as a small number of provisional or affidavit ballots were counted on Thursday.
Up to up to 20,000 outstanding ballots remain to be counted in the state, including late-arriving mail ballots that are valid if they were postmarked by election day and arrive by 10 November, and an estimated 8,000 to 12,000 affidavit ballots, which are those set aside from the initial count due to technical errors, like a lack of photo ID or an incorrect home address, that voters have until next Tuesday to correct. Such errors on ballots can often be corrected through a process known as curing.
Colom told reporters earlier on Thursday that he is “very confident” that the remaining ballots will add to his lead. “Anybody out there who does an analysis of this knows where this is going. The affidavits always move in the Democrat’s favor in Mississippi.”
“I am overwhelmed by the outpouring of support for my campaign as we fight to make sure every lawful ballot is counted,” Colom wrote on social media. “My campaign, alongside the DSCC and Elias Law Group, Lisa Ross, and our great team of local attorneys, are leading the effort to ensure every eligible voter is heard and I am confident that when votes are counted, we will flip this seat.”
The Mississippi Republican Party chair, Mike Hurst, disagreed. “We believe if the laws are followed to a T and these 20,000 outstanding ballots are counted, that senator Cindy Hyde-Smith will be reelected,” Hurst said at a news conference.
When he was pressed to say whether that meant he would accept a Colom win, should the count go go in the Democrat’s favor, Hurst said: “Let me be clear: there is no election-denying going on here. If the laws are followed and the voters spoke, that is it and Mississippi Republicans will accept that.”
Gavin Newsom, California’s governor, commuted the death sentences of more than 500 people on the state’s death row on Thursday, a move that ensures they can never be executed under future administrations.
The Democrat’s mass commutation at the end of his term has converted all of the state’s death sentences to life without the possibility of parole, the largest clearing of any US death row in modern history.
His decision affects 529 men and 19 women who were sentenced to death in the state, making up the largest death row in the western hemisphere, the governor said in a letter announcing his decision.
“I take this action because I firmly believe that state-sanctioned killing is incompatible with California’s values, undermines the foundations of our legal system, and diminishes each of us,” he wrote. “I know we cannot execute people in our imperfect legal system and remain true to our core values, first among them justice and human decency.”
Newsom, considered a probable candidate for president in 2028, has long opposed the death penalty and issued a moratorium on executions in 2019. He also dismantled the state’s physical death row chamber in the infamous San Quentin prison, transferring people with death sentences to other facilities where they had more freedom of movement and access to programs – for some, a dramatic change after decades isolated in solitary cells.
