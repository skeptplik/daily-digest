Most police want ban on New Year Eve fireworks
A survey by police union GdP has shown that some 69% of police in Germany are in favor of a ban on private fireworks on New Year's Eve.
According to the survey of 9,000 members, more than 70% feel that the situation for police on the evening would improve if such a ban were in place.
Altogether 54% of respondents who had been on duty during the past 10 years of New Year's Eve festivities reported having been in a dangerous or threatening situation caused by fireworks.
A whole 195 respondents said they had been injured by fireworks on the evening.
The poll was prompted by an open letter to Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt by a group calling itself #böllerciao (Goodbye firecrackers) with some 1.17 million signatories. The name is a play on the famous anti-Nazi song Bella Ciao.
The group includes 86 organizations, among them Environmental Action Germany (DUH) and the GdP, who have called for a ban on private fireworks to protect people, emergency personnel, animals and the environment.
Rwanda return hubs in focus as higher Interior Ministry budget accepted
Plans foreseeing a rise of €1 billion ($1.1 billion) in the budget for the Interior Ministry have been approved by the three parties in Germany's coalition government, after much debate centering on something that makes up a relatively small proportion of that money: the controversial planned centers for rejected asylum seekers in Rwanda.
According to Klaus-Peter Willsch of Chancellor Friedrich Merz's Christian Democrats (CDU), just €5 million are earmarked for the so-called return hubs, shared between several European countries.
He said the €20 million that has often been cited as being for the Rwanda migration centers was not only for the hubs in that country, but for other potential projects in third countries as well.
Despite the relatively small amounts of money involved, the Rwanda plans devised by Interior Minister Alexander Dobrindt of the Bavarian Christian Social Union (CSU) along with Austria, Denmark, the Netherlands and Greece came under fire not only from coalition partners the Social Democrats (SPD) but particularly from opposition parties.
The Green Party and the Left Party both rejected the idea for ethical reasons, with Green lawmaker Leon Eckert speaking of "state-organized human trafficking," while Dietmar Bartsch of the Left pointed out that the deal was being made with an authoritarian regime.
The far-right Alternative for Germany (AfD), for its part, said it found the plans too soft, with parliamentarian Marcus Bühl calling them a "smoke screen," as just 20 rejected asylum-seekers from Germany were to be initially sent to Rwanda under the scheme.
Dobrindt defended the scheme, saying that the opposition was trying to stir up controversy about a concept that had been approved at European level. He also called criticism of the political system in Rwanda inappropriate, citing a school for Afghan girls that had been set up in the country amid the ban on secondary education for females by the Taliban government.
The interior minister also praised the additional funds given to his ministry as a contribution to Germany's defense against hybrid attacks from Russia, with a rise in the budgets of both the IT security and the civic protection departments.
The draft 2027 budget, which was approved by the conservative CDU/CSU bloc and the SPD, however includes less funding for the BAMF migration authority (from €999 million in 2026 to €994 million) and for integration aid for migrants and refugees (down €328 million to €1.07 billion).
Trial of alleged Russian spy delayed over health issues
The trial of a German-Ukrainian woman due to go on trial on Thursday on charges of spying for Russia was delayed in the morning after the accused developed health problems that required treatment by emergency medical workers, a spokeswoman for a Berlin court said.
It was not clear whether her trial could go ahead as planned.
The 57-year-old woman has been accused of passing information to an officer from Russia's GRU intelligence agency who was posing as a diplomat at the Russian Embassy in Berlin.
Prosecutors allege that she used contacts with current and former German Defense Ministry officials to collect information on military aid for Ukraine, drone test sites and the arms industry.
She also faces accusation of having provided her alleged handler access to key political events in Berlin.
The woman was arrested in late January, with the alleged involvement of the Russian Embassy causing considerable outrage in Berlin.
Russia's ambassador was summoned over the incident, and the handler — identified by news weekly Der Spiegel as the embassy's deputy military attache — was expelled from Germany.
Parliament continues budget debates
The Bundestag is conducting its concluding debates on the budget for 2027 ahead of a final vote scheduled for Friday.
On Thursday, the focus will initially be on the individual budget for the Interior Ministry led by Alexander Dobrindt of the conservative Bavarian Christian Social Union (CSU), the sister party of Chancellor Friedrich Merz's Christian Democrats (CDU).
The government foresees that the ministry, which is responsible for the federal police and the domestic intelligence agency BfV, among other things, will receive some €16.8 billion in 2027 ($18.43 billion), around €1 billion more than this year.
Lawmakers will then go on to debate the individual budgets for the Economics and Energy Ministry, the Ministry for Housing, Urban Development and Building, the Ministry of Food and Agriculture and the Ministry for Education, Family Affairs, Senior Citizens, Women and Youth.
Opening the debates on Wednesday, Merz described the tight budget as an "accurate reflection" of the "difficult situation as a country — geopolitically, technologically, and demographically" — that Germany finds itself in.
"We will no longer be able to afford everything with tax revenue that we have become all too accustomed to for far too long," Merz added.
