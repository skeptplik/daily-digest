These are the major developments from the closing phase of the UN's COP31 Climate Summit in Antalya, Turkey, on Friday, November 20 and Saturday, November 21:
COP31 talks go on — we're taking a break
The UN climate summit in Antalya, Turkey, has gone into overtime, with countries still divided over key issues, including fossil fuels and climate financing.
The talks were scheduled to end on Friday evening, but negotiators have yet to reach an agreement. Germany and several other countries are pushing for a clear commitment to phase out fossil fuels, while poorer nations are demanding more concrete financial support to cope with climate change.
Negotiations are expected to continue into the weekend. We are pausing our live coverage for now and will bring you further updates as talks progress.
WATCH — Turkey's beaches are becoming too hot for sea turtles
While Turkey hosts COP31 in Antalya, a growing climate threat is unfolding on its Mediterranean beaches. Rising sand temperatures are causing most newly hatched sea turtles to be born female, raising concerns about the long-term survival of local populations.
Conservationists warn that climate change, combined with pollution, coastal development and fishing-related injuries, is putting increasing pressure on the species.
DW correspondent Julia Hahn follows the people working to protect Turkey's iconic loggerhead turtles and shows how the climate crisis is already affecting wildlife far beyond the COP31 conference halls.
WATCH — COP climate summit under scrutiny over fossil fuel influence
The UN climate summit in Antalya is drawing criticism from activists and campaigners who question whether global climate talks can deliver meaningful change while fossil fuel interests remain influential. Turkey and Australia, both under scrutiny for their energy policies, are leading this year's conference.
Activists, including Greta Thunberg, argue COP summits have become increasingly ineffective, while others defend them as a vital space to pressure governments. Climate policy experts say the talks have helped reduce projected warming, but stress that real progress depends on action taken by countries between summits, not just agreements reached at them.
WATCH — Maldives environment minister: 'We are not going to live in the dark'
For the Maldives, climate change is already affecting everyday life. Heavy rainfall has forced people from their homes, warmer oceans are damaging coral reefs, and changes in fish stocks are putting pressure on the country's fishing industry. Rising sea levels pose another threat to the low-lying island nation. Meanwhile, global greenhouse gas emissions continue to rise, despite more than three decades of international climate negotiations.
At COP31 in Antalya, Turkey, the Maldives' climate minister Ali Sharif discusses why progress has been so slow, what really happens during climate negotiations, and why moving away from fossil fuels is not as straightforward as it might seem.
For island nations like the Maldives, switching to renewable energy comes with practical and financial challenges. So what would it take to accelerate the transition? And can the UN climate process still deliver meaningful results?
COP32 hosts Ethiopia call the conferences a relay, not a single race
The hosts of next year's COP, Ethiopia, found a nationally tailored sporting metaphor to sum up the difficult, ongoing, sometimes tortuous process that the global environmental meetings can prove to be.
Speaking at the end of Friday's plenary to confirm Addis Ababa's intentions to host next year's summit, Gedion Timothewos, the foreign minister and upcoming president of COP32, made reference to Ethiopia's longstanding success in endurance running in track and field.
Referring to the COP process as a marathon, not a sprint, Timothewos said the country was looking forward to picking up the baton from Turkey and leading the next leg of the relay, taking the ongoing process forward into 2027.
His comments came during a late-night plenary session, but more talks are still expected in Antalya.
Late-night plenary session convenes after delay
Shortly after 9 p.m. local time in Antalya, delegates gathered for a plenary session, well past the official closing time.
The Turkish presidency's opening address was partly drowned out by a protester shouting objections in English such as "you are killing the future," and gaining applause within the chamber.
"We've been negotiating for days to find the common ground," COP31 President Murat Kurum, Turkey's environment minister, told the plenary. He hailed the progress made and said the world "waits for good news from this room."
But the plenary session noted how several of the more ambitious aspects of a political declaration remained incomplete, with no agreement yet reached at the summit.
Several national delegates spoke during the plenary to bemoan the elusive progress or even backsliding during the two weeks of negotiations.
The evening's session, however, has been dealing with technical issues, and the possibility that discussions continue into the weekend remains.

Activist Menotti: Europe has 'learned a few of the Trump tactics'
Veteran climate justice advocate Victor Menotti of the Demand Climate Justice NGO told DW that European governments appeared to be copying US President Donald Trump's negotiating playbook at COP this year.
"We know the US isn't here, but we can see that Germany and other European governments have learned a few of the Trump tactics, maybe in the trade disputes with the US," Menotti said. "And we see them applying here in Antalya, where it's like: Don't admit to anything. Try to dilute your responsibilities. Weaken whatever obligations you have. And then take even more."
He argued these countries were also expecting unreasonable or unfeasible commitments from developing countries, for instance restrictions on what they can spend money on and obligations to report on their expenditures.
"Germany is subsidizing quite a bit of coal still and fossil fuels, LNG expansion infrastructure," he said, arguing that wealthier countries' energy spending also had room for improvement.
"We need the North to act first," Menotti said. "The deal from Paris, the deal from Rio 30 years ago, is that the North will lead. But yet we see too much bullying of the global South and not enough leadership like they agreed to."
Talks formally enter overtime as scheduled close elapses
You can cross off another box on your COP31 bingo cards, dear readers.
As of the top of this hour, the session has officially gone past its scheduled end time of 1500 GMT/UTC on Friday, or 6 p.m. local time on site in Turkey.
This is something of an annual tradition at COP summits, which are always scheduled to finish at the end of a working week but also almost invariably run into the closing weekend. Delegates and members of the media tend to book their travel plans with this in mind.
'Electrification': What to know about the revised draft declaration
The revised draft declaration, which critics have slammed as "watered down" due to the omission of fossil fuels, highlights the "electrification" of the global economy as a central pillar of the energy transition.
"Electrification is the key to transitioning away from fossil fuels," Australian Climate Minister Chris Bowen told reporters.
What are the key points to know about the "35-by-35" electrification target?
- The draft wants to increase electricity's share of global energy use from 23% to 35% by 2035
- The draft highlights electrification as a way to cut emissions and boost energy security — while omitting prior mentions of fossil fuels
- It aims to shift more transport, heating, cooling and industrial activity onto electricity, allowing those sectors to be powered by renewables such as wind, solar and hydro power, rather than by burning coal, oil or gas
But the text does not specify what energy sources would supply the electricity. Some countries are demanding that the text call for "clean" electrification to clearly favor renewable sources over coal, oil or gas.
Bowen, who has been trying to secure compromises, pledged to continue working over the "coming hours, for as long as it takes, to get the job done."
"People have strong views of course — I am not stepping away from that. But no one has said to me let's give up and go home," Bowen said.
Climate Action Network blames rich nations for 'worst COP ever'
The Climate Action Network (CAN), a global network of over 2,500 civil society organizations, has accused wealthy developed nations, led by the EU, "of blocking every track" towards a substantive agreement on fighting climate change and curbing fossil fuel use.
At its final press conference of the summit, CAN executive director Tasneem Essop said the group was "rejecting what is currently on the table."
"There is nothing (...) that protects people or addresses the priorities of the Global South. In fact, the opposite is true. The current text has opened the door to the most harmful and dangerous options of any COP so far," Essop said.
She pointed out that this year's COP had the highest ever number of lobbyists for fossil fuels, for AI, for CCUS (Carbon Capture, Utilization, and Storage) and for CDR (Carbon Dioxide Removal), which only represent corporate interests and do not have the interests of the people from developing nations who are most at risk from global warming in mind.
"What we’re leaving this COP with is decisions made to promote the interests of those corporations making profits from the destruction of people," Essop said.
Mohamed Adow from Power Shift Africa, which is also part of CAN, said: "The COP Presidents want to tell us this has been a great COP. Well, I can tell you without any doubt that this has been the worst COP ever.
"Overseeing a failing COP is bad enough, but trying to sell it as a success rubs salt into the wounds of those people who need it to deliver."
Adow cautioned that communities that live with the constant threat of drought, floods and failed harvests were "promised action."
"They cannot survive on an annual renewal of promises," Adow said.
He laid the blame squarely with the world's most developed nations: "They demand action from developing countries, then block public finance, block Just Transition, block climate finance, and they expect ambition. You cannot keep your foot on the brake and complain that countries are not moving fast enough."
'Are you serious?' Youth climate activist questions rich nations' commitment
Xiye Bastida, a New York-based youth activist from Mexico, spoke to DW's Katharina Schantz to describe her frustration over the lack of progress and ambition on display at this year's COP Summit, which is the seventh the 24-year-old has attended.
Bastida, who has played a leading role in the Fridays for Future climate movement in New York, said she did not think she "could be more disappointed" at how climate targets are "being rolled back."
"This is really the time of reckoning," Bastida said.
She explained that she had changed her flight plans to be able to stay until the end, because she feels "we have to be here. We at least to be witnesses of how they're taking things away."
Bastida pointed to the hypocrisy from wealthy nations, who have blamed some from the Global South for not wanting the summit's joint declaration to mention fossil fuels.
"But they (the Global North) are the ones not bringing the money, and they're the ones who are not bringing the ambition.
"And they want to show themselves as the leaders. But if you want to mention fossil fuels so bad, where's the money for the transition? Where are the roadmaps?
"Are you serious?" Bastida concluded.
'We fear the last couple of weeks will be just a talk show': Sierra Leone spokesperson tells DW
The new draft declaration, which, as we've reported, has been panned by the EU and civil society, has also disappointed some of the world's least developed countries (LDCs) that are most vulnerable to climate change.
The LDC Group, which comprises 44 of the world's least wealthy nations, came into this year's COP summit hoping for stronger climate action than what has been achieved so far.
"It’s a moment of anticipation," Lamin Sheriff, a press attache for the Sierra Leone government, told DW's Katharina Schantz.
"Ministers from LDCs have requests on the table for the presidency. So whilst they wait on what the outcome would be, everybody is waiting patiently. So the moment is really: we are hopeful for the future, but we are also worried because if proper care is not taken, all what we have debated about here in the last couple of weeks will just be a talk show (…) We can't do anything at the moment."
