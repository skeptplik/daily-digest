It is harvest time in rural Wisconsin, and the Badger state’s most vibrant crop is painting the rolling countryside a brilliant, unmistakable red.
For nearly three decades, Nicole Hansen has been farming cranberries here. Wisconsin produces more than 60% of the nation’s supply. On a crisp fall morning, we waded into a marsh with her to round up the floating harvest.
“We need to corral all these berries,” she said.
Cranberries are one of the few fruits native to North America. Contrary to popular belief, they do not grow submerged in water; instead, they thrive on low-lying vines in dry soil. But when harvest time arrives, the growing beds are flooded. Specialized machines shake the fruit loose, and the buoyant berries rise to the surface.
Growing and harvesting cranberries relies on a highly choreographed combination of machines and manual labor, but the process is ultimately driven by Mother Nature. “So we are checking the weather, I don’t know, five to 10 times a day because everything we do is controlled by the weather,” Hansen said.
Across the US, that weather is growing increasingly erratic, due in large part to the human-caused climate crisis.
Shifting seasons and unpredictable extremes
In cranberry-producing states such as Massachusetts and New Jersey, record-breaking summer heatwaves frequently trigger fruit scalding, in which berries literally bake on the vine before they can be harvested. At the same time, increasingly humid conditions and heavy rainfall during the growing season create stagnant, moisture-rich environments that invite fungal pathogens, resulting in devastating crop rots.
In Wisconsin, the threats often emerge after the harvest is done. To protect the plants from winter’s bitter cold and damaging winds, growers spray water over the vines, building up several inches of ice that act as a natural thermal blanket.
“So if you don’t protect them, they do get cold damage,” Amaya Atucha, a fruit scientist at the University of Wisconsin-Madison, explained.
Atucha spends a lot of time thinking about cranberries and the changing climate affecting them. Data from Climate Central shows that winters in the upper midwest are among the fastest-warming in the nation. Cranberry-growing regions now experience an average of 14 more warm winter days than they did in 1970, triggering erratic cycles of thawing and freezing.
“We know for certain the climate is changing,” Atucha said. She pointed to recent years in which unusually warm February temperatures melted protective ice sheets prematurely, leaving vulnerable vines exposed to sudden deep freezes.
Innovation in the bogs
To keep this seasonal favorite on holiday tables, scientists and growers are racing to adapt. Researchers are leveraging artificial intelligence to identify and breed new, resilient crop varieties capable of withstanding extreme weather and disease. Meanwhile, growers are integrating advanced technology into their daily operations, utilizing drones to precisely apply fertilizers and monitor temperatures across their marshes and bogs.
“When you have a system that works and then out of nowhere you change the rules of the game, then you need to adjust,” Atucha said.
Adapting to change is simply part of the job description for modern farmers. And on a perfect autumn morning, surrounded by glassy waters and floating red cranberries, Hansen acknowledged the pull of this crimson tide.
“It’s amazing,” she told us. “It’s a great way to raise a family.”
