How security officials had concerns about Mandleson's 'overseas contacts'
Here is one document from the bundle. It is an email sent by Ian Collard, the chief property and security officer at the Foreign Office, after Peter Mandelson’s secruity vetting (DV – developed vetting) had been approved.
It shows that there were still concerns about his “overseas contacts” (ie, some of his foreign business friends), and that his accesss to the most sensitive information would depend on what was subsequently decided in relation to those.
A transcription of that second email is below:
From: Ian Collard * Sent: 30 January 2025 10:12 To: Corin Robertson; JCS Subject: Personal/LPM
OFFICIAL-SENSITIVE - HR - MANAGEMENT
Dear Corin, *JCS (for Olly),
An update. This information is provided in strictest confidence, to respect vetting confidence principles, because Corin and Olly are directly in my line management chain as Chief Security Officer.
ESND have approved the DV clearance of Lord Mandelson. He was sent notification and his DV certificate by email (on Osprey) on the evening of 29 January 2025. It will be valid for five years.
As part of the usual clearance process, UKSV identified some areas in his application for ESND to review:
- I understand that Lord Mandelson’s private sector engagements are being managed by HRD and Legal Directorate through the conflict of interest process.
- With regard to personal conduct, I understand that Lord Mandelson has received a letter from Mervyn Thomas, informing him of his responsibilities as an FCDO employee, including under the Diplomatic Service Regulations.
- Matters pertaining to his overseas contacts will certainly be reviewed by the STRAP Authorities. I am unable to assure about the decision they will take. Their decision will determine the access that Lord Mandelson has to the most sensitive information and meetings.
ESND shared with Lord Mandelson (and separately with *JCS in Americas Directorate) the STRAP application forms, which we have asked Lord Mandelson to complete as an urgent priority. We will seek to expedite the processing of his application as soon as the completed forms are returned to us.
Best wishes, Ian

How Mandelson as ambasador sought to arrange 'under radar' meeting involving ex-lobbying colleague and Trump adviser
Henry Dyer
Henry Dyer is a Guardian investigations correspondent.
Peter Mandelson sought to organise an “under the radar” meeting in Washington with an economic adviser to Donald Trump and a member of his former lobbying firm, Global Counsel. He did so while ambassador in April 2025 and still holding his shares in Global Counsel.
Mandelson wanted to hold a lunch to discuss “alternative options” to the World Trade Organisation and on how “likeminded partners come to terms with China and its practices”.
After discussing dates with American counterparts, he forwarded the emails to a Global Counsel employee. He asked: “Do you have date preference for trade think in?”
How Mandelson's failure to sell his stake in lobbying firm after becoming ambassador concerned Foreign Office
Olly Robbins, the permanent secretary at the Foreign Office, was concerned that Peter Mandleson did not sell his shares in Global Counsel, the lobbying company he founded, when he was appointed ambassador, one letter in the file reveals.
It was sent in March, after Mandelson had been in post for more than a month. The Foreign Office thought the best solution to the conflict of interest this created would have been for Global Counsel to buy Mandelson out. But they refused to pay what Mandelson thought his shareholding was worth.
A few months later, as this continued to be a concern, Mandelson told colleagues in an email that Global Counsel should just “pull their fingers out and buy my shares”.

Migration Observatory on what has caused fall in net migration
The Migration Observatory, a migration thinktank based at the University of Oxford, has released this statement about the reasons for the fall in net migration recorded in today’s figures. It says
The fall brings UK net migration below the “tens of thousands” level that was a key unfulfilled government target throughout the 2010s. The 62% fall over the past 12 months-from 204,000 on the year ending June 2025-results from lower immigration. Net migration has fallen over 90% since the peak of 944,000 in the year ending March 2023.
The data now cover two full years of data since the election, and close to a full year since Labour restricted work migration in July 2025. Immigration fell by 17% over the year, driven by fewer non-EU citizens in all major categories: work (down 25%), study (down 11%), family unification (down 30%), and asylum (down 22%). This follows 2025 restrictions making care workers and several mid-skilled jobs ineligible for work visas and suspending family reunion for refugees. The government has also raised compliance requirements for universities sponsoring international students.
Emigration also remained relatively high but fell by 4% (-28,000) compared to the year ending June 2025. High emigration contributes to the overall low net migration figure, but does not result directly from current government policy – most emigrants were non-citizens who previously migrated for work or study, including under the post-Brexit immigration system when immigration was unusually high. EU citizens, primarily people who arrived under free movement, also contributed to the emigration figures, with a negative net migration figure of 45,000.

Security minister Dan Jarvis to address MPs about further release of Mandelson papers
The Government is expected to publish more documents on the appointment of Peter Mandelson as US ambassador shortly, with the security minister set to address MPs at about 12.30pm, the Press Association reports. PA says:
Dan Jarvis will give a statement in the Commons on the latest release of papers which cover the vetting process into Mandelson before he was sent to Washington.
The disgraced peer was sacked from his position last year after more details emerged about his close links to paedophile financier Jeffrey Epstein.
The scandal played a significant role in the eventual resignation of Keir Starmer, with questions raised about how much the then-prime minister knew when he made the appointment.
More than 1,500 pages of documents were published earlier this year, including messages between Mandelson and cabinet ministers.
However, some were withheld at the request of the Metropolitan police as part of their investigation into the former Labour minister and spin doctor.
Mandelson was arrested in February on suspicion of misconduct in public office. No charges have been made. He has denied any allegations and is co-operating with police.
The Met has since informed the government the documents can be published without affecting their investigation.
In February, MPs voted for the papers to be released using a parliamentary mechanism known as a “humble address” and agreed that unredacted files would be first viewed by parliament’s intelligence and security committee (ISC) before being published.
The latest tranche of documents have been seen by the ISC and are now expected to be put before MPs.
Previous documents have shed light on messages between Mandelson and Labour cabinet ministers.
Former chief secretary to the prime minister, Darren Jones, had contacted the peer to tell him he had done a “great job” on the day of his sacking.
Meanwhile, work and pensions secretary Pat McFadden had complained to Lord Mandelson that Labour backbenchers were asking “who can we tax in order to pay benefits to others”.
Mandelson himself was seen to describe No 10 as “beleaguered and bereft”.
Irish PM says he's confident UK and EU will resolve outstanding tuition fees issue
Jakub Krupa
Jakub Krupa writes the Guardian’s Europe live blog.
Irish prime minister Micheál Martin said he was confident the EU and the EU “will be in a position to resolve the outstanding issue” of tuition fees, as he hailed the deals on sanitary and phytosanitary rules, youth mobility, and energy.
Speaking at the beginning of the European Council meeting in Brussels, Martin said that “for the young people of Britain and the young people of Europe, this is a win-win.”
He said:
I often felt with Brexit that the young people of Britain perhaps were not consulted as strongly in terms of the implications for youth mobility.
Young people love to travel, and we don’t need to be placing barriers in the way of young people accessing education, accessing work. In Europe, that should never be the case and I believe we can facilitate that.
On SPS, he said the deal was “very good news for Ireland and the UK” as it will “remove barriers” affecting small to medium size companies in particular.
The deal on the emissions trading scheme also “makes sense” as having the UK and the EU both “in the European energy market also offers up opportunities for both sides”.
Martin was perhaps the only leader offering any sort of detailed answer on the EU-UK relations when asked about the hold-up caused by the lack of agreement on tuition fees, with most of them just delivering general platitudes about the EU-UK relationship post Brexit.
Czech Republic’s Andrej Babiš chuckled and said he thought it was a mistake for Britain to leave, but welcomed its involvement in peace talks on Ukraine, while Latvia’s Andris Kulbergs said he wasn’t across the detail on that issue.
Sweden’s Ulf Kristersson also said he was “not into details” on that point, but welcomed “a major step forward” in relations with Britain.
Farage responds to sharp fall in net migration figures - by complaining too many Britons leaving the country
Nigel Farage, the Reform UK leader, has spent much of his career complaining about there being too much immigration. In response to today’s figures showing it has again fallen (see 9.38am), he is now complaining about – too much emigration by UK nationals! In a post on social media, he says:
Net emigration of British people over the last 10 years is nearly 1 million (941,000), we’ve lost a whole generation of young Brits and replaced them with small boat migrants.
Farage is wrong about the numbers. While a large number of people have arrived in the UK on small boats in recent years (217,000 since 2018, when the Home Office started compiling the figures), that is nowhere close to matching the net emigration figure he quotes.
His only social media comment on immigration (so far today) is to say that Reform UK would abolish the graduate visa system. He says:
It’s too hard for young people to get a job in Britain.
So Reform will ABOLISH the graduate visa.
It lets foreign students stay after their degree for two years, compete for entry-level jobs OR claim asylum!
