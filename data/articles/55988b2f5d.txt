As UN Secretary General's last day approaches, search for his replacement continues
The UN Secretary General's term ends this year, but the big powers at the UN have been unable to agree on a replacement. UN watchers worry that there will be little time to get up to speed.
A MARTÍNEZ, HOST:
It's crunch time for the U.N., as it looks to appoint its next secretary-general. Antonio Guterres is due to step down next month. But after nine rounds of straw polls in the Security Council, there's still no consensus on a successor. Here's NPR's Michele Kelemen.
MICHELE KELEMEN, BYLINE: The Trump administration has been tight-lipped about its preferences for a new secretary-general and had been holding out hope for new candidates to jump in the race. But time is running out, says former U.S. Ambassador Robert Wood.
ROBERT WOOD: I think it's too late, and I'm not sure anybody would want to enter the race at this particular point.
KELEMEN: He says there's too much at stake.
WOOD: Given the turmoil that we have in the world, all these raging wars, the bad need for the U.N. to be reformed, confidence in the U.N. at an all-time low, we need to get this decision right. It has to be the right person.
KELEMEN: Wood sees this mostly as a race between two candidates - Rebeca Grynspan, a former vice president of Costa Rica who's led the U.N.'s Trade and Development Organization, and Italian Argentinian Rafael Grossi, who heads the U.N.'s nuclear watchdog. Guyana's former foreign minister and U.N. ambassador, Carolyn Rodrigues-Burkett, is also in the mix. A former ambassador from the United Arab Emirates, Lana Nusseibeh, is, too, though she's facing opposition from activists because of her country's support for a militia carrying out atrocities in Sudan. Latin American countries also say that this is their region's turn to lead the U.N., according to U.N. traditions of regional rotations.
ELIZABETH COUSENS: It's a confounding process at this point because it is a very strong field.
KELEMEN: That's Elizabeth Cousens, president of the U.N. Foundation, which advocates for a strong U.S. presence at the U.N. In the straw polls, all four of these top candidates have faced opposition from two or more permanent Security Council members. Cousens says it's time for the council to rally behind someone to get the candidate to the General Assembly floor before the year is up.
COUSENS: It's a group of really capable people who've led institutions. They've reformed institutions. They've led negotiations. They've been in the front line of major geopolitical files. I mean, this is a serious group of people running for this role, and I do think it's important to give the field credit for that.
KELEMEN: Another U.N. watcher, Anjali Dayal, who teaches international politics at Fordham University, says whoever emerges will have little time to get up to speed at a complicated time in world affairs.
ANJALI DAYAL: We would never think that a private organization appointing someone in December to pick up the reins in January would be a good idea. We would not imagine that we would give them that little lead time to take over the stewardship of a massive, complicated organization - to staff up, to be briefed, to hit the ground running.
KELEMEN: The State Department says it wants a secretary-general to get back to basics, working on peace and security rather than, quote, "the absurd, politicized, woke ideology that has undermined" the U.N.'s effectiveness. Dayal points out that the Trump administration did not go through the U.N. to launch a war against Iran, and Russia violated the U.N. Charter by invading Ukraine.
DAYAL: And so being able to get this position involves satisfying permanent members that want to violate the terms of the Charter while committing to upholding the terms of the Charter - to hold the job, to perform it well for everybody else. So it's a tricky proposition, and that's why it's taking so long.
KELEMEN: Behind the scenes, U.N. experts say there's probably some horse trading going on that could include promises of top U.N. jobs to permanent Security Council members to overcome veto threats.
Michele Kelemen, NPR News, the State Department.
