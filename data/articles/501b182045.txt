Grounded flights. Bank websites crashing. Microsoft Outlook outages grinding business operations to a halt. Zoom, Ikea, and PokerStars websites hacked. In the past weeks, there’s been a distinct sense that the complex systems that undergird our daily life might be on the verge of total collapse. Or, at the very least, they seem pretty wobbly.
Even those who aren’t prone to conspiracy theories have been wondering out loud if these recent disruptions are somehow connected. Speculation runs wild about targeted cyber-attacks by Iranian hackers, or AI agents autonomously hacking critical (air traffic, banking) and not-so-critical (the Ikea website) infrastructure.
For their part, politicians and the spokespeople of affected companies have denied such master plots. And while many presumed that the nationwide airline ground stoppage on 13 November resulted from the Federal Aviation Administration’s rollout of new AI-assisted administration software (called, perhaps aspirationally, the Smart System), the FAA itself laid the blame on a downed router in Arkansas. Microsoft has chalked the recent outage to Outlook not to malicious intrusions, but some buggy code that has been lying dormant for months. Some people will buy such explanations. Others, naturally, may remain skeptical.
Just how worried should we be? Are these disruptions just a new, extremely annoying part of daily life?
The Guardian spoke to experts across fields to understand how AI and other threats are jeopardizing critical infrastructure, and how exactly we’re mitigating worst-case scenarios.
Aviation: ‘The idea that one router in Arkansas could take down an entire network is not credible’
Bob W Mann Jr, former airline executive and airline industry analyst
Did the FAA’s nationwide ground stoppage in November surprise you at all?
The extent of this outage was surprising. And to be honest, the excuses about what caused it are not credible. The FAA’s telecommunications infrastructure is fully duplicated, fully redundant; every link, every node. There are tens of thousands of nodes and pieces of hardware. The idea that one router in Arkansas could take down an entire network is not credible.
Can you give me a sense of how in need of upgrades the FAA’s systems are?
Well, they’re held together with rubber bands and chewing gum and paperclips. But paperclips are conducting! They’re actually good conductors! But they’re 50-year-old systems. A lot of the basic architecture is old, and analog, and it’s being replaced over time.
There was some speculation that the outage had to do with the FAA rolling out this new AI-system, the Smart System. Do you give any credence to that?
That happens to be my theory on the outage. I work in this area, and we have deployed what you can call “AI” if you want, but I would call computer-based operations research techniques. But it’s designed to reduce airspace congestion and complexity. This Smart System has some AI, which is to say it involves computers talking to pilots. It’s designed to help flight-tracking, and reduce delays and it’s been tested on a carrier-level, with individual airlines. There’s been no issues. What happened, I believe, was that the FAA tried to roll it out simultaneously, for all flights across the nation, which has a bigger risk.
Can you explain what that risk is?
The network gets overwhelmed with these administrative messages, saying, basically, “instead of flying this trajectory, fly an alternate trajectory.” If you have a large number of these messages, it will cause quality problems with radar data, radio data and, to some degree, voice data. When you overwhelm the network with administrative crap, you can cause the quality of radar data to deteriorate. That’s when people working air-traffic control get understandably concerned. And they respond to that by cutting the volume in the system, and just shutting it down.
What are the things you are worried about, as the airlines and FAA try to modernize their infrastructure?
It’s understandable that we want everything to be up to date. Since I’ve been looking at this problem, we’ve spent close to $150bn over four generations of system upgrades. And we basically have the same system. And it doesn’t address the issue of delays. It’s an own goal. They’re not using their institutional knowledge of what is the likely outcome, and not testing it adequately. They tested it on 180 flights over 60 days, in Washington, and then said: “We’re good to go. We’re just going to do all nationwide fights.”
So the worst-case scenario is people implementing things at scale, without adequate testing, and understanding what could happen. You can overwhelm that telecommunications infrastructure. Don’t be concerned about flight safety. Pilots, and their dispatchers, and FAA, they’re professionals. They ensure the system is safe. That’s what gets us to a vanishingly low accident rate.
In your view, what motivation does the FAA have to cover up the cause of the outage?
They don’t have much invested in a single router. But they do have a lot of investment in their new AI system. And I’m not alone in thinking this. A lot of air traffic controllers have been speaking out about it. They don’t buy the router theory either.
Banking: ‘Everyone is getting attacked all the time’
William Wetherill, chief information security officer at DefenseStorm, a cyber-attack risk management agency servicing banks, credit unions and financial institutions
A recent report on the state of the banking industry showed that 81% of financial institutions surveyed reported unauthorized access and other cyber-security intrusions in the past year. That seems like a lot.
The amount of attacks that you see is kind of incredible. Banks have always been juicy targets for attacks. And that doesn’t change today. AI has made attacks cheaper. When you’re in this world, and see what’s happening, well, everyone is getting attacked all the time.
What do these hackers want, exactly? To drain out bank accounts?
If they could just steal money, they’d do it. But that money is all insured. And it’s traceable. The FBI can track them down in 24 hours. Sometimes they’ll try moving money: hack a business email and get into the middle of the transaction and change the routing number. But it’s usually more about extortion. They’ll steal data from banks and credit unions, which we call “target rich”. Then they threaten to release it, unless they’re paid however many million dollars. It’s more about the ransom. They’re looking for the institutions to pay crypto, so it’s not traceable. The damage you can do is pretty high. You could shut down a credit union, if they get ransomed too much and consumers lose confidence.
What is the closest a financial institution has come to a total breakdown?
What we just witnessed with multiple banks going down was a major event. For those of us whose money is stored in online bank accounts, and whose livelihoods are stored by credit unions, we don’t have access to our funds, that’s about as critical as you can get. That’s devastating, on a smaller scale.
The biggest thing that can happen is attacking our backbone: infrastructure like Swift payments that are the underlying fabric of financial transactions across the globe. If those come down, that would be our doomsday. But that hasn’t happened, yet.
The majority of cybercrime is financially motivated. [Financial criminals] don’t have the motivation to kill the one thing that’s helping them. You destroy banking, you destroy this technology, and you destroy the entire world.
So how worried should people be about losing all their money?
The first thing to understand, and I’ll say this loud and clear, is that nothing has fundamentally changed. The speed and breadth of attacks has changed. But it’s all things we’ve already dealt with. There’s a lot of paranoia. And people really, really suffer for it. When you have your identity stolen, it’s really exposing, and mental health comes into play. You have to understand that these are blanket attacks, you’re not being targeted, personally. When I deal with individuals, I want them to understand that in this case, you aren’t special. And that should make you feel better. An individual shouldn’t have a mindset where they’re prepping for the end of the world constantly.
National Security: ‘The worst case-scenario would be incursions into water treatment facilities and hospitals’
Dr Scott J White, director of the cyber-security program and cyber academy at George Washington University, former officer with the Canadian Security Intelligence Service
How much does AI increase the threat of a major cyber-attack?
Anyone with a laptop computer and connectivity can now be an asymmetrical threat against the west. A lot of minor actors, who never had the capability, now AI makes them active players. AI is a threat multiplier.
Who are these threat actors?
We have what we call our advanced persistent threats, or APTs. There are four major ones: China, Russia, Iran and North Korea. But every time America moves in the world, we’re going to get a proxy. For example, with the American incursion into Venezuela, you’re going to see people who support Venezuela, or Venezuelans, start to attack the United States. As the US continues its conflict with Iran, we have Iranian proxies globally that will be launching attacks against us globally. Now you’re adding a very low-level, low-skilled individual with AI, and we have to start using AI as a defensive structure as well. We’re asking AI to try and find these threat vectors, as they’re coming in. It doesn’t surprise me at all that these attacks are coming in. You have to remember that every new piece of software that comes out, our adversaries are also getting that same piece of software. They’re reverse-engineering it.
In the event that these defenses aren’t sufficient, or aren’t implemented swiftly enough, what’s the worst thing that can happen?
The worst case-scenario, globally, would be incursions into water treatment facilities, hospitals, and so on. Imagine launching a cyber-attack against a country: take its communication, and water treatment facilities, and energy distribution nodes offline. Make the country blind. Then follow with a kinetic attack. How successful would that be? It would be extremely successful. Leave the country blind, and unarmed. Then follow with a traditional attack. That’s a formula for success.
The probability of such a scenario remains relatively low. The Russians are well aware that an attack of this nature could trigger a rapid and significant escalation, potentially extending to the use of nuclear weapons. The consequences would be extraordinarily severe and the risks of triggering a broader global conflict would be difficult for any party to control or predict. That said, a low probability does not mean the scenario can be dismissed or overlooked.
It seems like cybersecurity can only play catchup to advanced attacks from AI hackers.
Absolutely we’re playing catchup. That’s exactly the problem. We have something called “security by design”, sometimes called the “zero trust” security approach. This is when you build out a system from the beginning, accounting for threat vectors. But not all systems are built like this. And it’s very difficult to retrofit those kinds of systems. We continue to layer security and try to create a smaller attack surface and limit the attacker, and how they can get to us. That’s the world we are in. Is it a dystopian world? I wouldn’t go that far. But it’s going to require us to do a lot more upgrading on our security.
