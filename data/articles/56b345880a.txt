My passion for HBO’s True Blood eventually led me to visit an alleged vampire’s grave in real life. I started watching the summer before my senior year of college in 2010, and by the time I visited Mercy Brown’s grave in Exeter, Rhode Island, I was into vampires enough to get college credit for it.
I signed up for a course co-taught by professors from the history and English departments. We read Dracula, The Historian, and all sorts of vampire folktales. We also studied the historical context surrounding the myths, and the superstitions associated with them. We even watched select episodes of Buffy the Vampire Slayer and Angel, along with The Lost Boys, Let the Right One In, and Interview with the Vampire.
For a mid-semester project, I wrote about the infamous Mercy Brown vampire incident, in which a Rhode Island family suspected that a dead 19-year-old was feeding on her brother from beyond the grave. In 1892, they exhumed her body to find it remarkably well-preserved, so they burned her heart and gave her dying brother the ashes to drink. He died anyway.
We were later given absolute freedom to explore whatever we wanted for our final project, so I wrote something like 25 pages on how seriously True Blood takes vampire mythology. I probably could’ve written another 25 on why I ship Eric and Sookie so much.
In the days after Thanksgiving, we at Polygon celebrate all things vampire and vampire-adjacent with Fangsgiving. There are plenty of great stories and characters to consider, but True Blood gave me an especially good excuse to revisit the series this year: its finale ends with a literal Thanksgiving dinner. Twelve years later, I’m still thinking about how the show really peaked in those middle seasons, and how frustrating that final season was at large.
True Blood had already given a vampire a perfect goodbye
One of my favorite things about True Blood was how much it could get away with in a single episode. I could spend an hour laughing at whatever Jason Stackhouse (Ryan Kwanten) had gotten himself into and then be genuinely wrecked by a vampire I’d only known for a few weeks. That’s what happened with Godric (Allan Hyde), the vampire who “made” Eric Northman (Alexander Skarsgård). Season 2 sends Eric to Dallas to find him, and for all the trouble it takes to get him back, Godric has already decided he’s ready to die.
On a rooftop just before sunrise, Eric begs Godric to stay. It’s one of the few times Eric looks young and exhibits any kind of emotional vulnerability. This smug, seemingly unkillable Viking is suddenly somebody’s kid, pleading with the person who made him. Sookie (Anna Paquin) stays with Godric after Eric leaves. She cries for him, and he’s surprised that a human would. Then the sun comes up.
I wrote an entire section of my final paper about this profound dynamic, and how often vampire stories warp the nature of family and relationships. “Fader, broder, søn,” is the vow that Godric and Eric share, a promise to forever remain a father, brother, and son to one another. Godric was stuck in the body of a 16-year-old boy for about a thousand years before he turned Eric, a grown man, into a vampire. Surviving for another thousand years meant occasionally posing as father and son.
I swear all this is relevant: When defending the maligned series finale, showrunner Brian Buckner, who also wrote that episode, compared Bill’s (Stephen Moyer) death to Godric’s in an interview with TV Line. “What Bill came around to was similar to what Godric came around to,” he said. “A human life is extraordinary too.” I can see why he wanted to return to that idea. The rooftop scene makes you feel the weight of immortality in a few minutes. The finale had seven seasons of history between Bill and Sookie to draw on.
Bill’s last request went too far
By the series finale, Bill has contracted Hep-V, a fictional disease that's fatal for vampires. Even though Eric and Pam (Kristin Bauer van Straten) are able to mass-produce a cure using Sarah Newlin’s (Anna Camp) blood, Bill refuses to take it. Instead, he asks Sookie to use her last burst of fairy light to kill him, which would also strip her of her powers and, in his view, give her a shot at a normal life. In the cemetery, she decides those powers are part of who she is. Instead, she helps him stake himself in his coffin. He explodes in spectacular fashion as True Blood vampires always do.
Aside from the fact that Bill hasn’t even lived 200 years, compared with Godric’s 2,000 years as a vampire — which makes you wonder how he could possibly be as world-weary — the worst part of this plot is how Bill tries to take away Sookie’s agency. He acts like he’s giving her a gift. Sure, early in the series when Sookie is presented as a telepath who cannot control her powers, it feels more like a curse than anything else. But as she grows throughout the course of seven seasons, her journey is one of self-actualization. Why would a man who supposedly loves her want to take all of that away?
I have a harder time with what comes next, when the show skips over the years she spends building a life of her own and lands on a pregnant Sookie with a husband whose face we never see. It’s a happy ending that offers very little substance. Why bother?
The finale does deliver a version of domestic happiness I find much more convincing. Jessica (Deborah Ann Woll) marries Hoyt (Jim Parrack), a human who wants to build a life with her even though she’ll never grow old alongside him. The reunion is rushed, and the weirdest part is that Hoyt has no memory of their past relationship, yet he proposes anyway.
There is something particularly sweet about Bill getting to walk his vampire daughter down the aisle. Probably the silliest twist is how Hoyt’s girlfriend, Brigette (Ashley Hinshaw), reacts to the situation: the show basically pulls a Family Stone.She comes to town with Hoyt, gets dumped by him, and then falls for Jason. By the holiday flash-forward, they’ve got three kids. Jason and Hoyt, who are practically brothers, end up with each other’s former partners. We see everyone enjoy a Thanksgiving dinner together, except Sookie's husband sits at the head of the table with his back to the camera. We never see his face.
Buckner told The Wrap after the finale aired that he loved Friday Night Lights' ending and wanted to leave viewers with the feeling that the people of Bon Temps would be all right. I wanted that, too. But I also wanted a little more of the bonkers show that gave us Eric rescuing Sookie one last time, then dancing behind the wheel of a stolen car with a pile of dead Yakuza in the backseat. That gag is in the same episode. Eric and Pam even get an infomercial. So much of True Blood’s final stretch feels rushed and unearned. The last season killed Tara (Rutina Wesley) offscreen, shot Alcide (Joe Manganiello) in the third episode, and got Sookie back together with Bill by the seventh episode. The worst sin of all? It’s all rather boring for a show that spent much of its run as one of the sexiest and most dangerously electrifying shows on TV.
I’d love to reread that college paper if I can ever find it. I remember caring a lot about the weird vampire rules, but the part I can still tell you about is “fader, broder, søn.” I loved that Eric and Godric had lived long enough to be fathers, brothers, and sons to each other, sometimes all at once. Two vampires on a rooftop made me want to write an entire section about what it means to be family when you’re immortal. At Sookie’s Thanksgiving dinner, I couldn’t tell you a single thing about the man she’d chosen to start her own family with. And that makes it feel hollow by comparison.
