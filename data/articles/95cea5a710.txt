Even as judges have blocked visa restrictions and other limits on foreign scholars and scientists, the Trump administration is winning outside court. New data show a clear drop in the enrollment of international graduate students, a key piece of the U.S. biomedical workforce.
That paradox was on full display last week. On Nov. 16, a federal judge issued a final ruling blocking a proposed four-year cap on how long international students and postdoctoral researchers can remain in the country. The next day, a judge in a separate case issued a preliminary injunction preventing the administration from narrowing eligibility for a work authorization program widely used by foreign students.
To student advocates like Fanta Aw, CEO of the nonprofit NAFSA: Association of International Educators, the rulings represented two wins in two days. “I’ve always believed that our judicial systems are strong and are very much still in play, and I felt relieved,” said Aw, whose organization is a plaintiff in both cases. “Our members, and in particular our students, I felt, were vindicated.”
But data released the same day as the ruling in the visa time limit case paint a less celebratory picture. A survey of more than 800 U.S. institutions conducted by the Institute of International Education found that foreign student enrollment for the current academic year has decreased 9% overall compared with 2025, with a 15% drop among newly enrolled students and graduate students. While IIE won’t have complete data until next year, the preliminary numbers suggest this will be the third consecutive year graduate enrollment has dropped — with the largest year-to-year decrease in at least 20 years, according to historical data from the nonprofit.
That trend extends to biomedicine, where foreign students make up nearly a quarter of life science Ph.D. graduates, according to new survey data from the Association of American Medical Colleges. A poll of 37 AAMC member institutions showed that the number of international students starting biomedical Ph.D. programs has decreased by about 30% since 2024, more than twice the 14% overall decline in matriculation in these programs.
The growing body of data as well as STAT’s interviews with students and immigration experts suggest that, despite the legal losses, the Trump administration’s policies on immigration have had a chilling effect on students considering coming to the U.S., and on those who are already here. Meanwhile, enrollment of foreign students is rising at some Asian universities, and experts warn that these opposing trends could jeopardize the nation’s biomedical preeminence and that it could take years to repair the loss of trust from students who no longer see the U.S. as a welcoming destination.
“People are looking at us from overseas, going, ‘We don’t really recognize this country,’” said Elizabeth Goss, an immigration attorney based in Boston. Even if Democrats’ new majority in Congress prevents further restrictions, she added, “I think we have a lot of ground to make up.”
When contacted for comment, a Department of Homeland Security spokesperson wrote in an email that the agency’s records don’t support the decline reported by IIE, though it didn’t say whether its own data show lower enrollment by foreign students, and that President Trump should be credited for cracking down on rampant visa fraud.
“Institute of International Education’s ‘reporting’ is completely misleading and not backed up by any real evidence,” the DHS spokesperson said. “So far, [the Student and Exchange Visitor Program] has not experienced a 9% drop of foreign student enrollment.”
A recent independent analysis by the Presidents’ Alliance on Higher Education and Immigration of the Student and Exchange Visitor Information System, a DHS database that has for more than 20 years monitored foreign students, found that international student enrollment declined by 6.3% between October 2025 and October 2026.
Concerns that the Trump administration would make it difficult for foreign scientists to enter or stay in the U.S. preceded the president’s inauguration. Since then, the administration has enacted travel bans extreme vetting” for visa applicants, including the review of social media profiles; and revoked more than 8,000 student visas. Immigration and Customs Enforcement has also stepped up enforcement of alleged visa violations, including the recent detention of a Princeton neuroscience Ph.D. student who had been traveling home after presenting at a scientific meeting.
But many of the administration’s efforts to more tightly regulate international scholars have not come to fruition. That includes the attempt to end “duration of status,” a policy that for nearly half a century allowed students and postdoctoral researchers to stay in the U.S. for the length of their training. DHS’s proposed four-year cap would likely have forced the vast majority of new foreign life science students to apply for extensions to stay in the country given that, on average, it takes five to six years to earn a biomedical doctorate. But a federal judge found that the proposal violated the Administrative Procedure Act, a law that governs how agencies implement new regulations.
The Trump administration has also proposed a $70,000 fee for a popular work authorization program meant to give students professional experience; academic institutions would be expected to pay the fee. And DHS is attempting to revive plans to institute a $100,000 fee for H1-B visas, which are often used by foreign workers in high-skill fields such as biotech.
Even though these restrictions have not been enacted, they’ve left many foreign students with a clear sense that they aren’t wanted here.
“All these rules, it just made me feel like my immigration was a crime. That’s the biggest recurring feeling that I’ve had these past few years,” said a biomedical Ph.D. student at the University of Washington, who requested anonymity to speak freely. “You know what? I’m just here to study. I’m just learning, and I’m focused on my science. But it has felt very disruptive.”
There are growing signs that fear is impacting student decisions. A poll this summer from the Association of American Universities found that, while domestic student applications for U.S. Ph.D. programs went up by 3% this year, international applications fell 21%.
A more recent survey of 437 universities, including more than 200 in the U.S., found that nearly 60% of U.S. institutions reported lower graduate enrollment between August and October compared with the same period last year. Ninety percent of U.S. respondents cited restrictive government policies as a significant obstacle.
That trend isn’t unique to the U.S. The Global Enrollment Benchmark Survey found that nearly 80% of institutions in the United Kingdom, which has also instituted tougher immigration controls, reported reduced foreign graduate student enrollment. By contrast, nearly 60% of Asian universities that responded, including five Chinese institutions, said graduate enrollment had increased. It’s a shift that Aw says could have major implications for the global economy and the future of innovation.
“There is truly a global competition for talent,” she said. “China has made no secret that one of its most pressing priorities is international education and to bring as many talented students to its shores as they can from around the world. That used to be the United States.”
