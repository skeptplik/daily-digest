Channel 4 has suffered a blow after one of Britain’s biggest advertisers ditched almost all of its TV spending with the troubled broadcaster, in favour of a switch to online influencer marketing.
Unilever, the owner of brands including Dove soap and Hellmann’s mayonnaise, has cut down its almost £100m UK TV advertising budget, with Channel 4 the biggest victim.
Analysis for the Guardian by industry sources shows Unilever’s UK TV media spend has plummeted by just over 50% so far this year compared with 2025. The Anglo-Dutch company has traditionally been a top-three TV advertiser in the UK, alongside Procter & Gamble and the Sky parent company, Comcast.
According to the figures, Channel 4 has been the hardest hit proportionally, with Unilever slashing approximately 92% of its spending with the broadcaster, which is making record swingeing job cuts amid a tough advertising market.
In March last year, Fernando Fernandez, who had just been appointed chief executive of Unilever, pledged to hire “20 times more influencers” in a marketing strategy shake-up that has had dire consequences for broadcasters.
This week, Unilever, one of the world’s biggest marketers with an annual budget in excess of €8bn (£6.7bn), revealed it had upped its creator network from 75,000 in 2024 to more than 500,000.
Sky, which also sells on behalf of partners including Channel 5, has taken a 44% hit, while ITV has experienced a 42% fall in Unilever advertising spend.
In spend terms, ITV and Sky’s sales operation experienced bigger declines than Channel 4 as Unilever spends more money with them.
“Unilever is a valued partner with whom we have a great relationship,” said a spokesperson for channel 4. “We continue to work closely with them alongside our large portfolio of advertisers, crafting exciting projects to reach audiences and deliver marketing campaigns.”
The consumer goods company declined to comment.
In an update on Unilever’s strategy this week, Leandro Barreto, its chief marketing officer, said creator-led content was delivering 1.5 times the return on investment of branded content, which includes media such as traditional TV, press and digital display ads.
Unilever’s high-profile campaigns have included tie-ups between Persil and the Olympic champion Usain Bolt, and a Hellmann’s ad during the Super Bowl that featured the actor Elle Fanning.
The company has worked with influencers including the former Love Island contestant Molly-Mae Hague on the laundry detergent and fabric conditioner brand Comfort and the beauty creator Jen Chae, who inspired an actual Vaseline product.
However, research by Thinkbox, the marketing body for commercial TV in the UK, found that broadcast TV ads generated 39% of all uplift in awareness created by a campaign. This was three times more than any other media channel, with influencers driving just a 1% uplift, it found.
“TV makes other media work harder, it makes other media work harder, including social,” said Lindsey Clay, the chief executive of Thinkbox. “Social media is already wildly over-invested by brands relative to what it delivers, whether that is profit, ad awareness, or the cultural impact brands are seeking.
“New money for influencers should come from underperforming parts of social media. Influencers clearly have their place, but shifting money from TV to fund them is self-defeating.”
Unilever continues to spend significantly on TV globally including sponsoring this summer’s Fifa World Cup, for which 100,000 pieces of content were deployed in 120 markets and working with 50,000 creators.
The company also said the use of technology, including AI tools, had enabled it to produce 12 times more content than in 2024, while cutting content production costs by 90%.
About 70% of Unilever’s marketing budget is now spent on digital channels, with Fernandez promising last year that half the digital spend would shift to social channels.
According to industry figures Unilever spent about £92m on TV advertising in the UK last year and had been spending as much £122m as recently as 2023.
“Brands across all sectors are increasing their influencer marketing spend,” said Jessica Tamsedge, the chief executive of the ad agency Dentsu Creative in the UK and Ireland. “As marketing spend comes under pressure, influencer marketing delivers both long- and short-term results and AI integration makes deployment more precise and measurable.”
In March, Unilever agreed to combine its food business with the US-based McCormick in a $44.8bn (£34.2bn) deal, maintaining 65% control of the spin-off. The move brings together brands such as Knorr, Marmite and Pot Noodle with McCormick’s condiments and spices including French’s mustard, Old Bay seasoning and Cholula hot sauce. The deal is expected to complete in mid-2027.
