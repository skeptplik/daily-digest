It sounds benign: the Com, short for the Community. But crime agencies around the world warn that it is far from that and the young cybercriminals involved are causing havoc and putting children at chilling, sickening risk.
Beau-Jack Mensforth was jailed by a UK judge on Friday for his activities as part of the Com. He was one individual operating from his bedroom in north-east England, but there are probably thousands more like him and parents are being urged by police to be aware and to check their children’s devices.
The Com, crime agencies say, is a sprawling and fluid global network of online communities populated by young cybercriminals. A typical Com member is a boy or young man aged 16 to 25. They are primarily native English speakers. Their crimes are broad and may include data theft, crypto scams, bringing down IT systems and causing chaos through attacks that slow down networks.
Then there are young men such as Mensforth who targeted vulnerable young girls and encouraged them to harm themselves or sacrifice their pets.
Often the criminals coerce girls to take indecent images of themselves which they then share. They blackmail and threaten their victims with swatting and doxing, police say.
Swatting is the term used for reporting fictitious incidents at the victim’s address to the emergency services, so they turn up in numbers, sometimes armed. Doxing is threatening to put all of someone’s personal details online.
They operate on every platform, according to DCI Steve Brown of Northumbria police, who led the investigation into Mensforth. “They target people they know have vulnerabilities, people who need somebody to make them feel wanted, to feel valued,” said Brown. “They gain that trust and then they turn that trust against them by asking them to perform horrendous acts.”
In July 2025, the FBI issued its first big public alert about the Com under the headline: “Theft, extortion and violence are a rising threat to youth online”.
The FBI said the Com was a growing and evolving online threat, calling it “a primarily English-speaking, international, online ecosystem comprised of multiple interconnected networks whose members, many of whom are minors, engage in a variety of criminal violations”.
It added: “The FBI estimates thousands of individuals identify as current or recent members of the Com with varying levels of associated activity.”
The UK’s National Crime Agency has said reports of Com networks increased sixfold in the UK from 2022-2024, describing members as “usually young men who are motivated by status, power, control, misogyny, sexual gratification, or an obsession with extreme or violent material”.
The Molly Rose Foundation, a leading UK online safety charity, this year issued a stark public warning about the Com and called for a coordinated global response from governments, regulators, law enforcement and tech companies.
Andy Burrows, the charity’s chief executive, said: “The growing threat posed by Com networks is the most chilling and urgent threat to children online today and it requires a swift and comprehensive response.”
The motivations of Com members vary but, according to the FBI, often fall into one of these categories: “Financial gain, retaliation, ideology, sexual gratification and notoriety.”
They wanted to have control, said Brown. They got a kick out of getting people to do things they would never normally do.
Courts around the world are increasingly dealing with criminals linked to the Com. Sentences vary but there’s thought to be none longer than the 77-year jail term handed down in August, in Tennessee, to Kyle Spitze, 27, for abetting the distribution of animal crushing videos and child sexual exploitation material offences.
The US Department of Justice said Spitze’s crimes occurred as part of his association with an online network known for its “sadistic extortion of young and vulnerable people around the world”.
Crime agencies such as the FBI urge people “to exercise caution when posting or messaging personal information, photos or videos on social media, dating sites or other online platforms”.
For parents it can be bewildering and scary but they do need to monitor their child’s online activity, police say.
After the gruelling slog of the Mensforth case, Brown said he now checked the devices of his own children. “Personally, since this investigation, I do that to my kids,” he said. “It’s simply asking parents: talk, know and act. Talk to your children, know what they’re doing and act upon it.”
