LONDON — Long before wellness became a $6.3 trillion juggernaut, growing at twice the pace of traditional luxury goods, Iris Ventures founder Montse Suarez was already making the connection between consumers’ pursuit of the inside and outside feel good factor.
A eureka moment came when the former Morgan Stanley banker was setting up Walgreens Boots Alliance’s corporate venture fund nearly 15 years ago. It didn’t take her long to realize that women going into Boots were not thinking in terms of product categories.
Instead, “they were looking for benefits, thinking, ‘I want to feel glamorous, I want to feel good, I want to feel empowered or I want to to solve a certain problem,'” Suarez said in a wide-ranging interview, adding that it was clear to her at the time those women were looking to achieve an overall sense of well-being.
Suarez would later cofound Vaultier7, an early backer of the baby food company Little Spoon; honey-based hair care expert Gisou, and 111Skin, the skin care brand born from the London Harley Street clinic.
She launched Iris in 2021 around her original idea that people were looking to feel good on the inside and the outside, and that they wanted to live healthier lives for longer. The pandemic, when people were working from home and spending more time cooking, exercising and trying to improve their quality of life, intensified that desire for overall well-being.
Iris invests in what Suarez describes as “culturally relevant” brands that help people “live longer, and feel healthier and happier,” with as little fuss as possible. Investments range from home interiors companies to food and nutrition ones, to those targeting gut and hormonal health, skin and hair care.
It takes stakes at the early growth stage, typically Series A, but can sometimes swoop in at seed, or Series B level. It is on track to close Fund II, a 200 million-euro vehicle, in the first quarter of 2027 and, in doing so, will roughly double its debut fund.
Its checks are now running 10 million euros to 20 million euros per investment, with backers including Puig, and some of Europe’s leading private family offices. Each fund has a 10-year life, with Iris looking to help build a brand’s value over a four- to six-year period.
No Time Like the Present
Suarez believes the global wellness economy is brimming with opportunity due to growing interest in high-end services, specialist skin care and hair care, pharmacy-grade formulations and the pharmacy as a retail channel.
She added that macro factors such as the pandemic, the rise of GLP-1 drugs and the interest in longterm quality of life have only accelerated consumers’ needs for the brands that Iris backs. There are currently 16 businesses in the portfolio, with one more set to be revealed before the end of the year.
Brands include Olistic, which combats hair loss; Goddess, which aims to strengthen hair from the inside out, and Superlativa, a plant-based, drug- and hormone-free supplement designed to help women manage stress and cortisol levels.
Iris has also invested in the plant-based, probiotic drinks brand Biomel; Reformed, which blends coffee or ceremonial-grade matcha with vitamins and minerals, and Lucille, a healthy, high-protein, calorically dense shake aimed at older people, and which anyone can consume as a snack or light meal substitute.
Other investments include the slick online platform Healf, where customers can order vitamins and supplements, access blood tests and health and wellness advice. Healf, founded by brothers Max and Lestat Clarke, opened its first physical retail space earlier this year at Harvey Nichols in Knightsbridge.
Room for Disruption and Expansion
Iris is always on the hunt for new opportunities.
“We continue to look at the food space because we believe that there’s still a lot of room for disruption. We’re also looking into service propositions because we feel there are ways to modernize some of the more fragmented traditional” models, Suarez said.
A year ago, Iris led a $15 million investment into Innerskin, a French company specializing in noninvasive skin care treatments at its clinics. The brand also has a medical-grade product line with signature formulas that are able to mimic hands-on skin care treatments, such as microneedling.
Iris invested for a number of reasons, including Innerskin’s potential to scale further. Innerskin opened its first clinic outside France, in London’s Notting Hill, and the plan is to continue rolling out more sites, potentially in well-heeled neighborhoods such as Chelsea and Marylebone.
Suarez said the aim is to take a clinic format “and turn it into a global brand with a look, feel and service offer that is consistent across different locations. Innerskin is digitally savvy but there is still a human touch, with treatments and techniques that are advanced, but not too intrusive,” she said.
There are a lot of good aesthetic clinics that have one or two locations, Suarez said, “but they lack the ability to scale. That Innerskin has been able to scale to almost 30 locations in France in such a short period of time speaks to [founder, Chrystelle Eid’s] ability to be consistent with her mission, the look and feel of the brand, and the quality of the service.” That’s what made the investment so attractive, she said.
There is also room for growth in hair care, Suarez believes. “There’s still a lack of innovation. It’s true there are a lot of brands being launched in the space, but I still think that the consumer is not yet super happy with the benefits of certain products. There is still room for solving more problems and solving them well,” she said.
Premium Skin Care Is a Wide-Open Opportunity
“The consumer wants choice and results, and I feel there’s still room for new brands to come to the market, or existing brands to push the level of innovation one step up. I also think there is an underserved consumer — and that’s the mature woman,” she said.
Suarez added, “Everybody going through perimenopause and menopause sees changes to their skin. We haven’t experienced this before, we still don’t know exactly which brand to go to. And at the end of the day, we’re the women with the disposable income, the time, the resources to buy these products, so I still think there’s an opportunity” in the space.
She added that Iris is also interested in skin care that solves problems such as acne.
She argued that Krème, a French dermocosmetic brand now in the Iris portfolio, was appealing because of its focus on skin science and the microbiome. Krème touts clean, creamy, probiotic formulations that aim to nourish the skin’s microbiome, which shields against germs, pathogens and inflammation.
Suarez believes Krème, which is sold in more than 3,000 pharmacies and at Galeries Lafayette, and which has doubled its revenue every year since 2020, “is owning the sensitive skin space with its pharma-grade product.”
Looking Beyond the Obvious
Asked what sort of investments Iris eschews, Suarez said, “color cosmetics. It’s overcrowded. At the end of the day, everybody is launching the same products,” and using similar contractors. She added that Iris is also steering clear of “brands that are betting on just Sephora” as a retail channel.
“I think there’s a lot of space in other channels. The pharmacy channel is one we’ve been able to leverage significantly with Olistic, Superlativa and Krème, and it’s given us extraordinary results,” she said.
Pharmacies also come with an inherent sense of trust and possibility. Suarez believes there are still so many reasons for walking into Boots, which was bought earlier this year by the Weston-owned Wittington Investments. “You don’t enter Boots because you’re looking for beauty specifically. You walk in — ultimately — because you want to feel well,” she said.
Iris also needs all of its brands to be backed by pharma-grade science, and proof of efficacy.
“The buyer looking to acquire our businesses [eventually] will also be looking heavily into the science that is behind any specific product. We’ve been very, very focused on backing businesses that have a true science behind them,” Suarez said, adding that Maurten, which makes carbohydrate-fueled hydrogels for sports endurance, has more than 90 patents, while Olistic has more than eight.
“It is very important for us to be able to demonstrate that science,” she said.
Staying Close to the Founders
Suarez describes her multilingual, multicultural team as hands-on, working daily with founders across eight countries, mainly in Europe. She believes the cosmopolitan nature of the team is a super power.
“The ability to influence a German founder is so different from the ability to influence an Italian one,” said Suarez, adding that knowing languages and being sensitive to cultural nuances also helps American founders succeed in Europe, and European founders make deeper inroads in the U.K., and on the Continent.
Suarez also argues that Iris’ own backers see the firm as “the most international consumer investor in Europe at the early growth stage,” and able “to capture the value created when consumer categories collide and innovate together.”
Iris strategizes with founders as if they were a sports team, and that’s deliberate. Suarez, a mother of three, is a former gymnast, trains for Hyrox races and plays field hockey for a team in her home city of Barcelona. When she meets a founder, she needs to know they have winner’s mindset, and the ability to play as a team.
“You have to see that energy and momentum in the founders’ eyes, but you also have to connect with them. At the end of the day, investing is about connecting. That’s why, perhaps, our portfolio has a bit of a feminine angle. Half of my team are female professionals, we connect with female founders and we understand the problems they’re trying to solve,” she said.
That sports mindset is so embedded in Iris that, the day after the annual general meeting, Suarez convenes the brands’ founders, and her team, for a gym session.
Suarez, an early investor in Saysh, which was founded by the 11-time Olympic medalist Allyson Felix and her brother and business partner Wes Felix, has a soft spot for athletes.
That’s one reason why Iris hosts Beyond the Finish Line, an educational giveback program that helps athletes with financial, brand-building and entrepreneurship tools to help build their post-competitive careers. Suarez refers to them as “the next generation of genuine culture-makers,” and wants to “unlock their untapped potential.”
Sports will be the next investment frontier for Iris, although Suarez wants to take her time.
She said that investing in sports teams in Europe isn’t as straightforward as it is in the U.S., “so we’re looking at sports through different lenses to see if we can find an opportunity, and find where we have the right to play. I would love to invest in women’s sports, but I don’t think the European market is ready to receive capital from a fund that needs to come in, and come out, in few years.”
In the meantime, Iris will focus on its founders, whose success is of paramount importance. Suarez said Iris wants to help them “deliver on their promises” and continue to create products that make consumers’ lives easier, healthier and happier.
