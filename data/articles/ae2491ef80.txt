To say I was up until midnight last night with GTA 6 is technically correct, but not the full picture. What I was actually doing was headshotting undead nightmares in Street Trash 3: Dezombifier, a moreish isometric twin-stick shooter that lives inside Jason's PS5-alike JoySystem.
You play it briefly with Cal during the story, and it impressed me enough to want to beat it fully. After nearly four hours, I still haven't.
It's clearly inspired by the Sony-published Dead Nation from 2010, but it mashes the zombie slaying with a roguelike deckbuilder. Every time an enemy squelches, their blood splattering the pavement, you gain XP, and at each new level you pick a card to adjust your build. Basic cards boost movement speed, fire rate, damage or critical hit chance, but rarer cards transform your tactics, such as one that doubles your damage given and received.
Your goal is to collect six gears strewn around a deceptively large map and leave via a rooftop chopper. You'll die a lot, but on each run you'll learn a little more about the world's layout and discover new cards to experiment with.
The satisfaction of Street Trash 3 is in crafting a build on the fly from the cards you're dealt, and in feeling your power spiral. You're constantly levelling up and that escalation, the way your build rapidly grows and branches depending on what you find, reminds me a lot of Vampire Survivors. Like that game, it's most fun when you lean into a specific skill: the closest I've come to victory was when I swapped my pistol for a micro-SMG, buffing and buffing my fire rate until the bullets streamed like a deadly snake.
I also got deep on a run boosting my speed and stamina so I could infinitely sprint from groups of enemies, and another where – inspired by a detailed Reddit thread discussing the best builds – I found a card that improved my critical chance by 50% while standing still. When combined with other critical hit cards, I could basically stand in the same spot and mow down everything down around me, painting a gruesome circle of guts.
It is ludicrous that something as meaty and polished as Street Trash 3 exists inside another video game – and even more ludicrous that it's one of three games you can play on Jason's console (Rockstar told Kotaku that "an internal dev team within the dev team" makes these games).
But, like in real life, not every game available for the JoySystem is a banger. Of the three games on Jason’s hard drive, Time Jackers Rewind is the most forgettable. It's a stylish top-down shooter that plays a bit like a slower, sci-fi, less-good Hotline Miami. I appreciate how its checkpoints are tantalizingly spread far enough that you can't just sprint between them, close enough that after every death I was sure I was seconds away. Sadly its best weapon, a concussive shotgun that rumbles your controller, runs out of ammo after just a few seconds, and it's tricky to see enemies among all the on-screen effects.
Tenenbrae Lux is the third, a joke about auteurs stretched into an entire puzzle game. The developer is Johannes Zuch, a clear play on The Witness developer Jonathan Blow (Zuch/Suck, get it?), and his name is listed 38 times during the end credits, including as "architect as perception" and "left stick sensitivity tension consultant". This, surely, is a send-up of Hideo Kajima popping up multiple times in his own credits. The looping stairway in its central puzzle evokes Kojima's P.T. but if you squint you can also see riffs on Alan Wake, Dear Esther, and Robert Eggers’ black-and-white 2019 film The Lighthouse.
As a puzzler, it's intriguing. I say intriguing rather than fun because I can't say I really enjoyed it. The narrator's dialogue is deliberately grandiloquent and recondite ("Hark! An unearthly sound beckons. Like the sirens call, I am fated to find its source"), and the central puzzle is mind-bendingly complicated. Without completley spoiling it, you must loop through a series of rooms in a bunker and spot anomalies, cross-reference them with other clues to assign them a number, and then arrange those numbers in chronological order using a calendar in each room.
Rewarding when you've solved it, for sure, but a pain when you're trying.
The whole thing takes a couple of hours to complete, less if you look up the strategy guides than now exist across the internet (although note that the bunker puzzle is different for each player). And so if you want to beat all of the games on Jason's console, then, you'll need roughly five hours at the very least. That's a pretty impressive amount of game that's not Grand Theft Auto 6 in your copy of Grand Theft Auto 6.
What I love most about these three games is that they are entirely optional, like the majority of GTA 6. There is so much craft that most players simply won't see, so many developer hours spent creating missable moments of delight, so many systems I haven't yet engaged with. It speaks to both Rockstar's budget and its confidence, and as a player it's comforting to know that pretty much wherever I turn I'll find something hand-crafted and surprising. No matter how much time I put in, I couldn't possibly see everything.
What I can do, though, is see everything in Street Trash 3: Dezombifier. Back in I wade, for one more run.
