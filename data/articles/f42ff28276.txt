Russian President Vladimir Putin has proposed a humanitarian mission in the city of Kostiantynivka similar to a recent operation in Oleshky. According to Putin, Russia has control of the city in the Donbas; the remaining civilian population, he says, could be evacuated. It would take five days to clear the access roads. The Russian leader has suggested that human rights organizations from Ukraine and Russia and delegates from the foreign press be incorporated into the mission.
Putin's proposal comes after discussions between the Russian government and US representatives, who have been negotiating to try to bring an end to Russia's war against Ukraine. Experts say that while Russia is indicating a readiness to make agreements, at the same time it is propagating its own depiction of the situation on the front line.
In July, Russia announced that it had taken Kostiantynivka but did not provide any proof. Ukraine says the fighting there is still ongoing. Experts from the Ukrainian open-source interactive map of military operations project DeepState say that the city is still controlled by the Ukrainian army, but that the situation there is "extremely difficult."
Responding to the proposal, Ukrainian President Volodymyr Zelenskyy told journalists: "Putin simply wants us to leave Kostiantynivka so that they can move in and occupy the entire city." He emphasized that Ukraine had always given the civilian population in Kostiantynivka opportunities to leave, and said that Russia had used the temporary ceasefire in Oleshky to bring military equipment and weapons into the city.
What is the situation for civilians?
Kostiantynivka is the southernmost city in the so-called Donetsk "fortress belt," which also includes Druzhkivka, Kramatorsk and Sloviansk. Taking Kostiantynivka would open the way for Russian troops to advance to Druzhkivka, and on to the conurbation of Sloviansk and Kramatorsk.
Oleksandr Kovalenko, a military expert at the Dmytro Tymchuk Center for Security Studies, a Ukrainian think tank, said Kostiantynivka's role is similar to that of Bakhmut, Avdiivka or Chasiv Yar — towns whose defensive installations were able to hold back Russian enemy forces for years.
Local authorities say the humanitarian situation in the city, whose population before the start of Russia's full-scale invasion was around 70,000, is catastrophic and that some 340 civilians remain. They add thatKostiantynivkahas no central water, electricity or gas supplies, there is no public transport, and since the spring of 2026 people have only been able to evacuate on foot.
"Those who stayed in Kostiantynivka need to be evacuated, they need a chance of a better life," military analyst Denys Popovych told DW. However, he added that an evacuation was virtually impossible without a ceasefire, as Russian troops had occupied the road between the town and Druzhkivka and drones were being deployed throughout the region. Ukraine, therefore, could not carry out such an operation unilaterally. "It still takes two to tango — to evacuate the people, that is," he said.
Humanitarian proposals as leverage
Kovalenko believes that Moscow is simply using the humanitarian issue for purposes of manipulation and to strengthen its negotiating position. The Russian army, he said, wants to gain time in order to reinforce units fighting in the vicinity.
Yevhen Zakharov, the director of the Kharkiv Human Rights Protection Group, has similar concerns. "I'm afraid that this proposal by the Russians has very different objectives to those they claim to pursue," he told DW. He described Putin's initiative as suspect, and said the Ukrainian side should treat it with the utmost caution.
Kyiv is in a difficult position, as the idea of a mission for Kostiantynivka is also being pushed by US intermediaries. "If we reject it, they'll say to us: 'You're rejecting peace proposals, you're not taking care of your own citizens, why should we keep talking to you, Zelenskyy wants to continue the war.' This is precisely the calculation behind it all," said Popovych.
Ceasefire as military tactic
After the end of the ceasefire in Oleshky, which was in place from October 27 to November 1, the Ukrainian General Staff announced that, in that time, the Russians had rotated their troops, entrenched their positions, reinforced measures to repel drones and brought in ammunition.
Kovalenko is convinced that the reason Russia is proposing a ceasefire is because its army is gradually losing ground in Kostiantynivka. From Moscow's perspective, the Russian troops that are meant to be storming the city are in urgent need of reinforcements, which they can't get because of the fighting on the ground.
"If a so-called humanitarian mission were to take place in Kostiantynivka, it would be a disaster for Kyiv, because Russia would use that time to consolidate forces that would enable it to take the city," he warned.
How should Kyiv respond to Moscow's proposal?
Popovych, however, does not think Ukraine should reject the Russian proposal. In his view, Kyiv should base its tactics on experiences with previous ceasefires and insist on very clear conditions in advance. "If there is a ceasefire, soldiers currently at their posts should stay there and not move," he suggested.
Oleksandr Pavlichenko from the Ukrainian Helsinki Human Rights Union believes Ukraine should submit a counter-proposal, which envisages the withdrawal of Russian troops from the Ukrainian city and the unimpeded departure of the civilian population.
The human rights activist said that, for a genuine humanitarian mission to be put in place, a strong, neutral third party should be brought in to supervise and ensure safe passage. In his view, the only party with such a mandate is the International Committee of the Red Cross. "If a proposal comes from only one side, and from a side such as Russia, it doesn't inspire confidence," he said.
