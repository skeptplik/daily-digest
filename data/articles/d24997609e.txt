For New Scientist’s 70th birthday, I attempted to forecast what the world will be like another 70 years in the future, in the year 2096. I interviewed dozens of experts to inform what I wrote, though of course they did not always agree with me or each other – and the vision of the future I produced is mine alone. Nevertheless, I thought it would be interesting for readers to hear directly from some of the biggest names in science about their hopes and dreams for the end of the century. Here they are in their own words.
Jennifer Doudna, co-inventor of the CRISPR gene-editing system:
“I’d be very excited if we could get to a place where it’s possible to use CRISPR in a preventive fashion. Rather than treating disease after it’s happened, thinking about ways to protect people from Alzheimer’s, Parkinson’s or similar diseases. Eliminating or even reducing incidents of these age-associated degenerative disorders would have an enormous impact. It would mean that people are able to continue doing the kind of work that they want to do, and contributing to the economy, to their families, in ways that currently can be limited. As I get older, it sounds very appealing to be able to turn the page and have another chapter that maybe wouldn’t have been possible otherwise.”
Sally Davies, former chief medical officer for England:
“The divide between rich and poor is likely to be ever wider, and the poor will have the problems of lack of water, lack of access to healthcare, lack of access to good food and maybe lack of access to effective vaccines. As we go forward, how has community lasted? How has education lasted? And how has trust lasted? And how does populism and social media play into what you believe? I think we’re going to see dreadful divergences. And with that, of course, you get social unrest and war.”
Devi Sridhar, public health expert and author of Preventable:
“When people moved out of manual labour into desk-based jobs, physical activity levels dropped, and we realised that is bad for us – we have to move, we have to exercise for longevity, for our mental health, for lowered risk for a whole bunch of diseases. People brought that into their leisure time. They started going to gyms, they started going to yoga and doing triathlons. I wonder now, if we’re replacing so much of thinking with AI, will we realise the mental consequences of not thinking? What will it do to our levels of happiness, our levels of dementia, our ability to be human? Are we now going to have places people go to use their brain as a muscle?”
Tim Spector, co-founder of gut health company Zoe:
“Food will be recognised as biological information, not just fuel. The gut microbiome will become part of every person’s medical record. Maintaining microbial diversity will be considered as important as monitoring blood pressure or cholesterol once was. Personalised nutrition will replace generic dietary advice, with food recommendations tailored to each person’s microbiome, genetics, metabolism, immune system and lifestyle, and AI acting as a personal nutrition coach. Most long-term conditions will be prevented or controlled through personalised nutrition and early biological monitoring. The greatest medical breakthrough is prevention. Instead of asking, ‘How do we treat disease?’, doctors will ask, ‘How do we maintain the biological ecosystem that keeps people healthy?'”
Chris Hadfield, former Canadian Space Agency astronaut:
“One of the big questions we haven’t answered is this: what is the minimum gravity required for a child to grow and develop normally? If we discover that we can’t raise children on the moon, then it will always just be a temporary outpost for adults, like Antarctica. And hopefully we’ll have something like the Antarctic Treaty System for the moon, so that, despite our eternally imperfect human greed and short-term thinking and insecurity and selfishness, we can still manage to find a way to settle the moon that is beneficial with long-term human presence.”
