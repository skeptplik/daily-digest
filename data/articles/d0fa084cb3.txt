The corruption case against Benjamin Netanyahu just suffered a serious blow. What comes next?
Israeli prime minister faces charges of bribery, fraud and breach of trust in years-long corruption trial
Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu was handed a major legal victory this week when the judges in the years-long corruption trial against him suggested the prosecution reconsider one case, work out a plea deal in another and entirely drop the third — the most serious.
While the court clarified these were recommendations and not rulings, it was still a significant blow to the prosecution, which has sought to convict the prime minister on a series of charges related to allegations of bribery, fraud and breach of trust after he was indicted in 2019.
The prime minister has denied any wrongdoing, casting the charges as a witch hunt by hostile media, police and prosecutors.
But now, the future of the trial is in serious doubt as prosecutors decide how to proceed.
Here's a look at the cases, the charges, what the judges said and where it may go next.
What is Netanyahu charged with?
Netanyahu is facing charges of bribery, fraud and breach of trust in three separate cases known as case 1000, case 2000 and case 4000.
On Wednesday, the judges overseeing the trial summoned Israel's Attorney General Gali Baharav-Miara to appear at the Jerusalem District Court.
"That never happens," Yael Friedman, the legal correspondent for the Israeli newspaper Haaretz, told CBC News. She has been covering the trial since it began in May 2020. "You just need to understand that it was a very irregular act."
Speaking for the court, Judge Rivka Friedman-Feldman proceeded to lay out their concerns or recommendations for each of the three cases.
Case 1000
Charges: fraud, breach of trust
In this case, Netanyahu is accused of accepting nearly $200,000 in gifts from Hollywood producer Arnon Milchan and Australian billionaire James Packer.

Prosecutors allege that Netanyahu lobbied on Milchan’s behalf on U.S. visa matters, tried to legislate a generous tax break for him and sought to promote his interests in the Israeli media market.
What the judges said: Work out a plea deal or settlement
“It seems to us that it is possible to get to a conclusion," Friedman-Feldman said, according to the Times of Israel. "We are open to all possibilities — we need to find the procedural way to get there."
Friedman told CBC News that she believes the court was suggesting that though there could be a conviction, the two sides should instead get together to come up with some kind of plea arrangement to finish the trial.
Case 2000
Charges: fraud, breach of trust
This case centres around two audio recordings of conversations between Netanyahu and Arnon Mozes in 2014.
Mozes is the the publisher of Yedioth Ahronoth, one of Israel's largest newspapers and Ynet, one of the largest news websites.

At right, Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanyahu and U.S. President Donald Trump speak at the White House in January 2020, as U.S. casino magnate Sheldon Adelson looks on. (Reuters, Alex Wong/Getty Images)
Netanyahu was accused of offering to put forward legislation that would have helped restrict the circulation of the free daily newspaper Israel Hayom, also known as Israel Today, which is the main rival to Mozes' paper.
The irony is that Israel Hayom (Israel Today) is owned by the family of Sheldon Adelson, the late U.S. billionaire and casino magnate, who was also a supporter of Netanyahu.
Mozes has denied any wrongdoing.
What the judges said: Reconsider
The judges recommended prosecutors reconsider case 2000.
"Legally, this is a very, very problematic, case," said Moshe Cohen-Eliya, law professor and former president of the Tel Aviv-based College of Law and Business.
"What the court actually said is that basically there is no legal offence," Cohen-Eliya said in an interview with CBC News. "Maybe you have very good evidence about these kinds of negotiation between Netanyahu and Mozes. However there is no offence."
According to Friedman, the judge said that while she doesn't see the court convicting Netanyahu on case 2000, she gave the prosecution a chance to explain the charges.
"They're leaving it a bit open," Friedman said.
Case 4000
Charges: bribery, fraud, breach of trust
This was considered the most serious of the three cases as it includes the charge of bribery.
Netanyahu, while serving as both primer minister and communications minister from 2014 to 2017, was accused of seeking to ease regulations that would have benefitted Shaul Elovitch, then the controlling shareholder in Bezeq, Israel's largest telecommunications company.
Israeli businessman Shaul Elovitch appears in the Justice Court in Tel Aviv in one of the graft probes threatening Netanyahu on Feb. 22, 2018. Elovitch is accused of having given Netanyahu positive coverage on his Walla news site in exchange for policies benefiting his telecommunications business. (Jack Guez/AFP/Getty Images)
In return, it's alleged that the news website Walla, owned by Elovitch at the time, would provide positive coverage of Netanyahu and his family.
The regulatory favours would be worth around 1.8 billion shekels (about $500 million US) to Bezeq.
Elovitch has denied any wrongdoing.
What the judges said: Consider dropping the charges
As for case 4000, in relation to the bribery charge, Friedman-Feldman said there was "serious doubt as to whether the evidentiary basis supports this claim." The Times of Israel reported that she said "the chances of conviction are low" for all three charges.
This wasn't the first time the judges have raised concerns about the ability to prove the bribery charge. In May 2023, judges said prosecutors should consider withdrawing the charge, but the prosecutors declined to do so.
"So, from that perspective, it was already kind of like known that [the bribery charge] was the more weaker charge," Friedman said. "But what was surprising was how they basically overruled the whole 4000 case."
Has Netanyahu testified?
Netanyahu has testified in 98 hearings in 18 months of the trial, while Israel was at war in Gaza, Lebanon and Iran.
"It was quite crazy, the situation where he gets, like, this note from one of his secretaries saying he needs to take an urgent call," Friedman said. "And he goes up to the judges and says, 'Um, excuse me, I need to take a break.' "
She says Netanyahu's testimony, which included many breaks within the 18 month time span, is the longest in Israel's history of criminal trials, and also notes that Netanyahu would show up for the testimony of major witnesses.

What will the prosecution do?
The prosecution said it will take time to review the recommendations of the court. But Cohen-Eliya said he believes the attorney general will ignore the judges and continue with the trial.
"I can guess that she would go back to the court, and she would say, 'I heard you, but I'm still pursuing the case,' " he said.
Cohen-Eliya says the corruption case against Netanyahu is the most important one in Israel's history besides the trial of Nazi Adolf Eichmann in the 1960s.
"It's a lot at stake for the prosecution," he said.
Friedman says if the prosecutors "believe in the case and think that the judges are wrong" they could continue with the trial, get a verdict — even one that goes against them — and then appeal to the Israeli Supreme Court.
Another option, she says, is to start negotiating a plea deal.
"And then it depends how much they would want to compromise. And what would Netanyahu agree [to] compromise from his side?"
