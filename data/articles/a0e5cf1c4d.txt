In early November, some internet service providers warned their customers that pre-downloads of Grand Theft Auto 6 were causing network slowdown issues for everyone on the service. That problem became visible before millions of gamers tried to log into the same game simultaneously. A long list of recent outages and issues across industries reliant on tech and network services now makes it seem likely that the biggest game launch of all time won't go down smoothly.
If you've ever been around for the release of a big new game, you know that day-one network issues are fairly common. Some hiccups are worse than others, of course, but most people are probably already bracing for some potential hitches when we're talking about the biggest game launch of all time. What makes GTA 6 different from other game releases is its scale and timing.
The highly anticipated sequel first began causing issues for some ISPs when Rockstar opened preloads for the 212 GB game on Nov. 10. At the time, some providers claimed that the game was directly responsible for never-before-seen traffic peaks so high that they congested the proverbial internet tubes for everyone else.
Rockstar undoubtedly foresaw that the problem could occur or worsen by launch day. The studio dropped a couple of patches in a staggered rollout prior to launch. The release cadence made it less likely that GTA 6 fans could overload Rockstar's, PlayStation's, and Xbox's servers at the same time. Service providers have also been gearing up for Nov. 19, with at least one ISP advertising that it would offer increased download speeds specifically for GTA 6.
Yet network issues of some sort, both in the game and the internet at large, may be unavoidable on Thursday. Recent trends show that many online services are already hanging on by a thread. Earlier in November, flights across the country were halted due to apparent issues with aging routers that could not handle the amount of data shared by newer pieces of equipment. During that same week, Microsoft products like Outlook and Excel went dark for nearly 24 hours because a faulty gateway was overwhelmed by the amount of data passing through it. On Nov. 16, customers were unable to access accounts at major banks like Chase or through payment services like Zelle.
On Nov. 17, Apple users reported widespread issues with iCloud, AppleCare, App Store, and Apple Music. Apple has not revealed the cause of the issues, noting only that outages are slowing down services. On that same Tuesday, news broke that the United States' national security network also went down after weeks of wonkiness.
The quick succession of these instances has led to conspiracy theories online that they're the result of cyberattacks. So far, there's no evidence to support such suspicions in relation to recent newsworthy outages. It's probable that the specific causes of all of these events vary widely, but there's an overarching theme among all of them. Online services, by and large, are built on fragile interconnected systems. Some of those systems rely on old hardware and software that are buckling under normal but increasing strain. Even modernizing systems are not immune — those efforts may actually be responsible for the sudden tech difficulties.
Kris Beevers, CEO of a network and infrastructure company, told the BBC on Monday, “I expect we’ll see more meaningful outages in the infrastructure that essential parts of society depend on in coming months as the invisible infrastructure that our world depends on becomes more strained and visible.”
Since GTA 6 is already responsible for an internet-wide slowdown by some ISPs, it may not be a bad idea to preload the game and its updates ASAP. The same advice applies to those who have purchased physical copies — the game has to be downloaded from Rockstar. Nov. 19 will still probably be rocky for the game if not for the entire internet. But hey: if GTA 6 took over a decade to get here, what's another day?
