Some American retailers that received refunds from the federal government following the invalidation of President Donald Trump’s “reciprocal” tariffs in February have opted to funnel those funds into executive bonuses instead of making consumers whole.
TJX brands TJ Maxx and Marshalls were among a handful of companies named in a ProPublica report released Monday for disclosing their intent to give bonuses to leadership instead of reimbursing shoppers that paid higher prices for tariff-impacted products. Other entities, including golf brand Titleist and tool company Stanley Black & Decker, were fingered for doing the same.
During the second quarter of Fiscal 2027, TJX received aggregate tariff refunds of $331 million “for a portion of the IEEPA tariffs it previously paid,” a press release from Aug. 19, which detailed the company’s Q2 fiscal 2027 earnings, said. The refunds benefitted the firm’s 2027 cost of sales, excluding the 0.6 percent negative impact stemming from the extra compensation to certain individuals.
A TJX spokesperson confirmed these details to Sourcing Journal, writing, “TJX received aggregate refunds of $331 million for a portion of the IEEPA tariffs we previously paid. Due to these tariff refunds, the Company accrued incremental expenses of $112 million for year-end incentive compensation and discretionary bonuses for eligible Associates globally.”
“Given that this is an accrual, and we have not reached the end of our current fiscal year, we have not disclosed any breakout beyond this. The net benefit of tariff refunds was $219 million for the Company’s second quarter Fiscal 2027 pretax profit. The remaining amount will be used to support our future growth,” they added.
The federal government has, as of August, processed around $138.3 billion in tariff refund claims through Customs and Border Protection’s (CBP) Consolidated Administration and Processing of Entries (CAPE) electronic system out of about $166 billion owed to importers following the Supreme Court’s ruling on Trump’s International Emergency Economic Powers Act (IEEPA) tariffs.
The payouts have slowed, however, with somewhere between $28 billion to $50 billion tied up in pending claims or processing. The government is still collecting tariffs, too, under Section 301, Section 122, Section 338 and other tariff statutes that weren’t impacted by the high court’s decision—meaning that it’s now raking in more than it’s giving back.
Some companies that received refunds have vowed to share the windfall with consumers. Shippers like FedEx, UPS and DHL—which charged customers and clients for the added import taxes—have said they’ll dispense refunds directly to those impacted.
Others, like Costco, Walmart and Target, have made more nebulous claims about indirectly paying back shoppers through lowered prices. All three big-box stores have been hit with class-action lawsuits from disgruntled shoppers who believe they’re entitled to their part of the hundreds of millions of dollars refunded by CBP, as all three openly copped to raising prices to offset the cost of tariffs.
Burlington also vowed to share its refunds with shoppers with a slightly more transparent approach. During an earnings call in August, CEO Michael O’Sullivan said the off-price retailer, which operates over 1,000 stores across the country, received $55 million in tariff refunds.
“Rather than taking a one-time boost to earnings, we intend to fully invest these refunds back into the business in the back-half of the year, to deliver even sharper values to our shoppers,” he said. “Over the last few years, the rising cost of living has made life difficult for many customers. At Burlington, we already offer great deals. We plan to use the refunds to make these deals even better.”
“Given our intent to invest the refunds in sharper values, we expect the direct impact of tariff refunds to be neutral to full year earnings,” he added.
With refund recipients handling the issue in vastly different manners, Senate Democrats aren’t willing to leave it up to companies to opt to act ethically. Late last week, a group of seven lawmakers led by Massachusetts Senator Elizabeth Warren wrote to the Federal Trade Commission (FTC) urging an investigation into the “giant corporations that are pocketing tariff refunds after raising prices on consumers.”
In a letter to FTC Chairman Andrew Ferguson, the senators pushed the agency to probe these actions to uncover whether companies like Walmart, Target, Nike, Energizer and Apple violated federal law in retaining refunds for their own use instead of passing them back to the consumers that paid the tariffs.
According to the group, which included Senators Richard Blumenthal (D-Conn.), Sheldon Whitehouse (D-R.I.), Elissa Slotkin (D-Mich.), Tammy Baldwin (D-Wis.), Cory Booker (D-N.J.), Ed Markey (D-Mass.), and Mark Kelly (D-Ariz.), “the American consumer—who paid almost the full cost of the IEEPA tariffs—have received no direct refunds or restitution.”
The letter also called out the Trump Administration, saying it has “sidestepped Congressional inquiries about how American consumers will get their money back.”
“Not a single one of these corporations provided any assurance that they will actually pass along refunds directly to their consumers,” wrote the senators, who estimated that shoppers paid about 95 percent of the tariff costs, amounting to an average of $2,500 per American household.
The senators highlighted the responses they received from the companies in question, citing Walmart and Target’s unclear promises of promotions and lower prices, Nike’s total lack of explanation for how it planned to allocate the funds and Apple’s supposed investments in U.S. operations. “Not one of the five companies committed to directly paying back customers who covered the cost of the tariffs,” they said.
Others were also put on blast by the lawmakers while not being named explicitly as targets for investigation, including Wayfair, whose CEO referred to the refunds as “sort of a one-time working capital benefit companies will get” in an earnings call. Levi Strauss & Co. said it planned to use its sizable refund on holiday season marketing.
This “appears to be a massive theft from the middle class, facilitated by the Trump Administration and giant corporations that raised prices for middle class consumers in response to Trump tariffs and now are now pocketing their refunds,” the letter said.
These actions amount to deceptive practices and unfair methods of competition—both activities that the FTC is charged with investigating, it added. Retaining the tariff refunds could give companies a leg up on their competitors, and consumers are left in the dark about who actually deserves payback for money spent.
“The agency should act quickly to hold accountable any corporation that is ripping off consumers,” they concluded.
