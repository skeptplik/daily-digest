Many women work through their periods despite severe pain. One restaurateur from the Allgäu exempts affected female staff from duty. But how much consideration is appropriate at work?
When orders pile up in a restaurant, there is little time to catch your breath. For female employees with severe period pain, long hours on their feet, carrying heavy plates and working under time pressure in the kitchen and in service can be particularly hard.
At the Schlosskeller restaurant in Bad Hindelang in Bavaria they are not supposed to have to battle through their shift at any price. According to his own account, owner Peter Brutscher plans around their complaints when drawing up the rota.
On Instagram the restaurateur writes that for years he has deliberately taken female employees with severe period pain off the rota for two to four days, completely "without discussion".
Cramps, nausea or circulatory problems are physical limitations which, especially in the demanding hospitality sector, need to be taken seriously. His principle is: "The female cycle should not be ignored, but understood".
Not everyone has been understanding about this. "Of course I have been laughed at for it," Brutscher told the Allgäuer Zeitung. According to its report, his experience with one colleague prompted him to pay closer attention to such complaints when planning shifts.
How this consideration is organised is crucial, however. The magazine Focus reports that female employees can move shifts to another week. This does not automatically mean additional paid days off.
Many women work despite severe complaints
The fact that Brutscher is addressing a widespread problem is shown in a new study by the Institute for Employment Research (IAB), the research body of the Federal Employment Agency based in Nuremberg.
According to the study, 63% of the women surveyed feel limited in their daily work tasks by menstruation-related complaints, and 23% feel strongly limited.
For the study, a total of 2,107 women in dependent employment between 25-55 years of age were surveyed in spring 2026. They had all had their period in the previous six months.
According to the IAB, the weighted survey provides the first representative data on this topic for Germany.
The extent of the burden can hardly be read from days of absence alone. Only 13% had missed at least one full working day in the six-month period because of their complaints. Even among women who felt strongly impaired, 56% had no days off sick. Many therefore keep working even though they find their day-to-day tasks difficult.
In research this phenomenon is called presenteeism, which can go hand in hand with reduced concentration, resilience and productivity.
Menstruation still a taboo
There is also the concern about being perceived differently at work. More than 80% of respondents reject the idea that women should hide menstrual complaints in the workplace. At the same time, 64% report doing exactly that. More than half believe women would be seen as less resilient if they spoke about it openly.
Flexible working hours, longer breaks or working from home can ease the burden for those affected, but these options are not available to everyone. According to the IAB survey, 45% can arrange their working hours flexibly, and 37% can work from home.
Women with severe complaints in particular often make use of these possibilities, provided their job offers them.
Working from home is not an option in kitchens and front-of-house service. Other forms of relief can help here, such as more flexible shifts. Similar approaches exist in care work: RTL Nord recently reported on the concept of the intensive care service "Kunterbunte Butterfliege" in Grasberg, Lower Saxony.
The organisation takes account of the cycle and complaints of its female employees if they wish. Such models require staff to be able to express their needs and for work to be organised dynamically within the team.
Researchers recommend more flexibility
Menstrual complaints should also be taken more into account when designing working conditions. This is the view of a team led by Eva Schultz from the Institute of Occupational, Social and Environmental Medicine at Goethe University Frankfurt. In their review "The influence of the menstrual cycle on everyday working life" the authors summarised the existing research.
They describe how cramps, back and headaches, fatigue or difficulty concentrating can impair work. Tabooing the subject and a lack of flexibility in the workplace make the situation even more difficult.
As possible forms of support, the team mentions flexible working hours, working from home and more education in the workplace. At the same time, the authors call for caution when assessing the existing research. Many findings are based on retrospective information from the women surveyed, without their complaints being checked by a doctor.
Which measures are particularly effective therefore still needs to be investigated.
Spain as a pioneer
While Brutscher relies on arrangements within the business, Spain has created special legal protection. Since 1 June 2023, women there can obtain a special sick note for menstrual complaints that make them unable to work. This requires a medical certificate, and the state covers the financial security. The common term "menstruation leave" is therefore misleading. What is meant is health-related absence, not extra days off for rest.
Portugal has since followed with a more narrowly defined rule. Since April 2025, employees with severe menstrual pain as a result of doctor-confirmed endometriosis or adenomyosis can take up to three consecutive days of paid leave per month.
Now Spain is even extending its approach to certain conditions associated with the menopause. From 1 December 2026, affected employees will be able to obtain a special sick note if a medically diagnosed condition temporarily makes it impossible for them to work. The menopause alone does not create an entitlement.
Consideration must not become a disadvantage
A statutory special rule and flexible rota planning like Brutscher’s in the Oberallgäu are different ways of dealing with health burdens.
A voluntary shift swap is not a substitute for a sick note. In Germany, the general rules on illness apply to incapacity for work due to menstrual complaints.
The question is therefore not only what kind of support a company offers, but also how naturally employees can use it. The IAB survey illustrates the obstacle: more than half of women fear that an open approach to menstrual complaints will be taken as a sign of lower resilience.
In his restaurant, Brutscher relies on explicitly acknowledging such complaints. But for an offer like this to really provide relief, trust is crucial above all. Those who accept support should not have to fear for their professional reputation.
