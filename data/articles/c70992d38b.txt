Football clubs representing hundreds of thousands of players are preparing to break away from Football Queensland this week, saying the state governing body charges unreasonably high fees and is unaccountable.
Some parents and siblings are charged a $5 gate fee to watch children’s matches in parts of regional Queensland.
The gate fee comes on top of an annual cost of between $300 and $700 per family, depending on player age, club and other factors, and a range of other fees, fines and charges. Many football club members say they see little in return for the thousands they pay in fees.
Jessy Mulla is president of Leichhardt Football Club Cairns Incorporated, which was recently disaffiliated from the statewide body.
Football Queensland, which manages the club, introduced a gate fee in 2024 in order to pay for improvements to the ground, Endeavour Park, in Manunda, such as cleaning toilets, employing a full-time gardener, and “increased shade structures and grandstands”, at a quoted price of $20,000 each.
With a few hundred people attending most weekends, the amount raised through gate fees must be substantial, Mulla said.
But there had been “very, very” little improvement to the ground since, Mulla said.
“That’s why everyone asks where’s the money being spent? Where’s the money that gets raised? Where is it going?”
Under Football Queensland’s policy, registered coaches and team managers, referees, volunteers and children under 15 enter free. Pensioners get a discount, paying $4.
The sport’s governing body in the state is a member of the national football body, Football Australia. It provides a range of services for typically volunteer-run clubs, including managing competitions, governance, and developing player professional pathways, as well as managing some facilities.
Clubs have to pay an annual fee to affiliate with the central body, which they pass on to players.
A spokesperson for Football Queensland said the body was “continuing to progress the delivery and investment of long-term infrastructure projects and facility enhancements” in line with its regional facility master plan.
The spokesperson said three levels of government had invested $7.9m into regional Football Queensland facilities since 2023. The central body had also contributed $4.9m in operational costs and capital investment in regional Queensland during the same period, they said.
“A $5 gate fee (or $80 season pass) is consistent with other sporting codes and contributes to FQ’s master plan delivery, helping to offset grounds maintenance and operational expenses,” they said.
It recently created controversy by proposing to charge a $35 per player fee for summer competitions organised by the clubs themselves, and proposing other fee increases, which have since been dropped.
A rebel faction of football clubs is preparing to break away from Football Queensland this week, complaining of a lack of input and transparency of the central body.
Bardon Latrobe football club president, Gerard Doyle, said more than 100 clubs – a majority – had joined a movement pushing for reform.
Doyle estimated that the breakaway clubs represented more than 100,000 football players.
The group is now negotiating with the central body via a law firm. Others were on the fence, he said.
According to its bylaws, Football Queensland’s governing committee must hold elections for new board members this month.
But the board has yet to call elections.
Doyle said that it was now impossible for the board to meet its bylaws, given the need for a one-week period for people to nominate.
Meanwhile, clubs have been given until Wednesday, 25 November, to re-affiliate with the central authority, after an extension to the sign-up period.
Clubs have two options, he said.
“Number one is Football Australia comes in today or tomorrow, two days, and says we will facilitate open and free elections,” he said.
If that doesn’t happen, the clubs will refuse to affiliate and pay their fees and instead create their own rival central body, he said.
“I sound like I’m in a dictatorship in an African nation here, fighting for basic rights.
“To me, it just feels, I’m going to say un-Australian … it’s undemocratic.”
