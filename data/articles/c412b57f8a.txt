Bot and fraud protection firm Human Security says it has disrupted the largest ad fraud botnet by bid volume.
Dubbed BadBox 3.0, it is the third iteration of a large operation targeting low-cost Android devices. The first BadBox botnet ensnared more than 190,000 devices, while BadBox 2.0 was initially found on over 1 million devices, but eventually ensnared more than 10 million.
“At its height, BadBox 3.0 accounted for roughly 40 billion bid requests a day and more than 4% of all CTV traffic observed by the Human Defense Platform,” Human Security says, noting the bid request volume was three times the volume recorded for the VastFlux ad fraud scheme.
Portions of BadBox 3.0, the cybersecurity firm says, were operated via command-and-control (C&C) domains that were disrupted when Google took down XCPE, a botnet of over 1.3 million Android set-top boxes.
The botnet consists of low-cost, uncertified Android Open Source Project devices infected with backdoors delivering fraud modules designed to render hidden video ads and to enroll the devices into residential proxy networks.
Human Security says its Satori Threat Intelligence and Research Team has uncovered an actively maintained catalog of fraud modules hosted on public repositories, as well as links to Chinese electronics manufacturer TXCZ. Some of the modules were previously seen in BadBox and BadBox 2.0.
“Most modules in the catalog are residential proxy modules and the droppers that install them, but the collection also carries new ad fraud modules, and researchers traced both payloads to the same infrastructure and code,” Human Security notes.
Mimicking video ad software development kits (SDKs), the botnet’s ad fraud modules render ads in a hidden window, making the bid requests appear as coming from known, legitimate apps that the threat actors impersonated.
While the device owner does not see the ads, the supply chain sees a premium CTV inventory consisting of well-known applications that likely aren’t even installed on the infected devices, along with residential IP addresses and full video ads. In some cases, the threat actors relied on server-side ad insertion (SSAI) to monetize application spoofing.
“The volume reached roughly 40 billion bid requests a day, and much of it arrived from IP addresses associated with residential proxy networks, which helped it pass as ordinary household viewing,” Human Security notes.
Proxyjacking modules deployed on the same devices enrolled them in eleven residential proxy networks, including a rebranded version of IpMoYu, now named PXYEDGE, the network operated by the threat actors behind BadBox and BadBox 2.0.
Human Security has found several links between BadBox 3.0 and the Chinese company TXCZ: the modules check for TXCZ build properties, a TXCZ domain serves payload download routes and configurations for the hidden ads, and Human linked TXCZ to repositories distributing ad fraud launchers.
In addition to public repositories, the threat actor uses a second code platform and a file server to host payloads. For some payloads, it also uses a free open-source CDN that mirrors public repositories. The operators also use a self-hosted GitHub proxy.
Human Security has not provided an estimated size of BadBox 3.0, but noted that the mirror for one repository served over 367,000 requests over a month, which does not reflect the number of infections.
“These are requests rather than unique devices, and some come from researchers and scanners. It should be made very clear: these numbers are only for one repository, and only of the third backup delivery mechanism. The actual number of BADBOX 3.0 infections can be presumed to be significantly higher,” the cybersecurity firm notes.
