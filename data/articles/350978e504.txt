Fancì Club’s corsets have done most of their selling under stage lights, worn by K-pop stars, including a member of Blackpink, in front of arenas full of fans. This week the Vietnamese label made its U.S. retail debut on Revolve, its first major retail partner, betting that those fans want the look offstage too.
The launch is part of a broader change in how the retailer scouts. Over the past two years 52 percent of the new brands Revolve has added have been based outside the U.S., according to the company. Its buyers are now searching Australia, India and Asia.
For Divya Mathur, Revolve’s chief merchant, the change starts with the customer. “Consumers for the first time have a real willingness and excitement to try brands they’ve never heard of,” she said. In her view, social media, microinfluencers, celebrities and music now drive discovery as much as retailers do.
That appetite is strongest among shoppers who want to wear something their friends haven’t already seen everywhere. Mathur calls this reaction to fast-spreading trends the “anti-algorithm effect.” The hunger for newness, she said, gives Revolve more confidence to take a chance on labels with no established U.S. presence.
Recent additions from Asia and South Asia include AFEW Rahul Mishra, Lovebirds, Jacques Wei, Source Unknown, Osoi, Verandah, Markgong and Shushu Tong.
A Viral Brand’s U.S. Debut
Mathur first noticed Fancì Club on celebrities. She said the team’s interest went beyond that visibility, though: It saw potential in the broader product and in its appeal to customers looking for distinctive occasionwear. The opening assortment centers on corsets, alongside dresses and skirts.
For Duy Tran, Fancì Club’s founder and creative director, a key part of the partnership is helping shoppers picture the designs in their own wardrobes, not just onstage.
“I think seeing how someone wears a piece can make you look at it differently,” Tran said. “You might see a corset styled with jeans and suddenly realize you’d wear it on a night out, even if you’d only ever pictured it onstage.”
That was part of what drew Tran to Revolve. “Through the creators they work with and events like Revolve Festival, people get to see clothes in different settings and imagine how a piece might fit into their own wardrobe before deciding to buy it,” Tran said. “That matters to us at Fancì Club, too, because many of our pieces are first seen on stage, and people need to picture them in their everyday lives.”
Many customers first find Fancì Club through an artist they follow, Tran added. A wider retail network makes the designs easier to discover and buy around the world.
Mathur said Revolve wants partnerships with room to grow, not one-season trends. That can mean working with a brand on the operational steps needed to serve U.S. shoppers and helping turn awareness into easier access.
Australia: A Model for Scaling
Australia has been especially productive for Revolve, Mathur said. Its aesthetic often lines up with the American customer, and its brands tend to arrive operationally ready. Sndys shows what that can look like. Since launching on Revolve in 2020, the label has grown its revenue roughly sevenfold, and exclusives make up 20 to 25 percent of its assortment on the site.
The work goes beyond picking pieces from a collection. Mathur described working with Australian designers on product development, monthly drops, core styles and stock plans. She said that approach has turned some of those brands into multimillion-dollar businesses on the site relatively quickly.
Bringing Indian Craftsmanship to a New Customer
In India, Revolve sees an opening in special-occasion dressing. Mathur said Indian designers can offer intricate embellishment and handwork at prices below comparable European luxury, which suits customers looking for standout pieces for events.
The retailer has launched Rahul Mishra and La Fuori and developed a capsule with Papa Don’t Preach. The ongoing challenge, Mathur said, is translating design perspectives rooted in Indian heritage and bridalwear for American consumers.
Finding the brands is only the first step. Mathur said Revolve checks whether emerging labels can meet the demands of a large U.S. retailer, including shipping requirements, consistent inventory and the ability to replenish orders. By her estimate, only about 10 percent of the emerging brands her team sees are ready to operate at that scale.
Tariffs have made international buying more complicated. Rather than drop orders, Mathur said, Revolve has worked with brands to share costs or shift production where possible.
“Getting on is just earning the right to compete in the race,” she said. “Now it’s ‘How do you stay in the race?'”
