Ancient human civilisations in the Amazon have had a lasting beneficial impact on the rainforest. Places with a history of human occupation have, on average, 2.8 per cent greater biomass than unoccupied areas. This could affect estimates of how much carbon is stored in these rainforests and help spot previously unknown historical human sites.
Past studies have revealed that human societies in the Amazon, before the arrival of Europeans, altered landscapes by domesticating certain plants and boosted crop productivity by piling up biodegradable waste to create particularly fertile soils known as dark earth, or terra preta.
To get a better idea of how historical human occupation of the Amazon – which is estimated to have involved many millions of people – has altered the forests, Chris Doughty at Northern Arizona University and his colleagues began by working much further north at the archaeological site of Xmakabatun, a Maya city in what is now Guatemala, which was occupied from about 400 BC to AD 800.
The site was chosen because there is a wealth of information about past occupation in the region from mapping it with high-resolution airborne laser imaging.
Doughty and his colleagues analysed the present-day forest along 29 transects through the now-overgrown ancient stone city. Each transect was 30 metres long; nine of them incorporated ancient buildings, 11 went through former agricultural terraces, and nine were in regions of forest that have never been modified.
“It is really incredible, because on the lidar you can see these perfectly preserved ruins, but when you’re actually there, you can’t see a thing,” says Doughty. “We couldn’t have done it without the lidar data directing us to these places.”
Among other factors, the team estimated the total amount of biomass in each transect by measuring the diameter of tree trunks and their height. They also measured leaf mass and identified how many of the trees were five frequently domesticated species, including the breadnut (Brosimum alicastrum), grown for food and timber, and the copal tree (Protium copal), grown for its resinous incense.
“We saw some significant structural differences,” says Doughty. Crucially, they discovered there was greater biomass where ancient human-built structures sat than on former agricultural terraces, or on undisturbed sites. At both the structures and terraced agricultural areas, there were also more of the frequently domesticated tree species. The trees with fruit often grow slowly, but end up bigger and have more biomass, says Doughty.
“Because we know that there are legacies of past soil modifications, fire and cultivation for modern plant communities, it seems logical that those activities have also altered plant trait compositions,” says Crystal McMichael at the University of Amsterdam, who wasn’t involved in the work.
To see if the same changes could be seen by remote sensing from above, Doughty and his colleagues looked at data on biomass and leaf traits in the region around Xmakabatun, gleaned via lidar studies and Sentinel-2 satellite observations. This revealed a reduction in leaf mass and higher biomass at more densely settled archaeological sites than at control sites with no evidence of past human modification.
With the pattern corroborated, the team moved on to the Amazon, where it is harder to trace past human habitation because communities there had little access to stone for building permanent structures, instead building with earth. They focused similar remote sensing techniques on 500 areas listed in archaeological data sets as featuring fertile dark earth and almost a thousand additional areas with earthworks known as geoglyphs – both implying past habitation. They compared these with 3000 randomly chosen control sites.
Overall, they found that forests at dark-earth sites were shorter but had about 6 per cent more foliage than control sites, and forests near geoglyphs were taller, so had more biomass overall despite the trees being about 20 per cent less bulky in their middle sections than those in control plots. From this, the team has estimated that ancient human occupation has increased modern Amazonian biomass by, on average, 2.8 per cent, equivalent to 0.8 per cent of the globe’s above-ground stores of carbon.
This might mean evaluations of the Amazon rainforest’s biomass – and thus how much carbon is stored there – are slightly overestimated, says Doughty, because current figures are based on surveys in accessible places, which are often where people have formerly lived, so may also be areas with higher-than-normal biomass.
Vinicius Peripato at the Tropical Ecosystems and Environmental Sciences Laboratory at Brazil’s National Institute for Space Research, who wasn’t involved in the research, says the work could help us understand inaccessible rainforest areas. “The ability to detect the effects of ancient human occupation on present-day forest structure using remote-sensing products allows us to investigate these patterns even in remote forest areas.”
The findings have another implication, says Doughty: they suggest it might be possible to spot previously unknown areas where humans have lived in the past. “You could say biomass is better explained where you have archaeology, but you could also potentially use biomass to predict archaeology,” he says.
