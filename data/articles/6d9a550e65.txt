Mahmud Tanzid, a 27-year-old private-sector employee in Dhaka, has postponed a trip he had planned for the first week of December. After weeks of unrest, particularly in Gopalganj, and with former Prime Minister Sheikh Hasina announcing her intention to return to Bangladesh on December 16, he fears the situation could deteriorate further. He is no longer certain what the coming weeks will bring as Ms. Hasina’s Awami League has already called a nationwide lockdown on November 29-30.
Whether Ms. Hasina actually lands in Dhaka on December 16 or not, she has already reshaped Bangladesh’s political calendar. Her announcement has turned the countdown to Victory Day, which marks the end of the nine-month Liberation War in 1971 and the official surrender of Pakistani forces to the joint command of Bangladeshi and Indian forces, into a test of political will.
The 79-year-old former Prime Minister intends to end more than two years of exile in India on Victory Day. It is the first time she has specified a date for her return. In doing so, the Awami League, which was banned by the post-Hasina interim administration, has transformed what began as a localised law-and-order crisis into a national contest over political legitimacy.
The Bangladesh Nationalist Party (BNP) government’s response has been uncompromising. State Minister for Foreign Affairs Humayun Kobir said Ms. Hasina could return only through extradition, effectively “in handcuffs”. Home Minister Salahuddin Ahmed reiterated the government’s position on Wednesday (November 25), saying “a convicted fugitive” could return only through the extradition process.
Dismissing the shifting deadlines — initially November, then December, and now December 16 — Mr. Ahmed said the government felt “no worry or pressure” and that law enforcement agencies remained on constant alert. He warned that the Awami League would face strict legal action if it resorted to militancy, arson or attacks on people and property.
