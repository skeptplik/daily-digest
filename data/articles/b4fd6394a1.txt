Thu, 3 Sept, 8:10 pm, Final score, Dockers 40, Hawks 72
The AFL Draft occupies a unique space for footy fans: one of hope and ambition.
It's when the stars of tomorrow take their first steps in the professional footballing landscape, even if exactly who they are is unclear.
All 18 clubs have stocked up for the future, with each looking at how their future can be set up (and be Tasmania Devils-proofed) for the next decade.
The surest bet of this year's crop is second-generation Port Adelaide player Dougie Cochrane.
The youngster joins his brother at the club he loves most. Despite playing no junior football this year, Cochrane's potential is almost limitless: tall, agile, smart and skilful.
To some draft watchers, his potential is reminiscent of names like Adam Goodes, Matthew Pavlich or Anthony Koutoufides.
But Cochrane is far from the only prize from this year's crop.
This year's draft is considered to be particularly deep, with names such as Cody Walker, Ethan Drever, Harry Van Hattum, Arki Butler and Heath Mellody set to dominate footballing discourse over coming years.
The home state of football
Ask a football fan where the home of football is, and you'll get a different answer based on your location.
The passion for the game spreads throughout the nation, with several places holding strong claims to being the spiritual home of the sport.
While the draft attracted nominees from right across this country, it was one state in particular that continued to be tapped as the source of the league's future talent.
Victorians again dominated the draft with 13 of the top 20 picks and 58 per cent of all new draftees.
While not quite as dominant as Victoria's 2024 draft showing, it represents a shift back from some stronger years for other states in recent years, such as Queensland's big haul last year.
The strong draft year for Victoria comes off the back of yet another Under 18 National Championships victory for Vic Metro.
Despite this, the first two Victorian players chosen, Walker and Drever, come from the Vic Country zone.
It's also worth noting the top 10 was spread much further, with five states represented and only four Victorians among their ranks.
An absence in the middle
The draft is often topped by those who dominate traffic in the middle.
But just seven midfielders were taken in the first 20 picks, the equal lowest since 2023.
Across the whole draft, only 25 midfielders were tabbed.
Draft watchers have a couple of different theories on why this might be the case.
The simplest one is that each crop has a different shape, with some stronger in some parts and weaker in others.
Another theory is that because this year's draft was considered to be particularly deep and even, clubs focused on drafting for need more than pure talent.
One further theory posits that clubs are looking to target players who can perform in a defined role rather than those who need to be moulded.
Success stories such as Nick Watson have provided a pathway for young talents looking to maximise their chances of making a quick impact at AFL level.
There's only a small number of dedicated midfield roles in any best 23, so drafting for difference-makers elsewhere may have come back into vogue.
This year's average draftee also stood about 2 centimetres taller than their counterpart last year, an indication of where the strength of this year's crop lay.
North Melbourne had the tallest group of draftees at 194cm on average, while the Bulldogs and Cats had the shortest at around 182cm.
First Nations representation
Earlier this year, the AFL acknowledged the declining numbers of First Nations players in the AFL, saying there was plenty of work ahead to address the fall.
In recent seasons the number of First Nations players at the top level has dropped almost 30 per cent from 87 in 2019 to 62 this year.
But there are signs that the short-term prospects might be shifting even before new AFL-led measures take full effect.
This year's draft saw a number of records achieved for First Nations players.
For the first time two of the first three selections involved First Nations players (Cochrane and Walker).
Five of the first 14 picks involved Indigenous players.
By the end of the National Draft seven First Nations players were selected by clubs.
If each club uses their new dedicated list spot, this would mean 25 additional First Nations players will be on AFL lists this year compared with last season.
But, as Dillon noted in May, recruitment was not the only issue.
"It's not only just about identifying [talent]," Dillon said.
"It's then that the players are on a list. How can we make sure that the Indigenous player stays on the list as long as any other player?"
Time will tell if the tide has really turned.
Bidding wars
The talk of night one of the draft was the selection of Brisbane Academy player Caylen Murray by Sydney, with the Swans pouncing on a series of moves by the Lions that left him ripe to be poached.
The Lions have been aggressive in maximising the value of their picks under the various iterations of the draft points bidding systems.
But Sydney has perhaps had more experience testing other clubs' willingness to match.
Later in the draft, Geelong embarked on the longest streak of bids yet seen, racking up five in quick succession, eventually hitting on Adelaide's unwillingness to match for Anthony Long.
The Cats forced several clubs to draft players they may have been hoping to slide onto the rookie list.
Murray is the first top-10 bid to go unmatched, but there has been an uptick in clubs letting tied players go in recent years.
A third of the successful (unmatched) bids since 2015 came in the last two drafts. While many of these draftees were fringe prospects, the higher points value has now forced clubs to prioritise more strictly.
Last year, Sydney's Lachlan Carmichael (pick 21) and Brisbane's Tyan Prindable (pick 32) both went unmatched due to a focus on another prospect.
Previously, Sydney's refusal to match the Bulldogs' bid for Josh Dunkley in 2015 was famous as a rare occurrence.
The bidding wars have also created opportunities for arbitrage against other clubs' very short-term needs. Many trades saw future draft picks swapped for small changes of draft position.
Of particular note is that Brisbane (2027), Port Adelaide (2027) and Sydney (2028) added to an extensive slew of extra first- and second-round picks in a specific upcoming draft.
With the next two drafts set to be dominated by the Tasmania Devils, this year's draft might be the last chance some clubs have to tap into the best young talent pool for a while.
