Thursday’s (November 19, 2026) summit meeting between the leaders of the U.S., China and Russia will be “of groundbreaking significance”, Beijing said on Monday (November 16, 2026), hailing the symbolism of a meeting of the three leaders taking place in China.
China’s Foreign Ministry said President Xi Jinping will host the summit with President Donald Trump and President Vladimir Putin in Shenzhen on November 19, on the sidelines of the APEC summit.
“The upcoming China-Russia-U.S. summit in Shenzhen will mark the first-ever face-to-face meeting among the heads of state of the three countries. The three leaders will exchange views in depth on safeguarding world peace and promoting strategic stability in major-country relations, and the meeting will be of groundbreaking significance,” Foreign Ministry spokesperson Mao Ning said, adding that “strengthening communication, managing differences and stabilising relations among China, Russia and the U.S. serve the interests of all three countries”.
The meeting is likely to be more symbolic than substantial. U.S. officials have indicated that the agenda is the war in Ukraine, the situation in West Asia, including stabilising global energy supplies, and nuclear arms control. Whether there will be any concrete outcomes on any of those issues remains to be seen.
That has not, however, dampened enthusiasm in China, where state media has hailed the significance of the meeting. Beijing officially eschews references to a “G2” with the U.S. or a “G3” with the two countries and Russia, maintaining that it supports multipolarity. At the same time, the symbolism of China hosting the meeting has been seized upon to highlight Beijing’s global standing.
“The fact that China, Russia and the U.S. will sit down for face-to-face talks once again demonstrates that the head-of-state diplomacy plays an irreplaceable role in providing strategic guidance,” an editorial in the Global Times said. “The effort to foster a new type of major-country relationship marks a new phase in China’s major-country diplomacy with Chinese characteristics, featured by proactive leadership and systematic construction. The meeting to be held in Shenzhen is a manifestation of the international community’s high recognition of China’s image as a stable and responsible major country.”
Taking aim at naysayers, the editorial added that “those who view the trilateral meeting between China, Russia and the U.S. as major countries ‘carving out new spheres of influence’ or establishing a ‘new Yalta system’ clearly hold only a one-sided view of history, ignoring historical patterns.”
“They focus solely on the needs of individual parties while disregarding the overall and comprehensive interests, emphasise only the power aspect of major countries while neglecting their responsibilities, and are so preoccupied with the reality of polarisation in the international landscape that they lose sight of the ideal of democratising international relations,” it said.
Symbolism aside, few experts in China expect substantial outcomes amid what they acknowledge is growing strategic rivalry between China and the U.S. on the one hand, and an enduring rift between Russia and the West on the other, especially with the continuing war in Ukraine. Russia was “most enthusiastic” among the three for the summit, Su Liuqiang, a scholar at the Centre for American Studies at the Shanghai Institutes for International Studies, said in an interview with state media last week, adding that an informal exchange was likelier than a more structured, formal trilateral summit.
“On the one hand, there is substantial political resistance in the U.S.,” he said. “Anti-Russia sentiment remains strong in both the U.S. and among its European allies, with opposition to negotiating with Moscow while bypassing Ukraine and Europe. On the other hand, the three countries have differing priorities; Russia’s core concern is negotiations on a ceasefire in Ukraine, the U.S. is interested in the Russia-Ukraine conflict and non-proliferation, China supports peace talks but must also balance its relations with the U.S., Russia, Europe and Ukraine. In this context, a low-profile trilateral meeting is the more realistic option,” he added, noting that practical outcomes could emphasise stabilising global energy supplies.
While China preferred “multilateralism” over ideas such as the G2 or a U.S.-China-Russia triumvirate, Mr. Su said a meeting would still be significant in suggesting that while structural competition between big powers would still continue to define global geopolitics, there was increasing interest in “managing that competition and preventing it from escalating or spinning out of control.”
