PRESS REVIEW – Thursday, November 26: French media outlets Mediapart and Le Monde publish an additional trove of messages purporting to show far-right leader Jordan Bardella's rampant anti-Semitic thoughts as a young member of the party in 2013. Also: France holds the first trial of a female jihadist, Émilie König. Kilmar Abrego Garcia, once the face of Donald Trump's brutal mass deportations, arrives in Spain. Plus: we find out why the rich world is in the midst of a "sex recession."
French investigative website Mediapart and Le Monde newspaper have released a new trove of documents allegedly showing rampant anti-Semitism in messages posted by far-right National Rally leader Jordan Bardella. For the past few months, Bardella has angrily denied posting anti-Semitic messages on his Facebook page in 2013. Mediapart and French newspaper Le Monde have now published the content of dozens of new messages from 2013 in which "the far-right leader shows his hatred of Jews and his allegiance to far-right ideologist Alain Soral." The messages are damning. Over long paragraphs, Bardella deployed "thoughts as anti-Semitic as they were structured, filled with foul references", it is reported. He allegedly wrote: "The Zionist-Jewish community has a hold on France" and "Banks are controlled by Jewish people." Le Monde offers us 19 elements that "confirm" Bardella was the author of the posts. The most irrefutable proof, it says, is the Messenger account holder to whom Bardella was writing. Le Monde was able to extract the conversations of this account and the account holder, under the pseudonym Sacha, gave his ID card and Facebook logins as proof.
Here in France, the very first trial of a female jihadist begins this Thursday. Émilie König will appear in a French court for this special trial. The 41-year-old was one of the most active French propaganda makers for the Islamic State group. She was also the first female jihadist on France's "Fiche S" terrorist list. König was one of the first women to join the ranks of the group in Syria and one of the last to leave – in 2022 - after the Islamic State group's defeat, Le Figaro reminds us. She married four members of the group and had three of her children in Syria between 2015-2017, La Croix notes. Le Parisien says she was called "the muse" of the Islamic State group. The trial, which will last four days, will look at her path to Syria, as well as videos of beheadings that she reposted. The trial is also expected to trace her difficult childhood, sexual abuse and her path to radical Islam.
French justice has experienced a shift in the way female jihadists are prosecuted. La Croix notes that from 2012-2015, the general view was that women who joined the Islamic State group were victims; manipulated and coerced into joining the terrorist organisation. The turning point was in September 2016, when a commando of women tried and failed to explode gas cannisters outside Notre-Dame Cathedral. That attempt showed a willingness of female jihadists to participate in terrorist activity. From 2016, more women have faced court for their crimes, with harsher punishments.
Next, Salvadoran national Kilmar Abrego Garcia was illegally deported to El Salvador and became a symbol of US President Donald Trump's mass deportations. He has now arrived in Spain as a free man. Abrego Garcia and his wife, a US citizen, arrived in Madrid by commercial flight after voluntarily leaving the US. As El Pais notes, a year ago, Abrego Garcia was "bound and handcuffed" when he arrived in El Salvador. On Wednesday, he arrived in Madrid with his head held high, telling reporters, "Donald Trump does not decide my future." Abrego Garcia lived for years in Maryland with his wife and children, working as a sheet metal apprentice. He had come to the US from El Salvador as a teenager, fleeing threats from a gang there. A 2019 immigration order prevented US officials from deporting him to El Salvador because of that threat – but the Trump administration arrested him and deported him there anyway last year. A columnist from the conservative US magazine National Review writes that his case is a "cautionary tale in how benighted execution of a popular enforcement policy can turn a political asset into a liability."
Finally, the world is in a sex recession, according to The Economist. The magazine recounts that when a US aircraft carrier docked in Thailand recently, sex workers gathered in anticipation of some debaucherous activity with the 5,000 sailors and marines arriving. Instead, many went sightseeing or shopping for souvenirs. When they did go out, they spent more time looking at their phones than the pole dancers. It turns out that this behaviour is symptomatic of the rich world's sex recession. It's not just in the UK or France – in 2024, researchers found low levels of "hanky-panky" among Czech people, who on average were having sex less than once a month. Myriad reasons can explain this: smartphones, pornography and the argument that drinking less has also contributed to less spontaneous sex lives. In Japan, demanding work and family lives contribute to less sex. The most common reason married Japanese women give for not having sex? "Too much bother."
