Swedish retailer IKEA has unveiled its prediction for 2027’s Color of the Year – a dark, reddish-purple shade called Wild Fig.
Although revealed by IKEA US as part of its 2027 Style Guide, keen eyes would’ve already seen the shade pop up earlier this year in the sell-out floor lamp created by Dutch designer Lex Pott for IKEA’s PS Collection.
The company describes Wild Fig as “a deep, botanical shade with a rich purple tone”, although in certain lights, it looks closer to an earthy oxblood red.
“Full of character and the unexpected, it brings depth and a daring mindset to everyday spaces,” said Abbey Stark, home furnishing direction leader at IKEA US.
Used across furnishings and accessories, it can provide a subtle touch of colour without being overly aggressive, especially paired with warm woods and neutral shades, IKEA US suggests.
But the company equally pictures it being splashed across entire walls and argues it “unexpectedly fits in” with even brighter colours like baby pink and electric blue – IKEA US’s previous colours of the year.
The company’s Style Guide also pulled out a number of other interior design trends to look out for in 2027, including pattern clashing and Japandi minimalism.
Interior designers have already started making liberal use of Wild Fig, as seen in Note Design Studio’s scheme for the Lammhults showroom in Stockholm, where it blankets walls, floors and ceilings in the entrance, as well as an entire kitchen.
Other predictions for 2027’s colour of the year are set to start rolling in any minute, with Pantone’s set to be unveiled at the start of December.
Paint brand Dulux got ahead of the game when it named a muted green called Soulful Sage as its contender in September.
Some interiors industry insiders, including Dezeen columnist Michelle Ogundehin, have argued at least since the end of the pandemic that “it’s time to reconsider the whole colour of the year carnival”, which was originally invented as a marketing stunt for Pantone’s colour-matching system, much like the Michelin Guide was invented to sell more tyres.
