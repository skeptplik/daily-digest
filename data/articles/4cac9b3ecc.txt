Only three of the incidents tallied by Europe's main tech industry group have been linked to a culprit, and that ambiguity may be the attackers' strongest weapon.
On 2 and 3 November, supermarket freezers across Europe started switching themselves into defrost mode. In some stores, attackers set the temperature as high as 50°C.
Shops in Germany, Italy, the Netherlands, Belgium, Romania and Sweden threw away spoiled meat and frozen food, while several blood banks in France were also hit. The UK reported attacks too.
EU food and agriculture commissioner Christophe Hansen called it a "deliberate attempt to disrupt our food supply chain". Nearly three weeks later, nobody has claimed responsibility and the motive remains unclear.
One month, 16 incidents
The fridge attacks were one of 16 incidents mapped by DigitalEurope, the trade association for Europe's digital technology industry, based in Brussels, Belgium, between 18 October and 18 November.
Eleven were cyberattacks. Hackers took an energy company's IT systems in Norway, closed a district hospital's emergency room in Germany and stole data from a tunnel builder working across Denmark and Germany.
At an unnamed EU institution, HR, payroll and medical systems were knocked offline. The personal data of 18 senior officials from Italy, Germany and the Netherlands was published, and espionage malware was found in Romanian government email.
About €100 million was taken from a French bank through one of its suppliers, more than 700 fintech customer accounts were exposed in Italy, and a Danish shipping group's legacy system was raided for data.
The other five incidents were physical, including two sabotage plots. About 80 aircraft lost GPS over the North Sea, drones forced Moldova to close its airspace, and hoax bomb threats hit more than 35 flights across Europe.
"Resilience is no longer just an IT department job," said Cecilia Bonefeld-Dahl, DigitalEurope's director general. "It is security policy and industrial policy."
The group says its list is not exhaustive. Ransomware gangs alone list dozens of European victims every month.
The password is 12345
The November wave was not the first. In mid-October, hackers took over refrigeration units in at least 350 supermarkets in France, including Carrefour, Monoprix, Intermarché and Coopérative U stores, and manually overrode their temperatures.
France's cybersecurity agency said the units ran software from Danish firm Danfoss, as well as Copeland and Dixell systems. Danfoss said many had reached the end of their life, no longer received security updates or ran outdated firmware.
Supermarket refrigeration is often connected to the internet so contractors can monitor and repair it remotely. Security firm Forescout found nearly 13,000 refrigeration control services openly reachable online in 99 countries.
In November, attackers exploited weak security and known flaws in Danfoss's AK-SM system manager. Many systems still used factory-default logins such as "Supervisor" and "12345".
George Nott, technology editor at trade magazine The Grocer, said the attackers found exposed login pages and used vulnerabilities that had already been patched. In Sweden, the Coop chain said the equipment in its seven affected shops was connected over old 4G mobile networks.
Xavier Bellekens, chief executive of cybersecurity firm Lupovis, said routing the controllers through VPNs would largely have removed the risk.
Who did it?
Of DigitalEurope's 16 incidents, seven have no named culprit. Attackers claimed four themselves, investigators suspect an actor in two without formally naming it, and only three have been publicly attributed by a government or researchers.
"That is what makes hybrid attacks work: cheap, deniable and hard to pin on anyone," the group said.
The fridge attacks show how hard the question is. Lupovis said its decoy systems recorded more than 276 attempts by 91 threat actors to probe Danfoss controllers over 10 days, peaking on 26 October and stopping at the end of the month.
The activity was traced to "bulletproof hosting" services, built to resist takedowns and law enforcement, some of which Russian groups have used before. Bellekens said that did not prove Russia was responsible, but there was "a bit of a pattern". The coordination within about 24 hours pointed to a well-planned operation, possibly by a state, he added.
Others are less sure. Dan Cimpean, director of Romania's National Cyber Security Directorate, called the attacks "semi-amateurish" and said he did not suspect a state actor. After the French attack, Ken Munro of British firm Pen Test Partners said it could be "kids messing around".
No ransom was demanded, which investigators found puzzling: criminal and hacktivist groups usually move quickly to profit from or publicise an attack. Agencies cannot even agree on what to call it. Belgium, Italy and Estonia labelled it a cyberattack, Sweden and Ireland a "cyber incident", while the EU's cybersecurity agency ENISA stopped short of calling it an attack.
Same targets, different weapons
DigitalEurope's tally suggests the line between online and offline attacks is blurring. In the same month, one military drone maker in Germany was hit through a hijacked software update, while an arson attack on another in Estonia was foiled.
The group also counted a plot to start fires next to a railway and a power line in Germany.
"Digital and physical attacks hit the same targets," it said. Its map shows the incidents stretching from the North Sea to Romania and Moldova.
Find, fund, buy
Bonefeld-Dahl wants Europe to go further. "Europe needs the technology to detect, deter and recover – and it must FIND. FUND. BUY," she wrote on LinkedIn.
DigitalEurope is calling on EU leaders to spend at least 25% of the next EU budget, worth almost €2 trillion, on technologies to deter, defend and recover. It says only 14% currently goes to digital.
"There is no more time to waste," Bonefeld-Dahl wrote, in a message addressed to the bloc's prime ministers.
The 14% figure is the digital share of the EU's 2021–2025 spending, including its post-pandemic recovery fund, rather than of the budget now being negotiated. And as an industry lobby, DigitalEurope speaks for many of the companies that would sell Europe that technology.
In Brussels, the response is taking shape in the revision of the EU's Cybersecurity Act, led in the European Parliament by Czech MEP Markéta Gregorová.
What next?
Experts warn that fridges are only the start. Nott said heating, ventilation and air conditioning systems, electronic shelf labels and even floor-cleaning robots could have similar weaknesses.
Kumar Sokka, chief executive of Acre Security, said the same type of controller can run doors, access control, cameras, alarms and building climate systems. Organisations often leave them to facilities teams rather than treating them as part of their obligations under NIS2, the EU's cybersecurity law, he said.
Artificial intelligence could make the problem worse. Gianni Cuozzo, founder and chief executive of Exein, said AI was shortening the time between finding a vulnerability and building an attack, so connected devices needed to defend themselves as they run.
Bonefeld-Dahl will discuss Europe's response in Vilnius, Lithuania, next week. She ended her post with a question: "What's the next critical target?"
