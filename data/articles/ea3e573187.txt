We’ll be treated to a Supermoon tonight - and it’s set to be big and bright.
The night of 23rd to 24th November will see the Beaver Supermoon light up the sky.
November’s full Moon is traditionally known as the 'Beaver Moon', a nickname linked to the time of year when beavers prepare for winter by building dams and gathering food.
What is a Supermoon?
The November 2026 full Moon is a Supermoon, which means it occurs when the Moon is at or near its closest point to Earth. That is possible because its orbit isn't a perfect circle. Instead, it's elliptical, or slightly egg-shaped.
That means the distance between the two bodies varies. When the Moon is at its closest point to Earth, it's at perigee. When the Moon is furthest from Earth, it's at apogee.
The technical name for a Supermoon is a perigee full Moon, and it can appear up to 14 per cent larger and 30 per cent brighter than a full Moon at apogee.
How to watch a Supermoon - and the best time to see it
A Supermoon can be seen in clear night skies without any special equipment.
Try to find a wide, open space, preferably one that's elevated, such as up a hill.
It's such an exciting spectacle because, whether a Supermoon or not, the Moon appears bigger when it's close to the horizon because of what's called the 'Moon illusion'. This is a strange visual trick that experts can't yet fully explain. "When you have a supermoon, that effect is just slightly more striking,” said astronomer William Alston with the University of Hertfordshire in the United Kingdom.
The best time to view a Supermoon
Experts say the best times to view a Supermoon are at moonrise and moonset, when the Moon is close to the horizon.
For most of western Europe, moonrise will be between 4 and 5pm today.
You can check exact moonrise times for where you live here.
If you miss moonrise, don't worry. The Supermoon will still be impressive throughout the night and into tomorrow as well (25th November).
When is the next Supermoon?
An anomalistic month (from one perigee to the next) is shorter than a synodic month (from one New Moon to the next) - but only by around two days. This means that if a full Moon falls close to perigee in one month, it is likely to do so again the following month.
The consequence of this is that, when Supermoons happen, they happen in groups - we get four or five Super Full Moons in a row (or four or five Super New Moons in a row).
Tonight’s will be the second to last Supermoon of the year. The last Supermoon of 2026 could be particularly magical, as it will rise on 24th December, celebrated as Christmas Eve in many European countries.
The next two Supermoons after that will be on 22nd January 2027 and 20th February 2027.
How do Supermoons affect tides?
The tides are caused by the gravitational forces of the Sun and the Moon on the Earth’s oceans, explains Royal Museums Greenwich. When the Moon is closer to the Earth during a Supermoon, the gravitational pull is slightly stronger, and so the tides are bigger. However this effect is almost negligible, with only a few centimetres difference between a normal full Moon and Supermoon tide.
However, full Moon and New Moon tides can be much bigger than those at other times in the lunar month, as the Sun adds its own gravitational pull into the mix, producing the so-called spring tides.
The Beaver Supermoon is set to have potentially damaging effects along the west coast of the US, for example. High tides along the Pacific Coast could break 40-year-old records and flood low-lying coastal communities, complicating travel during Thanksgiving week.
In the San Francisco Bay Area, tides could reach almost a metre above normal on Tuesday before peaking on Wednesday, says Dial Hoang, a National Weather Service forecaster.
