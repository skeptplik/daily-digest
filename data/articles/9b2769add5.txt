Some psychiatric patients are refusing treatment over the NHS adding intimate details from therapy sessions to their medical notes, fuelling fears that breaching privacy is posing a risk to health, the Guardian can reveal.
One stopped medical care for a heart condition when he discovered that details of intrusive sexual thoughts he’d experienced as result of obsessive compulsive disorder (OCD) had been shared with a cardiac nurse.
A woman said she would never trust the NHS again for mental health support after full transcripts of therapy sessions about the trauma she had experienced when her husband was taken hostage were uploaded to her patient record.
Another patient said she would shun future NHS treatment for panic attacks after details of the child abuse she had experienced were uploaded to her record.
The patients came forward after Rosanna* told the Guardian she had been horrified to discover sensitive discussions with her psychotherapist had been uploaded to the London Care Record (LCR). As a result she had stopped treatment at South London and Maudsley NHS trust.
This week Rosanna’s ordeal was highlighted during a Lords debate about the new single patient record in the health bill. Tim Clement-Jones, the Liberal Democrats’ spokesperson on the digital economy, said the case “should be an urgent wake-up call” and expressed concern that the architects of the London system were involved in drawing up the record.
He said: “If we replicate that model nationally without statutory guardrails, we will force millions of patients into an intolerable choice: their privacy or their health.”
Sheila Hollins, an emeritus professor of psychiatry, proposed an amendment to prevent routine disclosure of therapy notes to protect the privacy of patients like Rosanna and others.
Caleb* said he had been “devastated” to discover that a psychiatric assessment about his OCD at Cornwall partnership NHS trust had been sent to a cardiac nurse who was due to treat him for angina. The letter shared what Caleb had told a psychiatrist about an unwanted intrusive thought about touching women inappropriately.
The psychiatrist later apologised to Caleb for misunderstanding what had been said and clarified that his assessment letter was describing a symptom of Caleb’s OCD and that he posed no risk to others.
Caleb said it was a breach of his privacy and irrelevant to his medical condition. He said: “I couldn’t face going to see the cardiac nurse because she was going to think I’m some kind of raging pervert, which I’m not. I discharged myself because of embarrassment.
“I thought there was presumption of confidentiality, but when I did talk about things they got shared with no medical justification. That loss of confidentiality can have a devastating effect on patient’s willingness to engage.”
A complaint he made to the information commissioner was dismissed. A spokesperson for the trust said it could not comment on individual cases but added: “Relevant information may sometimes need to be shared between healthcare professionals to support safe and coordinated care. We take concerns about confidentiality seriously.”
Patricia* from London was treated for post-traumatic stress disorder while she was pregnant after her husband had been kidnapped. She later found that very specific details of the kidnapping and threats to her husband, and her subsequent despair and anxiety, were set out in a three-page letter to her GP.
Patricia said: “I would never consult with the NHS again for mental health services. I do not understand why a transcript would be shared to my GP, especially as the letter concludes that I did not pose a risk to my unborn child.”
In 2024, Nadia*, from Cheshire, ended 12 sessions of cognitive behavioural therapy for the panic attacks she experienced due to post-traumatic stress disorder. She said she had been “freaked out” after discovering that detailed notes of every session were on her GP records.
She said: “Extremely explicit detail of childhood abuse was included – it was effectively a transcript of every session. There’s no need for my GP to have access to that information.
She added: “I definitely won’t be using the NHS mental health treatment again. If I had known this was going to happen I would never have consented to treatment. Part of the trauma was not being able to talk about it for fear of people finding out.
“I’m mortified. I want my record to be sanitised. I used to work for the NHS and I know that anybody with an NHS access card can potentially access those records.”
Minh Alexander, a retired consultant psychiatrist, said: “It clearly cannot be right for there to be blanket sharing of all aspects of patients’ sensitive medical history across the NHS, especially when it is clear that the NHS cannot guarantee records’ security. Instead, to maintain trust and psychological safety, the level of information sharing should reflect relevance and proportionality.”
Sam Smith, from the health data experts MedConfidential, said if patients wanted care, they may have to accept that tens of thousand of NHS staff could look up their notes.
He said: “While some patients will accept care on that basis, you can imagine many would not, or would limit what they disclosed, which is fundamentally harmful to patients.”
NHS England has been approached for comment.
* Names have been changed
