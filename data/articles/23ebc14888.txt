A doctor who gave expert evidence that helped to convict Lucy Letby, and who was barred during the trial from treating patients, was then instructed as an expert witness in another prosecution.
The Guardian revealed this week that Prof Peter Hindmarsh was excluded from clinical practice in March 2023, during the main trial of Letby, by his employer, University College London hospitals trust (UCLH). The jury, who five months later convicted Letby of the murder of seven babies, were never told.
Among the reasons for UCLH’s decision was that Hindmarsh’s advice to child patients relating to insulin was not considered safe. The restriction on Hindmarsh’s working practices has cast further doubt on the prosecution’s case against the nurse, who is serving 15 whole-life sentences.
Hindmarsh went on to produce a crucial expert report in August 2023 that the Crown Prosecution Service (CPS) used to support a case against two Birmingham parents, Damion and Tamara Thomas, who were accused of neglecting their son Joshua, 12, before he died.
Doctors had found that Joshua had suffered from his first episode of diabetes, which became critical and was a cause of his death on 10 December 2022. Hindmarsh, instructed as an expert in insulin and diabetes, concluded that the symptoms would have been clear and medical help should have been called for earlier.
In March this year, Tamara Thomas was convicted of gross negligence manslaughter and neglect for the delay in seeking medical assistance for Joshua, and she was sentenced in June to three years and nine months in prison. Damion Thomas was found not guilty of manslaughter but guilty of neglect and sentenced to two years and nine months.
The West Midlands police lead investigator in the case, Vicki Harris, referred to the importance of Hindmarsh’s opinion after the convictions, saying: “Both parents were aware of the signs and symptoms of diabetes and expert medical evidence showed their son’s death would have been preventable if medical attention had been sought earlier.”
The Thomases have always maintained their innocence and have applied to the court of appeal for permission to appeal against the convictions. Their elder son, Alessandro, 27, who is representing them, says that throughout the two and a half years of Hindmarsh’s involvement in the case, they were unaware that he had been under investigation by the General Medical Council (GMC) and that he was barred from treating patients due to safety concerns.
At the time Hindmarsh wrote the report, UCLH had excluded him from treating child patients for diabetes because it had concluded he was unsafe to do so.
As the Guardian reported this week, a UCLH medical director, Dr Tim Hodgson, informed the GMC in March 2023 that the hospital had excluded Hindmarsh from clinical practice because it could not “accept the level of risk Professor Hindmarsh appears to pose to children and young people”.
Hindmarsh appears not to have told West Midlands police of the investigation into him when, five months later, he wrote his report as an expert in the case relating to the Thomases. The CPS West Midlands region office apparently remained unaware of the GMC investigation and serious concerns about Hindmarsh’s practice until Hindmarsh disclosed details after being asked, in December 2025.
Hindmarsh declined to answer any of the Guardian’s questions about the GMC investigation, his exclusion from clinical practice or when he disclosed it to the police.
The Thomases and their seven children were living in Kings Heath, Birmingham, having moved from London in 2017 so they could afford to buy a house with a garden, Alessandro Thomas said.
The parents were teachers, although Tamara stayed at home to look after the younger children and was home schooling them, including Joshua, at the time. Joshua was a member of the Sparkhill Harriers running club where, the court heard, he was considered “confident and articulate, bright and fairly competitive”.
He had been unwell since mid-November 2022 with a cough and respiratory problems. He then became critically ill on 9 December. The criminal charges of neglect concentrated on a period of eight and a half hours that day.
Joshua had been up in the night, at 4am, and was ushered back to bed. In the morning, he was weak and unwell, then Alessandro became alarmed that Joshua’s breathing was low and alerted Tamara. She noticed Joshua’s lips were white and called an ambulance at 12.33pm. While Tamara was on the phone to the emergency call handlers, Joshua stopped breathing and they advised her how to perform CPR on him, which she did.
Joshua was taken to Birmingham children’s hospital where he died the following day. The postmortem examinations concluded he had severe diabetic ketoacidosis (DKA), a critical condition caused by a severe lack of insulin.
The police and CPS argued that Joshua’s parents should have known his symptoms were those of diabetes, particularly as Damion had it, and should have called for medical assistance earlier.
In Hindmarsh’s report written in August 2023, he focused on Joshua’s symptoms and signs of illness at 4am on 9 December 2022, saying they were sufficient and of a severity that “a medical assessment should have been sought”. Hindmarsh concluded “it is not plausible” there were no symptoms of diabetes and signs of DKA.
He did not firmly say that the parents should have sought emergency help from 4am, but wrote in his report: “The symptoms described were worrying and should have prompted an earlier call for help and medical review.”
Excluded from clinical practice
In his report, which was used throughout the two and a half years of the West Midlands police investigation, and in the trial this year, Hindmarsh wrote of his career biography: “I am Professor Peter Christopher Hindmarsh BSc MD FRCP FRCPH. I am currently employed by University College London Hospitals as a consultant in paediatric endocrinology and emeritus professor of paediatric endocrinology at University College London. All appointments date from 1991. My training in paediatric endocrinology commenced in 1983 at the Middlesex and Great Ormond Street hospitals. My research interests centre on diabetes care and my clinical practice includes the management of children with diabetes.”
At that time, UCLH was not permitting him to work as a consultant and had excluded him from clinical practice. The jury in the trial was never told that, nor that UCLH had lost confidence in his professional judgment relating to insulin and diabetes.
Expert witnesses have a duty under legal rules to disclose to the party instructing them anything “that might reasonably be thought capable of undermining the expert’s opinion or detract from the credibility or impartiality of the expert”, including any disciplinary proceedings by a regulatory body.
Tim Green KC, a specialist in disclosure, said that under the criminal procedure rules, “the duty on experts to disclose undermining material is to make such disclosure as soon as reasonably practicable”.
Hindmarsh’s involvement in the Thomas case raises questions over whether he properly complied with these disclosure rules, as he does not appear to have informed the police of his circumstances for more than two years after writing his expert report.
The Guardian understands that in November 2025, the CPS independently discovered that Hindmarsh was subject to GMC restrictions. The CPS then asked West Midlands police to raise this with Hindmarsh, and at that point he disclosed that he had been barred from clinical practice and been subject to GMC investigation.
The CPS nevertheless decided it could continue to rely on Hindmarsh as its expert in the prosecution of Damion and Tamara Thomas.
That Hindmarsh was presented as a distinguished expert and was allowed to give critical evidence on a child’s onset of diabetes, while his hospital considered him unsafe to treat children with diabetes, raises questions over the use of expert witnesses in courts.
The Guardian this year revealed the GMC investigation into Hindmarsh, which began on 25 November 2022. UCLH and Great Ormond Street hospital had both reported to the medical regulator “multiple and wide-ranging concerns” about his practice, including his “diagnosis and treatment of patients” and “cases where harm to patients has been alleged”. Hindmarsh had been an honorary consultant at Great Ormond Street, until his contract was terminated in July 2022.
In January 2023, a medical practitioners tribunal – an independent panel that determines GMC regulatory proceedings – ordered that Hindmarsh must be supervised in his clinical work to protect the public.
Then, in March 2023, Hodgson, the UCLH medical director, reported further serious concerns about Hindmarsh to the GMC. He wrote that Hindmarsh had breached the supervision order and independently advised that two child patients stop taking insulin at home.
One of the children had an episode of DKA, Hodgson told the GMC, although he said: “It’s not clear whether this coincided with the alleged advice from Professor Hindmarsh.”
Hodgson wrote: “On the grounds of safety UCLH are not presently in a position to allow Professor [Hindmarsh] to practice clinically even under supervision … I do not believe UCLH can accept the level of risk Professor Hindmarsh appears to pose to children and young people even with close clinical supervision.”
The GMC then applied for Hindmarsh’s registration as a doctor to be suspended. Hindmarsh’s barrister argued it would be disproportionate to suspend his registration and it would impede his ability to work as an expert witness.
In April 2023, another medical tribunal ruled it was not possible to determine if Hindmarsh had “technically” breached his supervision conditions, and declined to suspend his registration. Instead it imposed a new, stricter order that he had to be directly supervised. This appears to have had no practical application because by then Hindmarsh was already restricted to working only for UCLH and the hospital had excluded him from treating patients at all.
The tribunal agreed it would be disproportionate to suspend him, explicitly noting that he could continue working as an expert witness in legal cases and that its order would “not restrict his non-clinical work”.
Hindmarsh was a crucial expert witness for the prosecution of Letby, a nurse in the neonatal unit of the Countess of Chester hospital, providing an opinion that two babies had been poisoned with insulin. The jury in her trial, which ran from October 2022 to August 2023, was never told of the GMC investigation, the hospitals’ concerns, that Hindmarsh was barred from treating patients or of the April 2023 tribunal ruling.
Letby was convicted of murdering seven babies and attempting to murder seven more, then refused permission to appeal. She has always maintained her innocence. Her lawyer Mark McDonald has applied to the miscarriages of justice watchdog, the Criminal Cases Review Commission, to refer her convictions back to the court of appeal.
Seven experts have contributed to reports McDonald has submitted to the CCRC, arguing that Hindmarsh’s analysis of the two babies’ medical course was wrong and they were not poisoned with insulin.
‘Voluntary erasure’
Ultimately, the GMC investigation into Hindmarsh never reached a final adjudication. He applied to remove himself from the register of doctors, a process known as “voluntary erasure”, which was granted in November 2024. That meant the investigation ended with no conclusion.
Having been made aware of the GMC investigation, the medical tribunal’s April 2023 ruling and that UCLH had barred him from treating patients, the CPS informed the Thomases’ lawyers on 9 December 2025, shortly before their trial was due to start. Hindmarsh provided an “expert witness self-certificate” in which he disclosed that he had been under GMC investigation.
Hindmarsh stated in the self-certificate that he had been referred to the GMC and mentioned two previous tribunal rulings, in December 2022 and January 2023. He did not mention the April 2023 tribunal ruling, the concerns UCLH had reported to the GMC or that the hospital had barred him from clinical practice, or that his contract at Great Ormond Street had been terminated.
“I retired from all clinical practice in April 2024 and following some adverse health matters I was granted voluntary erasure from the GMC medical register,” he wrote. “There were not any matters proven against me as this decision brought the investigation to a close.”
Of all the categories of disclosure required by the rules, which include an obligation to state any adverse findings or disciplinary proceedings by a professional body, “a history of lax or inadequate scientific methods” or “a history of failure to observe recognised standards in the expert’s area of expertise”, Hindmarsh certified that the only one that applied to him was number eight, “lack of an accreditation”, because he was no longer registered as a doctor.
Alessandro Thomas says he believes Hindmarsh was not fully transparent in that document or in his career biography in the expert report.
Even when the disclosure was finally made, however, he claims his parents’ own lawyers did not inform them. The lawyers decided not to apply for the jury to be informed, or to challenge Hindmarsh’s credibility, professional judgment or record. Part of their reasoning appears to have been they believed the judge was unlikely to allow it.
Alessandro said the first they learned of the GMC investigation was on 3 March, towards the end of his parents’ trial and after Hindmarsh had given evidence, when the Guardian reported it.
The lawyers for Damion and Tamara Thomas declined to answer the Guardian’s questions about this, citing client confidentiality.
Alessandro said: “Professor Hindmarsh’s report of August 2023 was relied on throughout all the proceedings against my parents, but he said nothing in it about any of the proceedings against himself.
“The career biography he set out, portraying himself as currently practising at UCLH, managing diabetes in children, was misleading. He was presented throughout the investigatory process and then in court as a fully trusted, eminent authority on diabetes.
“It turned out his medical judgment was not trusted by his own hospital, which decided he was not safe to advise young people on managing diabetes and insulin. But his report was crucial in supporting the accusation that my parents had been unsafe towards managing my brother’s first onset of diabetes before his tragic passing, and in sending them to prison. This does not feel like justice.”
A spokesperson for West Midlands police said: “This was a tragic case which resulted in the death of Joshua, and the guilty verdicts were the result of more than three years of work examining complex medical evidence. West Midlands police fulfilled its obligations around disclosure, making information available to the CPS in a timely manner as it became available to investigators. Our thoughts remain with Joshua’s family and all of those affected by his tragic death.”
A CPS spokesperson said: “The CPS met all its disclosure obligations and no objections were raised by the defence at any stage in relation to expert evidence relied on by the prosecution.”
The CPS could not say in how many prosecutions it had instructed Hindmarsh as an expert since he was under GMC investigation, because the prosecuting authority is organised by regions and does not hold such information centrally.
The Guardian asked Hindmarsh detailed questions about the hospitals’ concerns, UCLH excluding him from treating patients, and the GMC investigation, and put to him that he appeared not to have made adequate disclosure under the criminal procedure rules. A representative replied: “He has no comment in relation to these questions and points.”
