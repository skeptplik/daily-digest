As a measles outbreak spread through Lancaster county, Pennsylvania, in August, a parent turned to a massive public Facebook group, Crunchy Moms, for advice. They were considering getting their three-year-old vaccinated, in part to protect their six-month-old.
“I’m nervous for my baby,” the parent wrote.
“First of all your children cannot die of measles. It is literally impossible,” one member responded. “Do not do it,” advised another. Others said the vaccine would spread measles or that it would be like “injecting poison”.
At the end of the post, Facebook’s AI system – run by its parent company, Meta – summarized the 589 responses, writing in part that commenters “claim the vaccine is unnecessary or even harmful”.
None of those claims are true. Pennsylvania has reported six measles-associated deaths this year, including two babies in Lancaster county. The measles, mumps and rubella (MMR) vaccine is safe and effective. Health officials say it is the best defense against measles spreading and injuring or killing people.
Scientists, doctors and advocates for health and children’s safety told the Guardian that what they saw in Crunchy Moms and similar groups was alarming. They worried that children could be getting hurt and that mothers were getting pulled into an online community that rejected science and fact-based medicine. One scientist who spends her free time monitoring the platform to highlight harmful health misinformation calls Crunchy Moms “the most dangerous group on Facebook”.
Meta did not comment on that characterization.
The spread of misinformation on Facebook has been a problem for years, but in the past, the company took steps to curb it. During the Covid-19 pandemic, for example, Facebook recognized it had a problem and changed its policies to “prioritize connecting people with accurate health information”.
But last year, shortly before the inauguration of Donald Trump, Mark Zuckerberg, Facebook’s billionaire founder, said there was “too much censorship” on Meta’s platforms.
Meta changed course; it eliminated factcheckers in the US and dialed down active moderation of anything but the most severe or illegal content. The company still maintains a policy that prohibits harmful health misinformation, but it is not known how Meta enforces it, and how its new permissive approach affects users at a time when measles and other infectious diseases are making a comeback.
In response to questions from the Guardian, Meta claimed nothing had changed in the policy or how it was enforced since 2023.
A Guardian investigation has found that dangerous medical misinformation is flowing freely on Facebook in groups like Crunchy Moms – now more than 490,000 members strong. What’s more, the company appears to have helped spread such ideas by promoting Crunchy Moms into users’ feeds, even when they do not seek it out.
Zuckerberg previously suggested that waiting for users to report “bad stuff” – rather than having automated systems catch them – would strike a better balance between what he termed censorship and free expression. But the Guardian found that Zuckerberg’s suggested approach appears to have little to no discernible effect on false information that circulates on Crunchy Moms.
Facebook took no action on more than 50 posts and comments a Guardian journalist reported to the platform from her own Facebook account. The posts appeared to violate Meta’s own standards, including that vaccines caused autism, that measles is not deadly, or that “miracle cures” like the caustic agent black salve that destroys skin tissue can treat cancer. Facebook did not acknowledge or respond to a single one of the Guardian’s reports, made in May, July and September.
In a response, Meta said its misinformation policies allow people to discuss personal experiences and scientific research. It said it removed misinformation when it was likely to directly contribute to the risk of imminent physical harm. But Meta did not respond to detailed questions about Crunchy Moms or experts’ assessment that such misinformation is commonplace in the group.
Meta’s revenue last year exceeded $200bn.
Doctors, researchers, former employees and public health advocates said the Guardian’s findings show that the pendulum at Meta has swung back to a time when there was little moderation of false health information.
“We were documenting Facebook recommending anti-vaccine groups to parents almost a decade ago,” said Renée DiResta, a professor at Georgetown University who studies online manipulation.
She and others have shown how that approach harmed children. Back then, there were stories of children being hurt or dying after their parents turned to Facebook groups for health advice.
“I’m afraid that we’re going to see that same kind of dynamic all over again,” DiResta said.
A Facebook recommendation sparks a Guardian investigation
A Guardian journalist was scrolling on her phone in March when something caught her eye. Her Facebook feed recommended she join a group called Crunchy Moms: Natural Living & Clean Products.
The group was created in March 2025, less than three months after Zuckerberg announced Meta’s new hands-off approach.
Crunchy Moms’ administrators describe it as “a supportive space for moms who want to raise their families using toxin-free products, low-tox alternatives, and clean ingredient solutions”. But a quick check of the group revealed, and experts confirmed, it was a hotbed of false medical information that could hurt people – especially children and their mothers, who turn to the group for community, advice and affirmation.
The group focuses largely on health, though some posters also come to seek parenting tips, product recommendations and other non-medical questions. Many posters appear to take it as a given that group members are – or should be – against vaccinating their children. They frequently describe vaccines and their ingredients as deadly, toxic or poisonous.
“We don’t vaccinate in this group. It’s the foundation of becoming a crunchy mom,” one anonymous member explained last month, in response to a question about the vaccine for RSV, a respiratory virus. A group administrator responded to a different post that “As a crunchy community we are generally against v@cs” – a code for “vaccines”.
Crunchy Moms has more than doubled in size since January, and receives hundreds of posts a day, and thousands of comments and replies.
Sara Rupe launched the group in March 2025. It was less than three months after Zuckerberg announced Meta’s new hands-off approach. The Connecticut mom said in an interview that her intention in creating the group was to share product recommendations for things like personal care products and makeup, but it turned into something bigger.
At least part of Crunchy Moms’ success, she said, was because it does not block “free speech”.
“Everybody has a right to say what they want to say as long as it’s within community standards of Facebook,” Rupe said. “I don’t censor things.”
Rupe added: “If somebody wants to say something, then I usually let them. But I mean, within reason, if I really think it’s going to hurt somebody, I don’t. But I also think letting some information get out there could be good.”
Crunchy Moms is the largest group in a network of groups that collectively has more than 1 million members. Rupe runs them with the help of several administrators and moderators, including her husband, who is a registered nurse; a pastor and wife couple in upstate New York; and a woman whose Meta-verified account describes her as a doctor of holistic health.
They post often with links to supplements and other products for sale on an Amazon store, with links to another administrator’s skincare line, or with affiliate links to products that provide a cut of sales to the person sharing it.
Rupe said she and another administrator use multiple accounts to run the group, which lets them maintain access to it if Facebook restricts her account. Her daughter was in seventh grade last year when Rupe used her account to post a listing of more than three dozen products, including herbal remedy bundles that run over $100, non-fluoride toothpaste, $25 tallow balm and red light therapy panels that cost $200 or more.
A second administrator, Dawn Mannion, said she didn’t make any money from the group, though others made a small commission that was “not even minimum wage”. She said she frequently recommended that members seek medical attention from holistic or other qualified healthcare professionals. She added: “We do not discourage people from seeking medical care. We encourage it.”
Other administrators and moderators contacted by the Guardian did not respond to Facebook messages and texts seeking comment.
Many Crunchy Moms members were already into “natural living” and questioning vaccines when they come to the group, and group leaders don’t try to sway anyone’s mind, Rupe said.
Rupe has a child with autism that she now believes is connected to vaccination, and said many others in the group believed their children were injured by vaccines.
“We don’t want anybody to be hurt at all,” she said. “We want everybody to be as happy and healthy as possible.”
While Crunchy Moms posters often vehemently counsel against vaccines and modern medicine, Rupe said she did not think the group was harming anyone. Instead, she said it was helping people make their own decisions.
Some members say vaccines are so dangerous that it is safer to get a deadly or life-altering vaccine-preventable disease than it is to get immunized. They tell other members that catching a disease like measles or chicken pox “naturally” can be more healthy than getting vaccinated. Others repeat the US health secretary Robert F Kennedy Jr’s unproven claims that Tylenol is linked to autism. One recent comment said that catching measles makes your body stronger, trains your immune system and results in a lower risk of cardiovascular disease and cancer.
Many posts spread the false assertion that vaccines cause autism and sudden infant death syndrome (Sids) – even though Meta specifies in its own policies that posts saying so violate its terms.
New or soon-to-be mothers looking for help and advice are frequent posters, and targets for swarms of comments telling them not to follow science-backed health recommendations for their children, like giving their newborns a vitamin K shot that can prevent brain bleeds.
One woman asked about a link between vaccination and Sids, suggesting she feared she was putting her baby at risk of death if she followed the recommended vaccine schedule. She said she was considering spacing out her infant’s vaccines to lessen the risk. She ended her post: “I’m terrified.”
“Delaying poison won’t do anything beneficial. Injecting poison WILL increase the likelihood of SIDS,” one woman replied. Another, labeled an “All-Star contributor” by Facebook, weighed in: “SIDS is vaccine death. SIDS was invented to cover up the fact that vaccines kill infants.”
Scrolling through a post asking whether vaccines cause autism, readers will find comment after comment saying that they do. In one post, a frequent commenter offered a false statistic that 79% of autism cases were caused by vaccines. She replied to a person who said they were neurodiverse: “Your parents caused this with their vaccines. I am sorry.”
The same commenter later counseled the Lancaster county parent who was worried about their community’s measles outbreak that “it is not worth risking death or a disabling lifelong neurodegenerative condition, such as autism, for a vaccine that is ineffective, at best, at preventing measles”.
The group includes a mix of views. Some commenters will offer encouragement to seek medical care and to vaccinate their children. The Guardian spoke to people who became members of Crunchy Moms to try to provide a counterweight to the false information shared there.
Mannion said they did not endorse every statement made by individual members.
One of the Crunchy Moms rules that is supposed to govern the group is that the information shared is not medical advice, and that members should consult a healthcare provider before making any health or medical decisions. But in the many posts from parents asking for advice or sharing what their doctors told them, commenters often urge them not to follow what the doctor suggests, or say there is no need to speak to a doctor at all.
Rupe said she believes many of the comments are from “trolls” trying to get the group in trouble, and that people are simply posting their opinions.
Dr Alok Patel, a pediatrician who reviewed the group at the Guardian’s request, pointed to those kinds of comments as particularly concerning. It is a time-honored tradition for mothers to turn to fellow mothers for advice, and Patel believes it is important for people to use social media to find connection.
“But when people are outright dismissing modern medicine and putting their foot down and saying don’t get vaccines, don’t get the vitamin K shot, don’t see a doctor, and instead promoting a product – that is outright misinformation and potentially dangerous,” he said.
Patel has built up his own following on Meta’s Instagram platform to deliver science-based medical information to people. Social media companies, he said, should show it is more important to provide quality information than it is to keep people on their apps.
“Prove to us that you are prioritizing public health as much as you prioritize profits for your investors,” Patel said.
Research has shown that health misinformation shared on Facebook can have a real-world impact on people’s decisions.
Jennifer Allen, a professor at NYU Stern who worked at Facebook from 2016 to 2018, has researched the effect of misinformation and vaccine-skeptical content on Facebook. She and her colleagues have estimated that exposure to vaccine-skeptical content on the platform during the Covid-19 pandemic reduced people’s intention to get vaccinated. Without that exposure, they estimated 5 million more Americans would have been vaccinated, saving approximately 21,000 lives.
While people should be able to express themselves, Allen said, there was evidence that the kinds of expression she saw in Crunchy Moms could be dangerous.
“The fact that this group seems to exist and is being recommended to people, and there is so much clear misinformation in it, it seems to me that that means that there’s a failure along the way of Facebook,” Allen said.
During the pandemic, she said Facebook got pretty good at identifying and taking down egregiously false information and also not recommending it to people. She said she was surprised to now see such explicit misinformation being shared in a public group, and Facebook recommending it to users.
One scientist who spends her free time trawling Facebook to highlight the harmful information she sees there spoke to the Guardian on condition of anonymity because she has received harassment and threats. She said Crunchy Moms stood out among the worst, but was far from the only group where these problems were happening.
“Meta, and specifically Facebook, is allowing for these things to continue,” she said. She cited posts she has seen that counsel mothers not to get the vitamin K shot for their newborns and others that sell unregulated supplements. She said she believed it was more dangerous than any other groups she monitored.
“Everybody should have the freedom to talk about what they want to talk about,” Rupe said when given the opportunity to respond to that criticism.
Given the volume of the posts, Rupe said she and other administrators couldn’t vet every single post.
“I’m assuming that if Meta sees that it doesn’t agree that it will just take it down. So I don’t really worry about that part,” she said.
Meta’s rules and how they are enforced
In 2019, Facebook announced it would stop recommending groups that spread misinformation about vaccinations, saying it was “fully committed to the safety of our community”. DiResta said the company responded to public pressure as it became clear anti-vaccine activists were using Facebook to undermine public health in places like Samoa, where a 2019 measles outbreak killed 83 people, mostly children under age five. Facebook’s regional policy director said at the time that its teams were “proactively scanning for any health-related misinformation and are taking action to prevent its spread on our platforms”.
Later that year, Zuckerberg was pressed on the issue by a Republican congressman who complained that Facebook was censoring users’ speech about vaccine risks and injuries. Zuckerberg, whose wife, Priscilla Chan, is a pediatrician and who donated $75m to a major San Francisco hospital which now bears their name, acknowledged the concern. But he said “we also hear consistently from our community that people want us to stop the spread of misinformation” and that he understood “scientific consensus is that it is important that people get their vaccines”. While users would be allowed to post anti-vaccine content or join such groups, Zuckerberg said Facebook would not “recommend or go out of our way to show people content that would encourage people to join those groups”.
After Covid hit in 2020, Facebook stopped recommending health groups altogether, explaining that “it’s crucial that people get their health information from authoritative sources”.
Anti-vaccine activists complained of censorship and joined with Republican politicians and others who took issue with Facebook’s response to false information about elections and other civic matters. Then Trump was elected to a second term. His pick to lead the nation’s health department was Kennedy, a longtime leader in the anti-vaccine movement who has accused Facebook of censoring him and the group he used to run, and sued over it. The case was dismissed.
Shortly before Trump was sworn into office, Zuckerberg and Meta announced the end to third-party factchecking in the US as well as rollbacks to moderation. A few weeks later, Zuckerberg and tech CEOs attended Trump’s inauguration.
Still, Meta retained a misinformation policy.
The current policy says it removes what it calls harmful health misinformation that is “likely to directly contribute to imminent harm to public health and safety”. It lists several specific examples of what it does not allow – much of it about vaccines. Among those are claims that vaccines cause autism or Sids; that vaccine ingredients are deadly, toxic, poisonous, harmful or dangerous; that vaccines are not effective; and that measles is not deadly. Meta’s policy also says it bars promoting or advocating for harmful miracle cures, including black salve, which is peddled online as a skin cancer treatment without evidence that it works, and it has disfigured people or left their cancer to grow.
Though Facebook issued a report on how it has enforced its community standards this year, it did not discuss how or whether it has enforced its standards that govern misinformation on health.
The Guardian spoke to several people who had reported Crunchy Moms posts to Facebook or who had tried to report posts outside the group that similarly contained dangerous health misinformation. They said they had little success.
“My own personal post reports have universally been ignored by Facebook,” said Dr Frank Han, a pediatric cardiologist in Connecticut.
“While Facebook claims that they moderate, the practical effect is that medical misinformation propagates much faster than medically correct information,” Han said.
