Plaid Cymru ministers have ruled out a mass badger cull as part of updated plans to tackle bovine tuberculosis (TB).
It follows months of speculation over the Welsh government's intentions, after an expert group warned "a step change" was needed in efforts to eradicate the disease.
While farming leaders have long called for tougher badger controls, a petition against culling has attracted more than 28,000 signatures on the Senedd website in recent weeks.
The Welsh government said it would take "a new and pragmatic approach to TB eradication that is evidence-based and science-led".
While there will be no widespread cull, targeted measures are under consideration which could involve trapping and killing some badgers if they test positive for the disease.
But further research is needed first and no timetable has been set out.
Farming unions called for swifter action, while wildlife groups warned that killing badgers was "not off the table".
Latest figures show nearly 96% of cattle herds in Wales are now TB free.
But the problem - which can be devastating for affected farms - is concentrated, with higher incidence levels in west Wales and along the border with England.
There were 10,090 cows slaughtered following TB testing in the 12 months to June 2026.
Under the previous Labour-run Welsh government, a target was set for Wales to completely eradicate bovine TB by 2041.
But before May's Senedd election an expert group set up to advise ministers, external warned that goal would "not be met", and called for urgent action and more funding.
The Bovine TB Eradication Programme Board's latest recommendations were published on Tuesday, alongside the government's response.
On badgers, ministers said they were "not pursuing a mass, non-selective cull".
However further research is proposed of the role played by badgers and deer in spreading the disease via new population surveys and fieldwork.
This would then lead to developing "targeted and proportionate" interventions which could include trapping and testing badgers before vaccinating or euthanising them, "where the evidence supports this".
Localised trapping and culling was trialled in Wales on nine farms between 2017 and 2024, but the cost involved drew criticism after hundreds of thousands of pounds was spent on killing a small number of badgers.
Officials told a briefing for journalists that "selective removal" of badgers was a possibility in future, but "[we] cannot give you a timeline".
Changes to cattle testing were also being proposed by the government, to help spot infection earlier.
They will work with the farming industry to explore "safe ways" of moving cattle out of TB restricted herds, to help businesses continue to operate.
Farms experiencing persistent issues and under so-called enhanced management will be offered more choice in how their situation is handled.
There is talk of a new advisory service for farmers and vets - and moves to provide better, more up-to-date data on the latest disease risk.
Cabinet Minister for Rural Resilience and Sustainability, Llyr Gruffydd MS, said bovine TB remained "one of the most serious animal health challenges facing Wales".
"We will not stand by while farmers face uncertainty and anxiety, nor will we pursue a mass non-selective cull," he added.
"Instead, we will be taking evidence-based and science-led action, incorporating the three pillars of cattle, people and wildlife.
"Farmers and the public will rightly judge us on whether we prevent disease and reduce its impact, even when the decisions are difficult."
NFU Cymru president Abi Reader said farmers needed to see action quickly on measures promised in the government's announcement.
"Going for a mass badger cull was never going to be on the cards - that's quite a seismic change," she said.
"There's certainly this leaning towards what we call a trap, test and remove [programme] - that's really good, but we need to see that starting and actually being acted on."
"That's what farmers will have wanted to see today from this statement," she said.
"For those farmers who are right in the eye of the storm - and have been dealing with TB for a very long time - I think they were hoping they would have seen something that would have given them more hope for the future."
Ian Rickman, president of the Farmers' Union of Wales (FUW), said it was "positive to note that targeted and proportionate action will now be taken on TB in wildlife where evidence supports this approach".
He said the loss of 92,000 cattle under bovine TB meant it was now time to intensify co-operation between groups including the industry, the veterinary profession and government.
RSPCA Cymru said it "cautiously welcomed the majority of today's statement".
"However, the fight has not been won yet. The killing of wildlife is not off the table, which is a concern," said Billie-Jade Thomas, the charity's senior public affairs manager.
"The Welsh government must prioritise a science-led, cattle-focussed approach towards combating this awful disease," she added.
Nigel Palmer, chief executive of The Badger Trust said he was "disappointed".
"It's good that the mass cull has been ruled out [but] we still don't see any need on a targeted or any other basis to bring badgers back into the equation.
"Wales has led for so long on bovine TB and actually has really good performance figures," he said.
