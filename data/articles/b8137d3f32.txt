I'm sorry in advance. I know we'd all far prefer to be spending our time running around the sun-soaked dystopia of Vice City this week rather than spending it reading yet another column about the game, but we need to talk about GTA 6 again. Specifically, we need to talk about the side story that's spun off from the game's launch – namely the scramble to get it running on PC, before Rockstar itself has so much as officially announced a PC version. It's a story that's getting an outsized amount of attention precisely because GTA 6 is involved, but the consequences of what's playing out will reach far beyond Rockstar or Take-Two.
The short version of events: within a day of GTA 6's launch last week, game files from an Xbox build were circulating online, and a project called Leonida appeared on GitHub with the aim of recompiling the Xbox code into a native Windows application. Within days, it had a Discord server with close to 180,000 members and almost 3,000 forks of its code had sprung up. On Monday, GitHub enforced a DMCA notice from Take-Two against the entire network of 3,007 repositories, and Leonida's creator – reportedly a 15-year-old – threw in the towel.
The DMCA notice was never going to be the end of this story; if anything, the Streisand effect kicked in with a vengeance, with reporting of the takedown bringing awareness of the porting efforts to an even wider audience. Copies of Leonida's code have been picked up and carried on elsewhere, and multiple rival projects are pressing ahead. At least one of them claims to have the game booting on Windows already – albeit running at very low framerates once you get out into the world.
There's more than a hint of Canute commanding the tide about Take-Two's legal efforts, as they're no doubt aware. The people involved are numerous and largely anonymous, the tools they're using are freely available, and anyone with the skills to contribute to a port project like this can certainly spin up a private git server to hold their code without breaking a sweat.
Aggressive legal whack-a-mole will slow things down and give some of the people involved in the various projects cold feet, no doubt. But it still seems very likely that there will be a working – or some degree of "working" – pirate version of GTA 6 running on PCs well ahead of any official PC release from Rockstar.
It's worth noting that in this particular case, leaked files are a key part of the story. Leonida was built on top of stolen files, making Take-Two's legal position even more straightforward than it would be otherwise – but from a technical standpoint, the leak probably gave the port projects only a marginal head start.
GTA 6 has made headline news out of a problem that's even more concerning for smaller companies and games
Even without the leaked Xbox copy of the game, the ground beneath us has shifted with regard to situations like this. The effort and skill required to turn a console game into a PC one without the publisher's involvement have been dramatically dialled down, a change which applies whether the code comes from a leaked build or a retail copy.
A few weeks ago I wrote about the wave of AI-driven, semi-automated "decompilations" that has swamped the retro gaming scene; those same techniques are now being turned on brand new releases. In an interview on this site earlier this week, Tinybuild's Alex Nichiporchik talked about how easy it had been for someone to make an unauthorised port of Graveyard Keeper using REA (Reverse Engineer Anything). This isn't a problem unique to the world's biggest game – it's just that GTA 6 has made headline news out of a problem that's even more concerning for smaller companies and games.
Will any of this take the lustre off GTA 6's launch? Not really. The vast majority of people interested in the game are not going to start messing around with dodgy torrent trackers and arcane terminal commands to get a juddering, half-functional build running on their PCs – especially with fake "GTA 6 PC" downloads already doing the rounds as malware delivery vehicles. But in all likelihood, next week's build will judder less and function more, and so will the one the week after that.
Nonetheless, when the game launches officially on PC, it will still be enormous. But it won't escape people's notice that the pirate version got there first, and that's a notion the industry really doesn't want taking root in consumers' minds.
A game like GTA 6 can more or less shrug it off – that's not necessarily the case for a smaller game
Brought down to brass tacks, of course, you could argue that this is just old-fashioned software piracy being done with fancy new tools. Piracy has always been an issue for games – sometimes a genuinely huge problem, sometimes more of an obsessive fixation for executives – but the specifics of this case will still be seriously troubling for a lot of people.
A game like GTA 6 can more or less shrug it off – whatever commercial damage is done by these projects will be a rounding error on the game's success in the end. That's not necessarily the case for a smaller game. Think of a title whose console exclusivity period is part of a deal that helped to fund its development, or one that was never planned for PC at all because the studio simply didn't have the resources to support another platform. Suddenly having a pirate version pop up on a platform where there's no legitimate way to buy the game could be devastating – not just in lost sales, but in what it does to the game's reputation, its community, and its eventual official launch on that platform, if that ever comes. (Adding insult to injury, there have already been cases where developers have found themselves fielding bug reports and angry reviews for versions of their games they never made.)
If there's one thing that has the potential to cause even more damage than the piracy itself, though, it's a misguided knee-jerk reaction to it. I described piracy as an obsessive fixation for some, and I really do mean that. I've met many senior people in the industry who have expended more time and effort seething about pirates than they did thinking about how to improve the experience of their paying customers, the results of which are exactly what you'd imagine. All too often, "anti-piracy" measures – like DRM dreck peddled by salesmen who know exactly which obsession they're feeding in their exec-level customers – have made things worse for paying customers while doing nothing to curb piracy. In fact, they've frequently had quite the opposite effect, making the pirate version of a game (which strips out the DRM and other cumbersome and ineffective protections) into a better, more seamless experience than the legitimate, paid-for one.
We can fully expect, in the coming weeks and months, to see all manner of technical solutions to the decomp problem trotted out. Encryption, obfuscation, phone-home systems, and quite probably some daft nonsense involving the blockchain will all be on offer – sometimes with decent intentions, more often with a huckster's eye on how much cash can be grifted out of games execs desperate to Do Something about this new form of piracy.
Piracy, for most people, isn't really about getting stuff for free; it's about getting stuff in the most convenient way possible
In truth, none of them will work. Code that runs on a machine in a consumer's living room can, ultimately, be understood and recreated by someone sufficiently determined to understand it, and the tools now available mean that "sufficiently determined" is a much lower bar than it used to be. Every layer of protection might buy a few days, or maybe weeks; it will also cost money, eat into performance, and very likely introduce bugs and headaches for the paying customers it's supposed to be serving.
What does work has been proven time and again. Piracy, for most people, isn't really about getting stuff for free; it's about getting stuff in the most convenient way possible, for a calculation of "convenience" that includes accessibility, safety, and any other barriers to entry that may exist, of which pricing is only one factor. The solution, therefore, is for companies to ensure that their legitimate, paid-for products are the most convenient and accessible versions of themselves. That's why Steam being a great digital storefront did more to combat piracy in the PC market than every DRM solution ever dreamed up.
Pricing really only comes into it where there's a genuine market problem with pricing strategy, a point which Nichiporchik also made in the same interview: that the best way to curb piracy is to lower prices in regions where the cost of games is high relative to average incomes. That's also a factor in convenience. Accessibility isn't only about how easy something is to buy, but whether it's within reach at all. If your game costs a week's wages in a given market, you're not really losing out to the pirate version – you never had a realistic offering in that market to begin with.
The simple, unglamorous solution to PC decomps, then, is going to be ensuring that games have a good-quality, reasonably priced version sitting on Steam for consumers to buy instead – sooner rather than later, and priced sensibly for the markets where it's sold. Most people will happily pay for that rather than muck around with weird pirate software. For studios with long console exclusivity windows, or no PC plans at all, that may mean some uncomfortable rethinking of release strategy; the gap between a console launch and a PC launch is now a gap that someone else may be all too happy to fill.
The problem is a new version of an old issue. The solution, reassuringly or otherwise, remains the same as ever.
