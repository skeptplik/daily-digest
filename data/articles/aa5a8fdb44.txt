Capture of cartel leader in northern Mexico sets off gunfight that leaves 11 dead
El Chapo Isidro, a key ally for Los Mayos faction of Sinaloa Cartel, was one of FBI's 10 most wanted fugitives
A burst of violence in Mexico's Sinaloa state after authorities captured cartel boss Fausto Isidro Meza Flores, known as El Chapo Isidro, left at least 11 people dead, authorities said Saturday.
Meza Flores, one of the FBI's 10 most wanted fugitives, was captured and flown to Mexico City on Saturday after a heavy firefight between Mexican forces and cartel gunmen overnight left residents of the city of Guasave terrorized and trucks and businesses burning.
Mexico's defence cabinet on Saturday afternoon said that seven alleged cartel gunmen and four Mexican soldiers were killed.
Meza Flores climbed the cartel ranks to lead the Beltrán Leyva cartel and later became the leader of his own group, the Meza-Flores Transnational Criminal Organization. The United States had a $5 million US reward offer for information leading to his arrest, and he was wanted for trafficking drugs to the U.S. and illegally carrying firearms.
Mexico's government said in a report early Saturday that intelligence from the U.S. helped with the capture of Meza Flores, but provided few details about how authorities in Mexico and the U.S. co-ordinated to detain him.
Meza Flores was also a key ally to Los Mayos, a faction of the Sinaloa Cartel, which was designated as a foreign terrorist organization by the administration of U.S. President Donald Trump last year. His capture represents a blow to the cartel as it fights with another faction for control of the northern Mexican state.
The Trump administration and Mexican authorities both celebrated the arrest as a security win, but experts warn it could set off another wave of violence in a region already ravaged by cartels.
Night of terror after forces moved against cartel
The capture of Meza Flores and his No. 2 in the city of Guasave set off a wave of violence overnight, terrorizing civilians. Calm appeared to set in Saturday morning.
Videos show screaming civilians fleeing gunfire at a baseball game, burning cars and businesses, and circling helicopters shooting at homes below. Such shows of force by cartels are common following the capture or killing of leaders.
Vehicles on fire on a highway near Los Mochis, Sinaloa, on Friday following the announcement of El Chapo Isidro's arrest. (Yohari Leon/Facebook/Reuters)
Guasave resident and firefighter Paola Sánchez said thousands of people were terrorized late Friday night as they took shelter from intense gunfire in grocery stores and other locations.
"Last night, many businesses were burned, including Oxxo [convenience] stores, kiosks and pharmacies, along with cars and trucks," Sánchez told The Associated Press.
The city's firefighters had to take shelter at their station and wait until Saturday morning for orders from their superiors to dispatch the first crews to check whether any businesses or vehicles were still burning, she added.
"Everything looks calm today," she said.
Mexican Security Secretary Omar García Harfuch wrote on X Saturday morning that armed forces would remain deployed in Sinaloa state to protect civilians.

U.S. pressure on Mexico to crack down on cartels
Meza Flores's detention marks another high-profile capture of a cartel leader as Mexican President Claudia Sheinbaum faces pressure from U.S. President Donald Trump to crack down on cartels.
Sheinbaum has gone after illegal groups more aggressively than her predecessors, and homicides have dipped significantly in her two years in office.
Both the U.S. and Mexican governments celebrated the capture on Saturday, with the U.S. Drug Enforcement Administration (DEA) commending Mexican authorities.
"The capture of El Chapo Isidro delivers a significant blow to a ruthless criminal organization that has used extreme violence to traffic deadly drugs, including fentanyl, a designated weapon of mass destruction, into American communities," the DEA said in a statement.
"This operation underscores the critical importance of intelligence sharing in targeting dangerous cartel leaders."
García Harfuch called the detention "an important step in pacifying Sinaloa."
Firefighters hose down a burning cargo truck on a highway between Guamuchil and Guasave in Mexico's Sinaloa state on Saturday, following overnight violence after Mexican authorities captured Meza Flores in Guasave. (Martin Urista/The Associated Press)
Mexican authorities said they used "stealth and surprise" to capture Meza Flores, establishing a security perimeter around the building where he was located. After coming under attack with guns and explosives from suspected cartel operatives, armed forces personnel entered a home and detained him, they said.
Video shared by authorities shows heavily armed men in camouflage escorting a man who appears to be Meza Flores through a dimly lit house and putting him in the back of a truck.
'Kingpin strategy' causes more violence, experts say
Sheinbaum's government has immobilized a number of cartel kingpins. Chief among them was Nemesio Oseguera Cervantes, known as El Mencho, who was killed in Guadalajara state in February. Similar to the aftermath of the capture of Meza Flores, that dramatic operation resulted in a burst of violence and burning cars.
Members of the Mexican army stand guard outside the Specialized Prosecutor's Office for Organized Crime in Mexico City on Saturday. (Paola Garcia/Reuters)
Mexican security analyst David Saucedo said the capture of Meza Flores marked another instance of the governments of Mexico and the U.S. increasingly falling back on a longstanding security strategy, which critics warn could fuel more violence in the long run.
The so-called "kingpin strategy" relies on taking out top capos as a way to combat cartels. But experts say it often triggers violent power disputes among groups, like the one underway in Sinaloa.
"What is evident is this is a product of pressure from the United States," Saucedo said. "What they do is capture high-profile kingpins — the leaders — but they don't dismantle the entire organization.
"They arrested the No. 1 and No. 2, but the entire network of hit men, street-level drug dealers, businesses and political and police connections remains intact."
