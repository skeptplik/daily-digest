A large expansion of the social media ban for teenagers has been a chaotic process of “chasing children around the internet” and could disrupt websites that pose no risk, the head of the UK’s tech trade association has said.
TechUK said the new regulations, which are expected to be significantly broader than those first announced by Keir Starmer, were causing huge frustration across the industry, including in e-commerce, which had not previously been included in the ban.
Antony Walker, its deputy chief executive, said the government’s intention to minimise the ways teenagers could circumvent the ban had caught many previously unaffected companies off-guard, and he said they were likely to be given just a few months to redesign their systems.
The Guardian reported last week that substantial expansion of the social media ban for under-16s could mean platforms such as YouTube Kids being caught in the ban. The culture secretary, Lisa Nandy, has confirmed the government is also looking at including sites like Vinted, Depop and eBay in future regulations because of the “stranger messaging” function that can be used to contact teenagers.
Walker said industry leaders wanted the ban to work practically because it was clearly the desire for most parents. “We don’t want to be in a situation where parents and families feel let down and they don’t feel that this is delivered,” he said. “We understand the public pressure for this.
“But I think when you start to look at where they’re going on e-commerce sites, you start to question, where does that stop? What would the limits be on that policy? I think this risks chasing children and young people around the internet in a way that over time significantly restricts their access to services that probably aren’t that problematic.
“There hasn’t been enough proper, thoughtful dialogue with the industry about some of these fundamental questions.”
The regulations do not require primary legislation in the House of Commons, and the first set of new regulations will be tabled as an addition to the Children’s Wellbeing and Schools Act, probably in early December.
Walker said there was a determination by ministers to stop “displacement” of teenagers from social media such as Instagram and Snapchat on to other platforms. Tech companies, industry groups and MPs have been briefed that the government’s working proposal focuses on recommender systems and user-to-user content.
Experts have said that such a far-reaching ban could in theory include examples such as the user-generated review sections of supermarket websites coming under the ban and requiring age verification.
“Surely the intention here is not to chase kids off the internet altogether?” Walker said. “We haven’t really had any sort of substantive discussion on that issue.”
Walker said companies were also in the dark about a potential “off-ramp” for banned sites. Ministers have said in the past that if platforms change their algorithm to be less addictive, they may be able to return access for younger users.
But Walker said companies were being given no guidance on how to make those changes in advance of the ban, and were in effect doing the work twice – to implement the ban and then to find the “off ramp”.
“They just may make it so that there is no practical off-ramp because you’re talking about services that are so integral to the operation of the service that you could never build one,” he said.
“We’ve got no idea what the final destination is, we’ve no clarity on the immediate kind of ban and how that is supposed to work in practice. I think at the moment there’s just this feeling of huge frustration.”
He said there had been none of the technical engagement he would have previously expected from the government on the regulation. “It is a really big engineering shift, and usually there is a chance for companies to give some feedback at a technical level. There’s just been a real lack of that and I think that risks undermining the success of the policy and that’s our concern.”
When the ban was announced by Starmer in June, the government said it would ensure the ban did not include educational services, e-commerce platforms or music streaming, saying there would be a “narrowly defined list of exemptions to the definitions, which will be kept under review”.
But Nandy and the digital minister Ian Murray have confirmed in recent weeks that a broader definition is likely. Murray told MPs that platforms that redesigned their services to remove harmful or addictive functions would have a route to removing themselves from the banned list.
A spokesperson for the Department for Culture, Media and Sport said: “We have listened to more than 116,000 parents, carers, experts and young people in one of the biggest national consultations undertaken by this government, with nine in 10 parents backing action to keep under-16s off social media.
“This is about protecting children from online harms – not restricting services for the sake of it. We’ve shown we won’t stand on the sidelines when it comes to that, but we will take a proportionate approach where that harm is occurring.”
