A woman with terminal cancer has been left “traumatised and violated” after being falsely accused of shoplifting and banned from Sainsbury’s by staff using facial recognition technology.
Monia Indge, 58, was shopping with her husband in the Walters Yard branch of Sainsbury’s in Bromley on 7 November when she was suddenly told to leave by a member of staff who told her she was “banned for life” from the store.
She said she was escorted from the shop by several security guards, but was given no answer when she asked what she had done wrong.
“I was so shocked, and being surrounded by security guards in that way was scary,” she said. “I used to be a teacher, and if any of the parents or children saw me, when I used to teach my students not to not steal … I thought my reputation was ruined.”
Indge was told to scan a QR code on a poster in the shop window, which directed her to the website for FaceWatch, a facial recognition security system used by more than 125 retailers across the UK, and being rolled out in 200 Sainsbury’s stores this year.
She spent several days desperately seeking answers from FaceWatch and Sainsbury’s. Ten days later, the supermarket responded with an apology, explaining that it had been a human error and offering a £150 voucher.
“My life is short as it is. To have to spend this time trying to clear my name, it’s horrendous,” she said. “The anxiety has been unbearable. I can’t sleep. I would not steal from anyone, and to be accused in front of everyone and escorted out with no reason is horrible.
“They view this as a glitch. I am not a glitch, I am a human being.”
Indge is the latest in a string of people who have been misidentified and asked to leave shops that use FaceWatch technology, and the case highlights growing concerns about retail staff being tasked with verifying real-time facial recognition alerts.
Large high-street chains including Sainsbury’s, B&M, Sports Direct and Home Bargains are all using FaceWatch to clamp down on rising rates of shop theft and antisocial behaviour.
The software scans the faces of shoppers in store and compares them against a database of people who have allegedly committed such offences previously. In Indge’s case, a member of staff mistook her for someone else who had been flagged in a FaceWatch alert.
The same thing happened to Oleh Sinkevych, 44, when he walked into his local Sainsbury’s branch in Canada Water to buy groceries on 24 October and was asked to leave by two members of staff.
“I started asking them, well, what happened? What did I do? What’s going on?” he said. “But they refused to talk to me.”
The email Sinkevych received from Sainsbury’s, apologising for the incident, explaining it was a manager’s mistake and offering him a £150 voucher, was identical to the one received by Indge a month later.
“It’s truly awful that your staff have to deal with aggression and theft,” Sinkevych responded. “I imagine the only thing worse is innocent people being wrongly accused of being aggressors or thieves. I’m not interested in any vouchers or certificates, but thank you for the kind offer.”
He has also complained to the Information Commissioner’s Office (ICO), saying whether or not the technology itself had failed, the system was “fundamentally broken” and the software was leading staff to “switch off their own judgement”.
Jack Coulson, the head of advocacy at Big Brother Watch, said the use of live facial recognition had turned shop workers into “untrained police officers” and he urged the ICO to take action.
The watchdog is advising the government on new biometrics laws and sets out guidelines on how companies should use facial recognition technology, but campaigners say it should be doing more to protect consumers.
Last month, Big Brother Watch organised a summit in parliament for people to share their experiences of being misidentified by facial recognition software – Sainsbury’s and FaceWatch were invited to represent their views, but neither attended.
Allison Gardner, the Labour MP for Stoke-on-Trent, chaired the event and said she was shocked by the stories she had heard. “Nobody really understands where liability lies,” she said. “I just want to get hold of that whole system and tear it apart.”
A Sainsbury’s spokesperson said: “We have sincerely apologised to Mr Sinkevych and Mrs Indge for their experiences in our stores and understand the significant upset these situations have caused, especially at such a difficult time for Mrs Indge and her family.
“Facial recognition technology has been introduced to help keep colleagues and customers safe amid unacceptably high levels of retail crime and it has been hugely successful, with eight in ten people added to the system not returning to reoffend.
“Our colleagues do an exceptional job every day in dealing with difficult situations professionally and helping to keep everyone safe but on the rare occasions we do see an error we provide training and support to prevent further incidents.”
A Facewatch spokesperson said: “We are very sorry for any distress these customers experienced, and we are particularly saddened to hear of Mrs Indge’s experience given her personal circumstances.
“In both cases the system correctly flagged the individuals it was looking for, but the human check before anyone acts on an alert is an important safeguard in the process, and we share responsibility for making sure it works every time.
“We are working closely with the Sainsbury’s team to support the managers using the system and to identify any further training or support they need.”
A spokesperson for the ICO said: “Facial recognition technology can help retailers detect and prevent crime and has clear benefits in the public interest.
“However, its use must comply with data protection law. Retailers should carefully consider the risks of misidentification and have robust procedures in place to ensure the accuracy and integrity of the personal information they collect and process.”
