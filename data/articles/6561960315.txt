In most walks of life, trying something 12,363,153,437,138 times and getting the same result is reason enough to assume it works every time. Not in maths, where a conjecture that holds up more than 12tn times looks very probably true but doesn’t qualify as proven.
That was the status of the Riemann hypothesis, one of the most famous mathematical conundrums in existence, until last week. Then Levent Alpöge, a mathematician working at Anthropic, said he and his collaborator, Claude AI, had crossed the line from conjecture to proof.
I don’t pretend to understand much of what Bernhard Riemann proposed in 1859. I know it relates to primes – whole integers greater than 1 that are divisible only by 1 and themselves – and the pattern of their distribution along the infinite number line. But you don’t need to know what a zeta function is to recognise that the solution to a near-170-year-old problem is a big deal in maths, or that the role of AI in completing the quest is a big deal for humanity.
Around two years ago, large language models that mesmerised users with their verbal dexterity sucked at maths. They made basic arithmetic errors. Now they resolve unfathomably hard problems with ease. In Alpöge’s breathless account of the process, Claude “flew, and danced, and sparkled” towards its conclusion. It left him “in awe”.
Other mathematicians whose discipline is being eaten by machines report more complex feelings. In October, OpenAI published hundreds of AI-generated papers containing proofs to unsolved problems, stunning faculties that had been toiling over the relevant questions for years. Some called it the “mathocalypse”.
There is still a long process of verification to run. Humans have to check that the machines haven’t taken rogue shortcuts, but early indications back the AI. Researchers are witnessing intricate edifices of abstract cognition, their life’s work, crushed under silicon bulldozers.
Many are upset, angry. Some are excited by the future possibilities, some more sceptical or feeling a combination of emotions that an LLM can describe and imitate but not, as far as we know, experience.
Mathematicians are not the first people to worry that a rising technological tide will wash away their livelihood. But their position is unusual in ways that go beyond the conventional fear of obsolescence.
Maths describes fundamental laws of the universe. Nothing is truer than a mathematically proven truth. The limit of what advanced mathematicians know delineates the frontier of human knowledge – or it has done until now. The soaring rate of computational capability raises the prospect of machines exploring dimensions that humans cannot comprehend, generating proofs that have to be taken on trust. What kind of knowledge even is it if no organic mind knows how it came to be known?
In the past, when great mysteries have been solved, the method has been as thrilling as the result. Andrew Wiles cracked Fermat’s Last Theorem by building bridges between previously disconnected realms of maths, opening new vistas of theoretical space for further exploration. It was an arduous ascent to higher truth. AI-generated proofs are windowless lifts that hoist you to the summit with little sense of how you got there.
They are also weird, according to people who can decrypt them. Hannah Fry, Cambridge maths professor, broadcaster, author and one of the world’s best communicators of hard concepts to a lay audience, called one of the recent OpenAI proofs “almost hilariously ugly”.
That might sound like a marginal quibble if the underlying logic is sound, but the aesthetic of an answer is not a minor consideration in maths. The human mind is gratified by the elegance of a solution. It is hard to celebrate an insight extracted by tens of thousands of AI agents torturing equations day and night, burning tens of millions of dollars of computing power, just so a tech company with an eye on its prospective stock market valuation can say it got to the answer before any human.
To that objection, AI evangelists say the mathematicians are being precious. If an LLM found a safe treatment for a previously incurable disease, doctors would prescribe it even if they didn’t understand how it worked. Does it matter if humans get left behind as the frontiers of knowledge are pushed forwards?
The answer depends on who controls the pace of advance and their motives. Tech boosters talk about godlike intelligence emerging when autonomous AI models start coding their successors on a trajectory of exponential self-improvement. The mathematical breakthroughs of recent weeks don’t tip us over that threshold but they bring it into view.
That a scenario is conceivable doesn’t make it inevitable. But it would be complacent to assume that soaraway superintelligence won’t happen just because it sounds far-fetched, or because tech bros are given to hyperbole, or because their businesses are perched on a Jenga tower of debt.
It can be true that AI investment is a bubble and also that the technology is capable of superseding humanity. When the first dotcom boom turned to bust at the turn of the millennium, it didn’t reveal the internet to be all worthless hype. Railways were a transformative technology for the US despite cycles of irrational exuberance, track laid to nowhere and spectacular bankruptcies.
The precedent of past industrial revolutions suggests today’s AI pioneers will be a coalition of canny entrepreneurs, philanthropic idealists, suckers, charlatans and sociopaths. Their product will offer benefits and harms; economic gains and toxic contamination. The industry will resist regulatory constraints. Resistance will take the form of increasingly blatant corruption. It will be overcome only when scandals generate a backlash of sufficient force.
In an optimistic reading of the current moment, the feeling of vertiginous peril indicates proximity to the top of the cycle. We are at the apex of hubris, when the people who are profiting most from the absence of restraint insist that laws of economic and political gravity no longer apply, shortly before discovering that they do.
This is why brute-forcing famous mathematical conundrums matters so much to the AI labs. Proving that the machines can transcend every conceivable human achievement is a way to sustain the idea that this time is different, that an AI-powered Icarus can soar beyond the sun. And this is the ugliness that despondent mathematicians discern in those proofs. It is the difference between exploration to advance knowledge and a land grab in pursuit of power.
- Rafael Behr is a Guardian columnist
