Who is the Opportunity Party, this year’s potential kingmaker?
Opinion polls in the lead up the election showed the Opportunity party, led by businesswoman Qiulae Wong, 38, clearing the 5% threshold to become kingmaker.
The centrist minor party, whose policies include a land tax and a $370 a week universal basic income, says it is looking to “market-driven solutions” while being committed to a low-emission future and evidence-based policy.
Opportunity will negotiate first with the party that secures the highest number of votes.
However, Luxon has ruled out working with Opportunity numerous times, saying its proposed land tax runs contrary to National’s “no new taxes” pledge.
The media outlet Stuff, which is hosting a panel of pundits, has just said it is looking like Opportunity could hold the balance of power by the end of the night, given how things are currently trending.
For anyone tuning in who needs a refresher on the MMP electoral system, here goes:
MMP means voters are asked to vote twice – once for their preferred party and the second time for an electorate MP.
There is a single chamber of parliament which generally has 120 MPs. This is the overhang issue which I think we’re going to be talking about tonight as results come in.
Under MMP it is very uncommon for a single party to form a government.
If there is no majority winner, the major parties will start negotiations after the election and can either form a coalition or confidence-and-supply agreements with minor parties. The party with the largest number of votes does not necessarily win, and negotiations can take days or weeks.
Some senior National heavy hitters will, remarkably, be out of a job if National’s party vote goes badly tonight.
Those most at risk include:
Nicola Willis (finance minister), Chris Bishop (like Willis, is tipped as a potential future leader of the party), Paul Goldsmith (justice minister) and Gerry Brownlee (speaker of the house).
Some background: All the pre-election polls looked bad for any National MP who was relying on the list vote to return them to parliament; such as Willis and Brownlee, who are list-only candidates. Goldsmith (Epsom) and Bishop (Hutt South) are both engaged in tense, marginal electorate battles. If either loses, they are then reliant on winning via the list. We’ll be watching all this closely.
Some of the Labour party candidates are locked in neck and neck races, with tensions high for those at the Labour party event watching the candidates in Hutt South. With more than 20% of the vote counted, Labour’s Ginny Anderson has edged just ahead of the incumbent, National MP Chris Bishop, by just a handful of ballots.
Green MP Julie-Anne Genter is just holding off Labour challenger Craig Renney in Wellington North, while Labour’s Kieran McAnulty is winning against National’s Mike Butterick in Wairarapa.
Elsewhere in Wellington, Labour party MP Ayesha Verrall was hoping to win Wellington North off the incumbent Green MP Tamatha Paul, but early votes suggest this won’t be the case. Paul is leading comfortably, at almost twice Verrall’s count in an electorate where Green is also leading the party vote.
Another key feature of this election has been the possibility of an overhang - where a party wins more electorates than its share of the party vote.
If that occurs, more seats will be added to parliament to make up the difference and could dictate which coalition can form a government.
Te Pāti Māori (the Māori party) has been calling on supporters to engineer an overhang for its party - by voting for its candidates in the Māori seats, and not giving them a party vote. This could benefit the left bloc.
Meanwhile, if National holds on to a good portion of its seats but its party vote falls short, that will give the party an edge to form a government but could see some of its most prominent MPs out of parliament.

Seven of the seats in New Zealand’s 120-seat parliamentare Māori electorates. Voters of Māori descent can choose to vote on the Māori roll – allowing them to vote for these seats – or the general roll. More than half of Māori voters are on the Māori roll.
They still get two votes; one for the electorate, and one for their preferred political party.
Last election, Te Pāti Māori won six of the seven seats. But internal turmoil has plagued the party since. Tonight, eyes will be on Te Tai Tokerau – covering the geographical area from New Zealand’s most northern point in Cape Reinga to West Auckland – where the Green’s candidate Hūhana Lyndon could help the party nab their first Māori seat.
Latest polls put her leading the race, followed by Labour’s Willow-Jean Prime and Te Pāti Māori candidate Aperahama Edwards.
Te Pāti Māori remain ahead in most other electorates, aside from Ikaroa-Rāwhiti and Te Tai Tonga (the biggest electorate, covering the entirety of the South Island and Stewart Island) where Labour candidates lead polling.
In an election night that has come down to Chris vs Chris, it’s important to know the difference.
Christopher Luxon, 56, the incumbent, leader of the National party and PM since 2023, is the former chief executive of the country’s national airline, Air New Zealand, and previously worked a slew of corporate jobs worldwide. He entered parliament in 2020, winning the Auckland electorate of Botany. A father-of-two, he is Christian but doesn’t discuss his faith, and has emphasised his business credentials — in 2023, his party was elected on its economic pledges.
Chris Hipkins, 48, the career politician who leads the center-left Labour party, is having a second crack at being PM tonight. Following the resignation of Jacinda Ardern in January 2023, Hipkins, also a father of two, took the reins for nine months ahead of that election, which his party lost. He’s held the Remutaka electorate in parliament since 2008. Some voters still associate him with Covid-19 and strict lockdown measures, while others connect with his pledges for better healthcare and improved cost of living.
