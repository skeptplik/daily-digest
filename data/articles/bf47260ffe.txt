Jamie Tahana has delivered this very handy explainer on the overhang.
Okay, let’s get down in the weeds, because it looks like the overhang is very much a factor today.
The National party’s support has collapsed. It’s struggling to get above 30% of the party vote. Yet, on current results, it has a good chance of forming the next government.
When Kiwis enter those cardboard voting booths with the chunky orange felt pen, we get two votes: the party vote and electorate vote.
Parliament has one chamber with 120 seats. Its 64 electorate seats are given to whoever wins those constituency races, and the rest are divided proportionately among the parties, so parliament looks like the national popular vote – made up from the party vote.
Currently, while National’s support is dipping, they look like they’ll hold on to most of their electorates. In fact, they could possibly gain a few. That would leave them with more seats than their proportion of actual support. When this happens, extra seats are added to parliament – an overhang.
This also increases what’s needed to form a coalition. So, with National’s extra seats, plus an expected overhang in several of the Māori electorates for Te Pāti Māori, there could be 130 seats in the next parliament, which means 65 seats would be needed to form a coalition.
Overhangs aren’t unusual, but it’s usually only one or two seats. Tonight, we could have as many as 11.
The incumbent speaker of parliament, National’s Gerry Brownlee, a former carpentry teacher, just joked on TVNZ he might need to get back on the tools and whip up a few new desks for parliament.
Two of National’s most senior MPs, Nicola Willis and Paul Goldsmith, arrived at the party’s event just after 9.30pm, where they told media they were unlikely to return to parliament.
On current numbers, National has secured more electorate seats than their party vote, meaning there will be no room for MPs who don’t win their electorate or are running as list-only MPs to enter parliament.
Willis, who was finance minister, and Goldsmith, who was justice minister, appeared downcast as they reckoned with their fate.
“It is looking very unlikely that I will be returning to parliament, so that does come with some disappointment. At the same time I feel immense gratitude for the opportunity I have had to serve as minister of finance in Luxon’s government,” Willis said.
When asked how she felt about several top ministers at risk of being out of a job, Willis said she was confident in the remaining team.
Goldsmith said politics was “a rough business” but he was proud of his achievements.

That’s true. The party is second in party votes in all seven Māori electorates, trailing Labour.
That could show Māori voters heeding Te Pāti Māori’s call for its supporters to not give them their party vote, just their electorate vote. (TPM is currently at 0.57%, which it’s claiming as a success).
But the Greens have also been pushing hard to boost its support among Māori, and in the past three years, has been one of the most vocal critics of a slew of Māori-related bills from the coalition government.
It’s also worrying for Labour, who once had a near monopoly in the Māori seats. They’re still leading in party votes in Māori seats, but their historic dominance has definitely waned.
