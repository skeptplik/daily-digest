A French research team tracked people’s loneliness and dementia risk over 30 years.
People who are almost always lonely may be at higher risk of developing dementia, according to a new study that tracked ageing adults over three decades.
There is no current cure for dementia, which affects 57 million people worldwide. But a combination of genetic and lifestyle factors, such as exercise, alcohol and smoking, can affect people’s risk of developing the condition. According to an expert group convened by the Lancet, social isolation is another risk factor.
Researchers from the University of Bordeaux set out to measure that risk. They found that older adults who were chronically lonely had a 38% higher risk of developing dementia compared to those who never felt lonely.
Notably, occasional loneliness was not significantly linked to dementia risk, according to the study, which was published in the journal Aging & Mental Health.
“This distinction is critical,” said Karine Pérès, one of the study’s authors and a senior researcher at the Bordeaux Population Health Centre.
“While most people experience loneliness at some point in their lives, it is when loneliness becomes chronic and persistent that it may increase the risk of developing dementia,” she said.
The researchers analysed data for 3,777 older adults who were tracked from 1988 to 2018. They classified people as “never lonely,” "occasionally lonely” or “chronically lonely” based on four research visits over an eight-year period. Then they looked at whether the participants developed dementia over the next 20 years.
Roughly one in three people who were never or occasionally lonely went on to develop dementia, compared to about 49% of the chronically lonely group, the study found. Most people in the study did not experience regular loneliness.
Loneliness and social isolation aren’t exactly the same. Loneliness is subjective, and people can experience it even if they live with family or regularly see other people, the Bordeaux team said.
“This vulnerability may also stem from unsatisfying relationships, caregiving burdens, or lack of meaningful activities,” said Yvanna Simon, the study’s lead author.
The study has some limitations. It relied on one question to measure loneliness, rather than a comprehensive assessment, and the researchers may not have accounted for all factors that could influence the relationship between dementia and loneliness. But the findings are in line with previous, bigger studies that found a similar link between dementia and loneliness in midlife and old age.
The Bordeaux researchers recommended that health care workers screen their patients for loneliness and encourage more social activities. More inclusive public spaces that welcome people of all ages could also help, they said.
According to Alzheimer’s Research UK, staying socially engaged in old age can help keep parts of the brain active and reduce changes in the brain that are linked to dementia. But the nonprofit emphasised that no one factor causes dementia on its own.
For example, people who are chronically lonely may also get less exercise and eat a less healthy diet, which may contribute to heart disease and diabetes. Those conditions, in turn, raise the risk of dementia. Social interactions are just another layer.
“Lack of social interactions limits cognitive stimulation, while unresolved social stress leads to an accumulation of biological wear and tear over time,” Pérès said.
