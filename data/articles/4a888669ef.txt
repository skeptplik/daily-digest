Last week, international health authorities formally announced that the US had lost its measles-elimination status, meaning that the virus is once again endemic. But the official determination is a bit like getting the technical readout of a cracked dam while the country is already feet under water.
The national tally of measles cases is nearing 6,000 for the year, the highest count since 1991. Transmission is widespread, with 47 states and jurisdictions reporting cases. There have been 43 outbreaks this year, which account for 96 percent of the cases. Four states have case counts above 500, including Pennsylvania, which leads with 1,638 cases as of November 23. Nearly 650 people have been hospitalized nationwide. As of today, 14 people have died.
Deaths and transmission
On November 23, Pennsylvania’s health department announced its ninth confirmed death, an unvaccinated resident of Centre County. On November 24, Wisconsin reported its third measles death, an adult in Lafayette County. The report follows two other deaths in the state, an infant and a child. New York has also reported a death, which was of a 7-month-old baby. On November 20, Virginia reported the death of a child between the age of 0 and 4.
The virus isn’t slowing down. Measles is the most contagious virus known; it can linger in the air for up to two hours. Up to 90 percent of people who are not immune will fall ill after an exposure. People are considered immune if they’ve been vaccinated or previously infected. With slipping vaccination rates nationwide, the number of susceptible people is only growing.
To keep measles from getting a foothold in a community, vaccination rates need to be 95 percent or higher. According to the latest data, only 92.4 percent of US kindergartners were vaccinated with the measles, mumps, and rubella (MMR) vaccine in the 2025–2026 school year. Only 10 states have MMR vaccination rates at or above the 95 percent threshold. But even in those states, there are pockets and communities that are undervaccinated.
