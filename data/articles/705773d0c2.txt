Pharmacies are being given more freedom over the prescription medicines they can give to patients when items are out of stock.
Staff will be able to offer the same drug in a different strength or form â two 5mg tablets in the place of a 10mg pill, or a cream instead of an ointment, for example.
The change should help patients avoid repeat trips to their GP for an alternative prescription when the issue can be resolved safely and easily at the chemist.
And for the first time, children in England who miss their flu vaccination at school will be able to receive it at a pharmacy instead.
What can be swapped?
For decades, pharmacists have usually had to provide exactly the medicine prescribed by a doctor under rules set out in the 1968 Medicines Act.
If a product was out of stock, patients often had to return to their GP for an alternative prescription or wait for new supplies to arrive, if it was safe to do so.
Now, pharmacists will be able to make some tweaks without sending patients back to the doctor, including:
- supplying the same dose of a drug in different strength pills
- supplying the same medicine in a different form, such as a capsule rather than a solid tablet
The changes will apply across the UK once they have been approved by the relevant parliaments and assemblies.
Public health minister Diana Johnson said the new flexibility would make life easier for patients.
"When you go to the pharmacy to pick up your prescription, there is nothing worse than being told you need to contact your GP again because the exact medicine they've prescribed isn't available at that time, or in that form," she said.
"That's why we're changing the rules so that people can get the treatment they need with less hassle, and no delay."
Pharmacy expansion drive
The move follows a broader expansion of pharmacy services in England, which has given pharmacists greater powers to prescribe certain medications themselves without patients needing to see a GP at all.
That scheme now covers 12 common conditions:
- migraines
- acne
- scabies
- ear infections
- badly blocked noses
- sore throats
- earache
- sinusitis
- shingles
- impetigo
- infected bites
- urinary tract infections
Many other medicines, like certain painkillers and cold remedies, can also be bought over the counter at pharmacies without a prescription.
'Endemic' shortages
Henry Gregg from the National Pharmacy Association welcomed the move, but called for more action on drug shortages which he said were becoming endemic.
"These measures are a step forward but do not fix the underlying causes of shortages," he added.
"It's clear the government undervalues medicines, including underfunding vital local pharmacies.
"It must take action so that manufacturers don't favour other countries when stocks are low internationally."
The changes are separate from existing powers used during widespread medicine shortages.
If the government is concerned about national stocks of a particular medicine or medical product, it can still issue a Serious Shortage Protocol, external.
The measure has previously been used during shortages of some hormone replacement therapy (HRT) products, for example.
In those circumstances, pharmacists can already dispense an alternative without having to contact a GP first.
