Pentagon prepared to 'up the ante' in defence dispute with Canada
U.S. official warns of further measures in defence spending conflict
The war of words between the Pentagon and the Canadian government escalated on Tuesday as a senior U.S. defence official warned of further unspecified measures if Canada doesn't focus its military investments on protecting the continent.
The remarks were made in a background briefing given to Canadian journalists and approved for release by the U.S. Department of War.
The senior official described how they have been taking a very deliberate, graduated approach to get the attention of the Canadian government, starting with the suspension of the Permanent Joint Board on Defense (PJBD) last spring.
"That sent a message but has not solved the problem," said the Department of War official.
"We're postured to up the ante when we need to. If Canada cannot credibly turn its defence spending pledges into investments important for North American defence, we'll have no choice but to start looking at more significant mitigation measures."
The official did not spell out what those measures might look like, but suggested there's a sense of urgency.
"Prudence demands the department explore how we protect the United States in a situation where we cannot rely on Canada to prioritize military optimization and meet its obligations in a meaningful time frame," the official said.
"Canada has dismissed a lot of our concerns, including in NORAD, for many years. So, we want to communicate in a credible but also intelligent way that the current situation is not acceptable."
Mounting pressure
The pressure began building over the weekend with a leaked report in The Washington Post that said the U.S. military now had restrictions for interacting with Canadians. On Monday, the Pentagon publicly released a fact sheet that said Canada is not a credible defence partner and pointed to subpar Canadian military readiness statistics.
"Canadian political leadership continues to pursue unfocused global commitments, resulting in personnel and platform overextension, deferred modernizations for key equipment and low readiness rates," said the fact sheet.
"Ottawa's prioritization of parochial economic and commercial interests over military optimization introduces further uncertainty for NORAD and will further burden our Joint Force and the American taxpayer."
The fact sheet accused Canada of not being able to meet its NORAD and NATO commitments.
In the background remarks, the senior U.S. official went further, saying when it comes to delivering to allies "the gap between rhetoric and reality in Ottawa is the widest in all of NATO."
The official pointed to Germany as a model ally that quickly turned around its defence spending and is on track to deliver.
The official also said there are a group of European countries that are on track to meet NATO's five per cent of gross domestic product defence spending target by the end of the decade — five years ahead of the timeline agreed to by allies last year.
"Canada has an extraordinarily long way to go," the senior official said. "We are encouraged by the modest progress thus far, but we need to see much more credibility in terms of spending commitments."
Going into cabinet on Tuesday, Defence Minister David McGuinty was peppered with questions about the Pentagon's fact sheet.
"Canada's a sovereign nation. I'll be very direct: We don't report to the United States," said McGuinty.
He pointed to the recent federal budget, which laid out a defence spending path to four per cent of GDP by 2030.
In its fact sheet on Monday, the Pentagon said Canada needs to be more specific about how it's going to meet the five per cent target.
The federal budget was vague on spending beyond 2030 and both McGuinty and Prime Minister Mark Carney said much will depend on NATO's planned review of the goal in 2029.
Kerry Buck, Canada's former NATO ambassador, said there's obviously more at play in the Pentagon's complaints than defence spending.
She acknowledged the U.S. concerns about Canada's military readiness and its effect on NORAD, but said decades of underinvestment will not be rectified overnight.
The fact that Carney is pulling Canada closer to Europe and has made buying military equipment from suppliers other than the United States a priority seems to be the subtext of some of the arguments she's seeing.
"Maybe the U.S. complaints aren't really about what we're doing at NATO," said Buck. "Maybe the U.S. complaints are about what our focus is and the fact that we're diversifying away from an overreliance on the U.S. military, including buying American."
Steve Saideman, a defence expert at Carleton University, also acknowledged military readiness is a problem in Canada, but he sees U.S. domestic politics at play.
"This is about [U.S. Secretary of War] Pete Hegseth," said Saideman. "He's trying to do something he thinks of as being manly … bullying allies as opposed to actually getting his own house in order."
In Washington, Hegseth is facing intense congressional scrutiny and recently survived a bipartisan effort to impeach him.
That pressure is only expected to increase as Democrats take both the U.S. House of Representatives and Senate in the new year.
