On the day the Princess of Wales undertook the Three Peaks challenge, there was rain, hill fog and, finally, watery June sunshine.
It is hard to not see it as a meteorological metaphor for Catherine’s journey – from cancer diagnosis through treatment and to remission – as she treks first Ben Nevis, then Scafell Pike and Yr Wyddfa (Snowdon) within 24 hours, the camera capturing her physical challenge for a new documentary.
Titled simply Heal and premiering on the BBC from Sunday, this is far from usual royal fare. – It is powerful emotional excavation as Catherine undertakes her “pilgrimage” to search for what life is, or can be, after cancer treatment, physically and spiritually.
“I think when you’re on your hospital bed and it is so clinical around you, you haven’t got fresh air, no greenery around you, it’s very stark and alien and stripped back,” she says.
“The darkness was a really scary, disorientating experience. I think I was sort of in and out of consciousness. It was a really dark time. But also, somewhere through that, too, I became aware of someone holding my hand.
“They weren’t talking to me. They weren’t trying to fix anything. They were just there. And the presence of someone that stood by me in that time, I’ll for ever be grateful for.”
Who that hand belonged to – doctor, nurse, husband – is left unsaid. But it is implied that she is speaking not just of the physical but also of a spiritual presence.
Catherine was diagnosed with an undisclosed type of cancer in March 2024, underwent chemotherapy, and announced she was in remission in January 2025.
But what then? “It’s very hard to go back to the person you were before,” she says in the 30-minute programme.
This challenge, through which she is raising money for the Royal Marsden hospital in London where she was treated, is her “then”.
The journey “is a chance to explore what life looks like, what healing looks like beyond diagnosis, beyond treatment,” she says. There is no end goal, it is simply “what comes along the way”.
It is her ringing of the bell, which patients customarily do when hospital treatment ends but which she “weirdly didn’t realise was a thing” and so never got to do.
The main voice throughout is hers. We see her walking alone and with her brother, James Middleton, occasionally chatting with other fundraising trekkers en route, or meeting people undergoing treatment in hospitals, at pub support groups and art therapy.
We see her talking directly to the camera, shot in closeup profile, gazing occasionally into the distance as she articulates the inner turmoil, describing how with cancer you “in a way lose yourself”. She is “having to learn again to live life fully”.
We see her smiling brightly, mostly, but also in tears. At the top of Scafell Pike, James whips out his phone and plays voice notes sent by Prince William and recorded by their children Prince George, 13, Princess Charlotte, 11, and Prince Louis, eight.
Louis’s little-boy enthusiasm serves as poignant reminder that throughout all this she is a mother of still young children, and of the ripple effect of the disease on loved ones.
“Hello, Mummy. I really love you and I hope you do well going up the mountain,” Louis says. “Remember, I’m always in your heart with you, and I love you very much. And I’ll be following behind your back with you, and I’ll always be with you. And remember, I love you really lots and lots, and I give you lots and lots of love.”
George says he is tracking her on Flightradar up the mountains “and you look really, really strong. Keep going and we’re right behind you.” Charlotte is “so proud of you doing your walk and your challenges”.
Catherine wipes away tears. “They gave me the reason to get out of hospital. They’re going to give me the reason to finish this challenge,” she says, placing a stone on a rock. “This is for the kiddies.”
Speaking at a private screening this week, Catherine said cancer “profoundly affects every aspect of life”. She added: “I know this personally and that the journey through and beyond treatment requires more than medicine alone.”
She set out on the challenge to explore life after diagnosis “as a solitary experience – in a way, as a pilgrimage. But what I quickly realised is that our vulnerability is not something that we simply have to overcome or that separates us from others. It is an essential part of being human. It could be the very thing that connects us.”
Her hope is that the film will encourage compassion and show “the real value in standing alongside one another in the hardest of times”.
She sees every day as a day “given back to me”. Catherine says she hopes to remind people that whatever road they face, whatever challenge ahead, they are not alone.
Nature has helped her healing. “Even when I physically couldn’t get into nature, I imagined nature from my hospital bed. And now that I’m well enough, I feel a deep longing for all those spaces, for the ocean, for the trees, for the mountains,” she says in the film.
As for her pilgrimage, it has become “so much more than a personal or solitary experience but really a collective one”.
There is light. Just being alongside someone is enough, she says. “So often you think you need to fix something or say something or do something, and you don’t. You just need to be there. And that’s what love looks like.
“That’s what I think the light is that we can often find out of the darkest of times. In the midst of the fog and everything, it’s just so special. That’s a moment of consolation.”
