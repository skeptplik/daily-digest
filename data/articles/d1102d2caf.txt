Over a third of people with moderate to severe eczema taking part in a clinical trial have seen their skin completely or almost completely clear up after taking an experimental pill for four months. The drug targets a part of the immune system that causes eczema when it gets stuck in overdrive, and it may be preferable to the current standard treatment, which has to be injected.
“It definitely looks promising,” says Gayle Ross at the Royal Melbourne Hospital.
Eczema, or atopic dermatitis, is a chronic skin condition characterised by itchy, inflamed rashes and broken skin that can become infected. It is driven by an overactive immune pathway involving two signalling proteins called interleukin-4 and interleukin-13.
In 2017, the companies Sanofi and Regeneron received approval in the US for their jointly developed eczema drug dupilumab, which blocks interleukin-4 and interleukin-13 signalling. But, although the medication is effective, it has had limited uptake in the US because it must be injected under the skin and costs up to $59,000 per year for those without insurance coverage.
Now, another company, Kymera Therapeutics, has developed an alternative treatment that can be taken in pill form, called KT-621. It targets the same immune pathway as dupilumab but degrades a protein called STAT6 that is activated downstream from interleukin-4 and interleukin-13 in the development of eczema.
In the clinical trial, 244 adults with an average age of 39 and moderate to severe eczema were randomised to take KT-621 at three different doses or a placebo pill every day for 16 weeks.
Those taking the most effective dose experienced an average 72 per cent reduction in their eczema severity on a standard scale, compared to 27 per cent in the placebo group. What’s more, 38 per cent of participants taking KT-621 had completely healed skin or almost completely healed skin by the end of the trial.
“These results exceeded our expectations,” said Terence Rooney, the chief medical officer of Kymera Therapeutics, when announcing the results in a webcast on 23 November. He showed before-and-after photos of one man who had had severe eczema all over his torso at the beginning of the trial, which disappeared by the end.
“It’s obviously early days and the trial had relatively small patient numbers, but [KT-621] looks like it has similar efficacy to dupilumab, which is currently the standard of care for moderate to severe atopic dermatitis, and it would be nice to have an oral option as opposed to an injection,” says Ross.
The medication also appeared safe. A small proportion of participants developed conjunctivitis as a side-effect, but it tended to be mild and didn’t cause anyone to discontinue treatment, said Rooney. In fact, since the trial, almost every participant has chosen to continue taking the daily pill as part of an ongoing study, he said.
People taking dupilumab can develop conjunctivitis too, so “we’re pretty used to dealing with that”, says Ross.
Kymera Therapeutics is now planning a larger trial of KT-621 for treating eczema that is scheduled to begin in mid-2027. The company is also currently running a trial of KT-621 in people with asthma, since the condition is caused by the same overactive immune pathway operating in the lungs. It hasn’t yet said how much the drug will cost if it eventually receives regulatory approval.
