The Dragon Quest franchise has been around for longer than some of us have been alive, and its first entry in 1986 was one of the JRPG genre’s pioneers. Over the years, the launch of a new title in the series has been a big deal, especially in Japan. Before players missed work to play Grand Theft Auto 6, they were using sick days to topple wicked dragonlords.
In North America, Dragon Warrior 7 (the last entry in the series to bear that localized title), arrived after two previous installments weren’t released outside Japan. Interest had waned. When the game hit stores on Nov. 1, 2001, it was as difficult to find as its NES predecessors had been. And when loyal fans finally tracked it down, we wound up being disappointed. But over the years, Square Enix has made some adjustments and tried again on multiple occasions. Happily, each new release of Dragon Quest 7 gets better than the one before it.
Dragon Quest 7 spent a long time in development and arrived late enough in the original PlayStation’s life cycle that it felt decidedly old-fashioned by the time any of us got the chance to play it. The visuals were a step above anything the Super Nintendo offered, finally rendered in 3D with a camera that players could rotate. But the experience still felt a bit like a step back compared to the flatter but more detailed environments of late PS1-era games like Final Fantasy 9. In some respects, it was a relic from a time many players had moved on from.
The quest’s opening hours didn’t correct that first impression. I remember playing for three hours without encountering a single enemy mob, which just felt odd. In the typical Dragon Quest game from years prior, you could head outside the castle walls within a few short minutes. An hour into the adventure, you would have already battled dozens or even hundreds of monsters and leveled up your characters several times. Instead, I had to spend that time solving tedious puzzles.
Slow opening hours eventually gave way to a lengthy quest with plenty of appeal, but I didn’t actually stick around to experience those later events until Dragon Quest 7: Fragments of the Forgotten Past launched on the 3DS on Sep. 16, 2016 in the West. Despite making its debut on portable hardware, the new release offered a superior way to experience the adventure.
The single change I appreciated most was an update to the formerly tedious first few hours of play. The introductory dungeon, with all of its levers and backtracking, was gone. Introductory areas still ate up more time than I was accustomed to, but they fit within a single gaming session and left me with time — and enthusiasm — to progress to more interesting activities.
The 3DS version offered other enhancements, as well. The bottom screen provided a helpful glow when the player approached stone tablet shards, a major component of progression. Players had a lot less guesswork to find essential items, a smart adjustment that eliminated a lot of pointless frustration.
Another new feature recapped the story, in case players stepped away for a few days, weeks, or longer. Accessible from the pause menu, a chronological log listed every major event that had occurred, along with hints about where to go next. (Later games in the series, all the way up to Dragon Quest 11, would keep this addition.) While exploring, party members might also offer suggestions if consulted, sparing players from some of the ambiguity that had been a franchise staple since its first installment.
Quality-of-life enhancements didn’t stop there, either. The game also introduced enemy mobs players could see in the field as they explored. Leveling up required less grinding, and menus were modified and streamlined. A map on the bottom screen made it easy to spot nearby points of interest. Most importantly, players could make a quick save outside of churches if they needed to stop playing suddenly. For a portable game, that option was a must.
Dragon Quest 7 on the 3DS remained the best way to play the game for a decade, until Dragon Quest 7 Reimagined launched in February 2026. Available on Switch, Switch 2, PlayStation 5, Windows PC, and Xbox Series X, the updated take on the classic adventure introduced a breathtaking new art style. Presented like a diorama, the gorgeous visuals paired nicely with quality voice acting to bring the world’s story to life like never before.
The newest release also introduced new changes to the character vocation system, allowing party members to adopt two vocations at once and gain mastery in both of them. Changing a class no longer required a time-consuming visit to Alltrades Abbey, either. And on the world map, striking weaker monsters defeated them automatically with lesser rewards, a welcome compromise that eliminated the need to endure tedious skirmishes. There were other changes, as well, including multiple difficulty presets, a shared inventory, and auto-revive after battle for party members who perished in the fight.
For RPG fans in 2026, these were smart adjustments. Few players appreciate busy work and random encounters that slow the pace more than necessary. It’s good to see developers revisiting past triumphs and modernizing them for contemporary audiences to enjoy. If at first you don’t succeed, try, try again. It certainly worked for Dragon Quest 7.
