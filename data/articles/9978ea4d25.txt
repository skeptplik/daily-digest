A Montrealer died in Istanbul. Authorities blame food poisoning but what we found suggests something else
Illegal use of pesticide in Turkey linked to deaths of other tourists and residents
Bérengère Deveaux-Cattino, a healthy 24-year-old Montrealer, died suddenly in Istanbul on June 17, 2025.
A Turkish autopsy report concluded the cause of death was food poisoning.
But for her family, that doesn't add up.
A joint investigation into Deveaux-Cattino's death by Enquête and the fifth estate revealed a different poison at work and 12 similar deaths.
The recent medical school graduate had decided to celebrate by taking a trip with a few of her closest friends.
"She'd never been to Turkey," said her sister, Éléonore Deveaux-Cattino. "She thought it was a super beautiful, vibrant country."
The group hit the tourist trail and its highlights, including the requisite balloon ride in Cappadocia.
Bérengère Deveaux-Cattino had never been to Turkey before her trip in 2025, says her sister, Éléonore Deveaux-Cattino. ‘She thought it was a super beautiful, vibrant country.’ (Radio-Caanda)
With only two days to go, Bérengère and one of her friends headed for Istanbul, a city she'd longed to visit.

They checked into Room 108 at the Seven Days Hotel and went straight out to explore. They soaked up the sights and street food, then settled down for a proper meal, a chicken casserole followed by Turkish tea and baklava.
The next morning, they woke violently ill.
Laurie recalls the final days
The details of those final days come from Laurie, the woman who travelled to Istanbul with Bérengère. What happened on that trip left her traumatized and we've agreed to protect her identity and not use her real name.
She said that within minutes of waking, Bérengère was throwing up.
"Then I started feeling unwell," Laurie said, "and suddenly we were taking turns running to the bathroom to vomit until we realized that whatever it was, it wasn't just going to pass."
They called the front desk and an ambulance took them to hospital.
‘My sister is younger than me,’ said Éléonore Deveaux-Cattino, right. ‘I’d never imagined a life without her.’ (Submitted by Deveaux-Cattino family)
'Things aren't going well'
That same day, across the ocean in Montreal, Éléonore Deveaux-Cattino saw her screen light up: Laurie was FaceTiming her.
"I thought: 'This isn't normal. Something's off. This is going to be bad news,'" said Deveaux-Cattino. "She says 'Élo, we're in the hospital in Istanbul, and things aren't going well. I've been told to contact the family.'"
Deveaux-Cattino and her father, Henri Cattino, scrambled to find flights to Istanbul. While waiting for their connection in Paris, Deveaux-Cattino called the embassy.
"I said: 'Please, can you tell me if my sister has died?'" said Deveaux-Cattino. ”And she said: 'I'm very sorry, Éléonore, but your sister is dead.'"
Three days into their trip to Istanbul in November 2025, the Böcek family from Hamburg fell ill. Five days later, all four family members were dead, poisoned by phosphine gas. (@ismailsaymaz/X)
The Böcek family
The Deveaux-Cattinos repatriated Bérengère's body and asked the Quebec coroner's office to examine it. They buried their daughter and sister without confirmation of the cause of death.
As they waited for answers from Turkey, the family speculated about what else could explain Bérengère's death: Organ trafficking? Alcohol poisoning? Medical malpractice?
Then, in November, as we were investigating, a diplomatic source texted with a tip that pointed to an entirely different explanation.
"A family of four has just died in Istanbul. Watch this report."
The Böceks, a Turkish-German family from Hamburg, were visiting Istanbul. Their hotel was blocks from where Bérengère and Laurie stayed. All four fell ill and died within days of each other.
Investigators initially suspected food poisoning there, too. But within days, authorities concluded that another poison had been at work: aluminum phosphide, a highly toxic agricultural pesticide used to kill rodents and insects.
The 'bomb'
Exterminators call aluminum phosphide "the bomb." It is used in pellet form. Once the pellets are exposed to air, they release phosphine gas. Exterminators say that when placed in a sealed room, the gas kills every living, breathing thing within 72 hours. In Turkey, it's illegal to use in urban areas.
But, like all big cities, Istanbul has a bedbug problem.
Exterminator Birkan Cihan said he can barely keep up with calls from desperate home and hotel owners. Some ask whether he treats bedbugs with "gas." He doesn't.
"We tell them their neighbours could die from it, and that they could, too. But they say: 'We have no choice, it's the only thing that works,'" Cihan said
The phosphine gas these pellets emit is so potent and volatile that it can spread through a building, even if the area being treated is sealed.
In the case of the Böcek family, fumigation in the neighbouring room was determined to be what killed all four of them.
Investigations by Enquête and the fifth estate have found similar cases. In 2012, two sisters from Quebec, Audrey and Noémi Bélanger, died of phosphine poisoning in Thailand.
In 2012, two sisters from Quebec, Audrey and Noémi Bélanger, died of phosphine poisoning in Thailand. (Submitted by Bélanger family)
'That doesn't make any sense'
Just before Christmas 2025, the Deveaux-Cattinos received the autopsy report from Turkey. It concluded that food poisoning caused Bérengère's death. Unconvinced, they handed the report to the Quebec coroner's office.
"The coroner here ran some tests, and she couldn't confirm that food poisoning was the cause of death because, usually, there are signs of intestinal irritation," Éléonore Deveaux-Cattino said. "There were none in my sister's case."
We showed the Turkish autopsy report to another Quebec pathologist. Although not authorized to speak publicly, he also questioned the autopsy's conclusions.
"Two young, healthy women who end up in the hospital with food poisoning — OK, I'll buy that. But to die of it so quickly, without any signs of bloody diarrhea or other complications, that doesn't make any sense."
Turkish freelance journalist Ayse Adanali covered the story of the death of the Böcek family in Istanbul in 2025. (Radio-Canada)
Women and children
In Turkey, we started working with Ayse Adanali, a freelance Turkish journalist who'd covered the Böcek story.
Together, we documented nearly 30 cases of phosphine poisoning, including a dozen deaths, since 2023 in Turkey.
Some were tourists, like the two Dutch teenagers found dead by their father in their Istanbul hotel room in August 2025. But most of the victims were Turkish, like Karan Yazıcı, a three-year-old boy poisoned in Istanbul in April 2025 following a fumigation in the neighbouring apartment.
The majority of the victims are women and children. Members of the same family were often poisoned together. While some survived, others died. In most cases, food poisoning was initially blamed for making them sick.
'This is not food poisoning'
Şebnem Korur Fincancı is a human rights activist, professor emeritus and leading forensic pathologist in Turkey.
She's studied aluminum phosphide and knows how difficult it is to identify victims poisoned by phosphine gas.
Turkish forensic pathologist Şebnem Korur Fincancı has studied aluminum phosphide and knows how difficult it is to identify victims poisoned by phosphine gas. (Radio-Canada)
She'd never heard about Bérengère; her death had not been covered by Turkish media.
"This is not food poisoning," she said after reading the Turkish autopsy report.
"I don't think that this is food poisoning because there is no story of diarrhea, only throwing up."
She added that the timing of Bérengère's decline suggests food poisoning couldn't be the culprit.
"Rather, it is the pesticide poisoning."
Fincancı sees one undeniable detail in this report, a finding common to all victims of phosphine gas poisoning she's examined: something called metabolic acidosis, which increases the levels of acidity in the body.
"There is the metabolic acidosis, which also indicates the pesticide intoxication," she said. 'It is one of the main findings of these kinds of cases."
She believes there could be more cases of phosphine poisoning than currently reported.
"With all these misdiagnoses, we have to consider the situation."
Bérengère Deveaux-Cattino and her friend, Laurie, stayed at the Seven Days Hotel in Istanbul. (Radio-Canada)
150 bites
We scoured hundreds of Google and Tripadvisor reviews for the Seven Days Hotel to see if they'd had a bedbug problem around the time of Bérengère and Laurie's stay.
One stood out: A tourist claiming he'd been bitten 150 times during his stay in June 2025.
We tracked him down. He confirmed he stayed in Room 107, the room next door to where the two women stayed 10 days later.
The windows in those two rooms open onto each other.
That left one critical question: Had Room 107 been treated after his stay? And, if so, with what?
We contacted hotel manager Bahattin Demir. He remembered Bérengère and Laurie and had gone to the hospital after they'd been admitted.
We told him about the tourist in Room 107 and asked him whether extermination work in that room could have killed Bérengère.
"I don't think so, because if it was that product, I'm sure that, you know, everybody would have this problem," he said.
Inside Room 108 at the Seven Days Hotel where Bérengère Deveaux-Cattino and Laurie stayed. (Radio-Canada)
He said exterminators spray the rooms in the hotel monthly. He offered to contact them and find out what product they use, but then wrote back, referring us to them for more details.
The extermination company is called Wolves.
A contact called them for us. We're protecting her identity because undercover work can lead to jail time in Turkey.
Posing as an Air BnB owner in Istanbul's Fatih district, she claimed to be struggling with bedbugs.
The employee was cautious on the phone.
He didn't name the product specifically, but his detailed description is similar to how aluminum phosphide is used.
"For 72 hours, nobody goes in, the doors are locked and nothing living enters."

We called Wolves for an interview. The employee who answered the phone was not authorized to speak, but denied using aluminum phosphide.
Neither the hospital that treated Bérengère nor the Forensic Medical Centre where her autopsy was performed replied to our inquiries.
The tourism police similarly declined our interview request.
Going over notes Laurie had shared, we noticed she and Bérengère had left the window open the first night. But the next evening, to avoid being woken by the call to prayer, they closed it and turned on the air conditioning. And that was the night they got sick.
We asked pathologist Şebnem Korur Fincancı whether phosphine gas residue could build up in air conditioners.
"Of course," she said.
'Those who are gone'
The Böceks' case went to trial in June 2026. The defendants were charged with criminal negligence causing the deaths of this family of four. The ruling came down within days. The owners of the extermination company were found guilty and sentenced to 18 years in prison. The hotel owner and employees received 12 to 13 years.
While awaiting the verdict, the paternal grandfather, Yilmaz Böcek, commented that even the stiffest penalty could never bring back his children and grandchildren.
"Those who are gone never come back."
Éléonore Deveaux-Cattino wants Bérengère to be remembered.
"My sister is younger than me. I'd never imagined a life without her. We used to be two against the world … now I'm all alone," she said.
"But I'm stubborn. And since she can no longer speak, I'll speak for her. I'll never let her go."
In the summer of 2026, the government of Canada added a new entry to its travel advisory for Turkey, alerting travellers to "cases of poisoning linked to the use of chemical pesticides in tourist accommodations in Istanbul."
After this story first aired on Oct. 15, 2026, Turkish consular officials contacted us, confirming the investigation into Bérengère Deveaux-Cattino's death in Istanbul remains open.
