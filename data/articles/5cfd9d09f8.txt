Labour’s internal divisions over the giant Rosebank oilfield have spilled into the open, with nearly 100 Labour MPs – including a handful of frontbenchers – writing to the prime minister to express their opposition to the project.
Ninety-two backbenchers and at least four frontbenchers have signed a letter to Andy Burnham urging him not to let his ministers approve the scheme for the biggest undeveloped field in UK waters.
It is the clearest sign yet of the deep rift the issue has caused within the Labour party. The chancellor, John Healey, and many Scottish MPs are pushing for it to go ahead, while dozens of others argue for it to be rejected on climate grounds.
Feelings are running so high that more than one frontbench ministerial aide is considering resigning if the project is approved, the Guardian has been told.
Catherine Fookes, the MP for Monmouthshire who helped organise the letter, said: “Concern about this project is widespread within the parliamentary Labour party. Some 92 people have signed our letter, and there are more who are worried about Rosebank who haven’t signed it.
“I know the prime minister is a good listener. I hope he will see these names and see the letter and take note and think carefully about oil drilling.”
In a separate article for the Guardian, she writes: “Our continued dependence on fossil fuels is driving up bills, fuelling deadly extreme weather and causing widespread harm to people in this country right now.
“Rosebank will do nothing to reduce energy bills for our constituents. The oil belongs to private companies who will ship it away to the highest bidder.
“If Rosebank is approved, champagne corks will pop in the offices of oil giants. But it will not make an iota of difference to our constituents’ energy bills. And it will accelerate the climate crisis, which is already pushing up food bills, dealing devastating floods in winter and in summer turning our green and pleasant land into a parched, burnt-out expanse.”
Speaking to the BBC on Friday morning, Fookes’s colleague Fleur Anderson said: “It’s a very long-term proposition with a very declining amount of tax revenue … But the balancing cost – and that’s what the secretary of state is weighing up at the moment – is huge in terms of the economic damage done by climate change.”
Speaking to Sky News on Friday, the environment minister Emma Hardy acknowledged her party was divided on the issue. “There are different views in the Labour party when it comes to Rosebank,” she said.
“I know from chatting to some of my friends and colleagues, some Labour MPs in Scotland feel differently to some Labour MPs elsewhere. But I know Andy [Burnham], as our prime minister, will be listening to everybody.”
Rosebank and the smaller Jackdaw gasfield have become the focus of a growing political battle over industrial policy and net zero that threatens to become Burnham’s first major political crisis since entering Downing Street.
Many Labour MPs are already concerned about Burnham’s commitment to fighting climate change, given decisions such as cutting green projects and proposals to water down electric vehicle targets.
With the Conservatives promising to scrap the Climate Change Act and Reform UK saying it will cancel all government green spending if it comes to power, many Labour MPs want their administration to do everything it can to promote the green agenda.
The Conservatives initially gave the oil companies Shell and Equinor permission to operate Rosebank and Jackdaw, but they were forced to resubmit their environmental applications after a landmark ruling last year which found companies should account for the carbon emitted by burning the oil and gas they produce, rather than just what the fields themselves emit.
Labour promised in its 2024 manifesto not to grant any more licences for North Sea oil or gasfields. Ministers approved Jackdaw last month, however, and could approve Rosebank without breaking that promise, given the projects already have their licences and are waiting for the separate environmental permission to be granted.
The energy secretary, Miatta Fahnbulleh, approved Jackdaw last month, arguing that the additional carbon it produced would be offset by the additional energy security it provided.
She made it clear though that the same arguments would not apply for Rosebank, given it would generate far greater emissions – an estimated 1m additional tonnes of carbon dioxide by 2042 – and most of its oil would be shipped abroad.
She is understood to be leaning against approving the bigger project. But she is under heavy pressure from trade unions and some cabinet colleagues – including Healey – to give the go-ahead, given it would create about 500 jobs and generate as much as £500m a year in tax revenues.
Burnham has said it will be up to Fahnbulleh to make the decision, but Labour MPs believe she will have little choice but to approve the project if the prime minister makes it clear he is in favour.
Now the dozens of Labour MPs who oppose the scheme because of the carbon emissions it would generate are hoping they can persuade Burnham to back Fahnbulleh in opposing the scheme – even if it causes a rift with the Treasury.
They write in their letter: “Approving Rosebank risks becoming an irreversible ‘point of no return’ for the party’s climate credibility, and risks alienating members of the electorate who have put their trust in our message of hope and positivity. We urge you to show that Labour is on the side of the people experiencing climate change’s impacts, not those fuelling it.”
Many argue that creating an additional 500 jobs should not outweigh the damage done to the climate, and that the hundreds of millions of pounds in lost revenue could easily be made up from elsewhere in the government’s budget.
Many, but not all, of the MPs who have written to the prime minister are in seats which are under threat from the Green party.
One said blocking Rosebank had become even more urgent in the wake of the collapse of climate talks at the Cop31 summit in Turkey. “Britain’s role in the world is really important,” they said. “If a country like Britain cannot make a decision like this, what hope is there for the rest of the world?”
