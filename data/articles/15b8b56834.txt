Vulnerabilities discovered in Bluetooth-enabled brake controllers made by Australian firm Elecbrakes could allow hackers to manipulate trailer brakes, potentially causing accidents, researchers warned.
The affected products include the EB2, VM1 and ELBC 2000 brake controllers from Elecbrakes. According to the cybersecurity agency CISA, which last week published an advisory on the vulnerabilities, the affected brake controllers are mainly used in Australia and New Zealand.
The controllers regulate the braking force applied to trailers, with the EB2 unit mounted on the trailer and the VM1 unit on the towing vehicle. The devices can be controlled remotely via a special remote or a smartphone app.
Researchers from Australian IT and cybersecurity consultancy Element Digital — with help from AI — discovered three vulnerabilities in the braking controllers, including two critical missing authentication and insufficient replay-attack protection issues.
They demonstrated that an unauthenticated attacker who is within Bluetooth range of the targeted vehicle (up to 100 meters or 328 feet) can use a laptop or phone to engage or release a trailer’s brakes or adjust the braking force, without any interaction required from the victim.
“Your trailer’s brakes slamming to maximum while your tow vehicle keeps going is the exact recipe for a jackknife – one of the more reliably fatal things a vehicle combination can do,” the researchers warned.
The third vulnerability is a medium-severity issue related to the firmware update mechanism. Unauthorized firmware changes could enable persistent compromise of the controller, allowing an attacker to interfere with braking, brake lights, and other safety functions. However, the researchers noted that this risk is theoretical, as they did not demonstrate the full impact or attempt to install malicious firmware during their tests.
Elecbrakes was notified about the findings in mid-May and released firmware updates with patches in early July for its EB2 and VM1 products. Element Digital disclosed technical details last week.
Once the firmware patches are installed, an attacker can no longer access the brake controllers remotely without an initial pairing that requires physical access to the device and cannot be performed over Bluetooth.
The vendor is still working on a remediation plan for the older ELBC 2000 product. In a customer advisory published on Monday, Elecbrakes advised owners of the older ELBC controllers to maintain an active connection between the controller and their smartphone or wireless remote whenever the device is in use.
Elecbrakes told iTnews that nearly 40,000 app users had initially been alerted to the availability of the update, and that it had been installed on 60-70% of controllers used to connect to the system daily.
There is no evidence of malicious exploitation of these vulnerabilities.
While the researchers praised Elecbrakes for quickly developing patches, they criticized the company over the lengthy disclosure process and its failure to initially identify the older ELBC 2000 as being affected.
