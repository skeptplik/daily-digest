Google Cloud CISO talks agentic AI security and the future of the SOC
In this interview with Help Net Security, Chris Betz, CISO of Google Cloud, answers five questions about security as AI agents spread. He says the old belief that human-led workflows would stay the norm has aged badly, since attackers use automation to move through networks in minutes.
Betz explains how agents could change the junior analyst role, why espionage groups can be hard to spot, which controls companies should drop, and what boards should ask their CISO in 2027. His main concern is tracing, auditing and reversing every agent action.
Security predictions have a mixed track record. Which industry prediction from the past few years aged worst, and what did it teach you about reading forecasts?
For years, the industry assumed manual, human-centric workflows would stay the baseline for enterprise defense. Defenders stitched logs across eight consoles, approved every defensive action, and made developers file tickets and wait weeks for a security review.
That assumption aged poorly, and it’s failing in real time. Autonomous threats operate at automated speed, probing networks and moving laterally in minutes. Adversaries now compress six months of reconnaissance into hours and exploit vulnerabilities before vendors even know they exist. Against agentic hacking, human reaction time is no longer a viable baseline.
Google is both a frontier lab and the operator of the world’s most comprehensive threat intelligence network. That gives us a unique vantage point: we see how LLMs are engineered, and how adversaries target them in the wild. We predicted the rise in autonomous threats and have been actively building autonomous solutions like AI Threat Defense to counter them.
If agents take over enrichment, correlation and triage, what happens to the junior analyst career path? Where will future senior incident responders learn the craft if the entry-level work disappears?
For decades, SOCs have suffered from crippling alert fatigue. Legacy security tools have traditionally handed human analysts half-finished investigations, leaving junior teams buried under a mountain of raw tickets.
The use of autonomous defensive agents to handle data enrichment, correlation, and triage automatically, does not replace the analyst. Instead, it changes the starting line. The “alert” of the future is no longer a noisy, uncontextualized event that a human has to spend hours researching; it becomes the final, verified conclusion of an automated investigation. As our security experts shared in the blog, 2027 is the year analysts stop chasing alerts and start shaping outcomes.
Instead of working raw tickets at a keyboard, junior analysts move up the stack to set strategic doctrine, define risk appetite, and verify automated actions built for rapid explainability, where any defensive decision can be understood by a human in 60 seconds and reversed in 30. Future senior responders will build their craft by establishing graduated autonomy between specialized agents and a central coordinator, and by running continuous, proactive adversarial red-teaming to test how agentic systems behave under pressure before deployment.
Espionage actors seem more cautious about automation than cybercriminals. Does that make them easier or harder to detect, and where should defenders look for them?
Not every adversary embraces automated speed the same way. Cybercriminals go all-in on autonomous velocity. State-sponsored espionage actors often hold back, avoiding loud, hyper-fast scripts that trigger alarms and blow their long-term stealth.
That caution does not automatically make espionage actors easier to detect. Because they optimize for persistence and intelligence gathering, not noisy extortion or disruption, they deliberately operate below traditional behavioral alert thresholds.
Still, defenders hold a decisive architectural advantage. Attackers rely on guesswork and fragmented external visibility. Defenders own the ground truth: identity privilege graphs, internal network architecture, code lineage, and real-time runtime activity. When espionage actors move slowly to evade automation alerts, they leave subtle footprints across these internal surfaces that defenders can correlate and catch by watching point-in-time transaction authorizations, open-source libraries pulled into codebases by coding assistants, and uncoordinated standalone agents spun up across departments
Which security control should most organizations stop investing in next year?
Organizations should stop investing in flat trust architectures, shared human service accounts, and blanket administrator tokens, along with manual checkpoints where developers file a ticket and wait weeks for a manual security review.
It’s more about investing in capabilities to move beyond zero trust. Traditional zero trust architecture operates too broadly at the network and machine layers, leaving autonomous workflows vulnerable when agents rely on shared service accounts or blanket tokens. Moving Beyond Zero extends verification down to the individual transactions and point-in-time authorizations. This gives each agent a verifiable, least-privileged identity whose actions can be dynamically allowed or blocked based on real-time conditions.
What should board members ask their CISO in 2027 that they aren’t asking now?
The risks in 2027 won’t be just server outages or stolen databases. As the agentic operations world takes off, security must evolve to protect the balance sheet through decision integrity.
Board members should be asking if every agent action can be traced, audited and reversed. They should examine whether the company has built centralized guardrails with tamper-proof decision traces. These logs need to record the inputs entered into the system, how the model reasoned through its decisions, which tools the model called, what parameters it passed, and why policy allowed it, so every consequential action an autonomous agent takes can be governed before agent sprawl outpaces human oversight. Collecting this data will help ensure that teams can reconstruct, validate, and audit every automated decision before scaling.
