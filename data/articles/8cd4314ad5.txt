In the history books, Elaine de Kooning is often eclipsed by her husband, the famed painter Willem. This fate befell the women in the Abstract Expressionist movement, including Lee Krasner, Grace Hartigan, Joan Mitchell, and Helen Frankenthaler, who became known as the “Ninth Street Women,” all of whom received their flowers after decades of being overshadowed by their male counterparts. But you simply cannot tell the story of mid-century art—and Abstract Expressionism—without Elaine.
De Kooning, who died in 1989 at 70, sat at the epicenter of the art world for five decades. She deftly balanced two parallel practices in portraiture and abstract expressionism, unafraid to allow these genres to intermingle. Beyond her painting, she founded the artist collective The Club in Greenwich Village and wrote art criticism for Art News, celebrating artists like Arshile Gorky and Jackson Pollock. President John F. Kennedy, New York artist Toni Ross, former Art News editor-in-chief Thomas Hess, and American painter Alex Katz are among the artist’s impressive roster of sitters. A number of these portraits currently hang at New York’s Berry Campbell gallery. Each image frames its subject with expressive brushwork and vivid, bright colors. Among these works, most featuring starry figures, is a young boy in a cowboy hat, featured in the painting Jemp #1 (1963). This little kid is De Kooning’s nephew, and the reason the show exists.
Gallerists Martha Campbell and Christine Berry connected with Chris Luyckx, the nephew, who had preserved De Kooning’s paintings for decades and felt her work deserved renewed attention. By the time they met, roughly a decade had passed since De Kooning’s last notable show, a retrospective at the National Portrait Gallery in 2015, followed by smaller shows that focused on specific periods of her life. “By doing these individual shows, you miss the incredible throughline of her work from the late 1940s until the day she died in 1989,” says Campbell. “It goes with our ethos of always believing in the artist from the very beginning until the very end.” Berry Campbell’s show, simply titled “Elaine De Kooning” and on view through December 19, celebrates the artist in her entirety, featuring around 30 works sourced directly from the family’s collection
De Kooning’s oeuvre retains its wonder by evading classification, collapsing boundaries between portraiture and abstract expressionism to forge a distinct visual sensibility. Part of her practice fit firmly in the world of abstract art. Paintings like Untitled (1960) reveled in obscuring the world around her; here, she depicts New York’s verticality. “Elaine de Kooning was an abstract expressionist, but as a woman artist, she didn’t have dealers breathing down her neck saying, ‘Paint more of what we see as Abstract Expressionism,” Campbell says, noting that this provided the artist with wiggle room.
This should not overshadow her exceptional command of pure abstraction. The 11-foot-wide California (1961), a barrage of vivid colors underscoring De Kooning’s compositional mastery, sits at the very back of the gallery. Berry affirms “it is the most important piece in the exhibition,” noting that “there is no other painting of that scale or magnitude or from that time period on the market, because very other painting that is like that is in a museum collection.” Doubling down on its consequence, Campbell recalls, “There’s a great note that we found from [legendary art dealer] Joan Washburn that says that when she was her dealer, she said, ‘This is the best painting that Elaine de Kooning has ever painted.’”
Elements of her abstract work often defined her portraiture. Energetic bursts of bright paint consumed her subjects. Tom Hess (1963), for instance, features the titular journalist among flurries of orange, yellow, and red paint, leaving most of the canvas untouched. “She was an abstract expressionist, but within her own definition of what that was, so she used these abstract expressionist brushstrokes to glorify what she loved.”
De Kooning loved her subjects, occasionally obsessing over one topic and painting it repeatedly, ranging from bullfighting and baseball to the painter Aristodimos Kaldis and President Kennedy. This new show highlights all of these hyperfocused periods, including a standout from her paintings of the former president. Her connection to him started in 1962, when Kennedy sat for De Kooning in Palm Beach to complete a commission for the Harry S. Truman Presidential Library & Museum. The fixation intensified after the president’s assassination in 1963. JFK #7 (1963), on view at the gallery, is only one example of a year’s worth of works.
“She did get fixated on JFK, because she was painting him and then he was shot,” Berry explains. “That was pretty shocking. She painted him for a full year without stopping. People only focus on JFK. But she did portraits from the beginning, in the 1940s, all the way through. She did self-portraits, as well as portraits of her neighbors and her boss, Tom Hess. The portraits are a through line of her career.”
A sophisticated command of color is evident across De Kooning’s entire body of work. Bright colors clash in her abstract works, and in her portraiture, color builds texture. Perhaps most surprising, however, is that De Kooning often avoided looking at color when creating her images. For instance, she preferred to use black-and-white newspaper clippings as references for her basketball paintings, imbuing them with color guided by her personal sensibility. “Color is key to what she does,” Berry explains, pointing to the portrait of poet Frank Lima, a friend with a drug problem whom De Kooning renders in muted greens.
One painting, Bacchus #20-G (1983), an image of Jules Dalou’s The Triumph of Silenus painted six years before her death, epitomizes what’s so striking about De Kooning’s paintings. The lush painting features the sculpture’s twisted bodies, rendered in an opulent violet, with a backdrop of verdant green. It demonstrates her capacity to seamlessly balance portraiture and Abstract Expressionism, a testament to her unwavering devotion to her distinct visual language. This vision, after years on the fringe, finally receives the flower it deserves, alongside, rather than below, her husband.
