Researchers will test whether an AI tool helps radiologists detect breast cancer.
Researchers in the United Kingdom will test whether artificial intelligence tools can help improve breast cancer detection, in the world’s largest study of its kind.
Early breast cancer detection can save lives, but diagnosis can be a time-consuming process. Currently in the UK, at least two radiologists review every mammogram, or breast x-ray, but very few — less than 1% — of these scans actually show signs of cancer.
In the trial, known as EDITH, radiologists will use an AI-assisted tool to help detect cancers that are too small to see or feel. The goal is to give specialists more time to diagnose and care for women with cancer, while reducing the time they spend reviewing mammograms with no signs of disease.
“If successful, this technology could help women get a breast cancer diagnosis and start treatment much more quickly, giving them the best possible chance of recovery,” said Yvette Cooper, the UK’s health and social care secretary. The UK government is backing the trial with £10 million (about €12 million).
The study will enroll about 660,000 women aged 50 to 71 who are due for a routine breast cancer screening, which is offered every three years in the UK. Women can opt out if they do not want to take part in the trial.
Women will be split into three groups: the first group will undergo a standard breast cancer screening, with two radiologists reviewing the x-rays. In the second group, a radiologist and the AI tool will both review the mammograms to see if the AI tool catches any signs of cancer the radiologist may have missed.
In the third group, the AI tool will take the first look at an x-ray and determine whether the likelihood of cancer is high or low. If it is low, one radiologist will review the images, but if it is high, two radiologists will check the images and decide whether to refer the patient for additional testing.
The AI tool could “help experts review images more efficiently,” said Fiona Gilbert, a radiology professor at the University of Cambridge and one of the study’s lead researchers. She is also studying whether AI can help identify women at higher risk of developing breast cancer.
Every mammogram will still be reviewed by at least one radiologist, according to the trial announcement, and the AI-assisted tool will only be integrated into routine breast cancer screening if the study shows it is as safe and effective as the existing screening programme.
Currently, around 2.1 million women in the UK undergo breast screening each year, preventing around 1,300 deaths.
Breast cancer is the most common form of cancer in the European Union, with an estimated 360,000 women diagnosed in 2024, EU data shows. That accounts for 29% of all cancer diagnoses in women.
