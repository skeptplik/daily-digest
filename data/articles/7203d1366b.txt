A river separating two counties could become home to beavers as work begins to explore where a colony could be introduced.
The River Waveney Trust is in conversation with Natural England and other agencies about potentially reintroducing the river-dwelling mammals along the River Waveney.
The 59-mile (95km) waterway rises in Redgrave and Lopham Fen in Suffolk and heads east, creating a border between Norfolk and Suffolk as it flows out into the River Yare at Great Yarmouth.
Ange Lamb, catchment resilience officer, said the upper reaches of the River Waveney and its tributaries could make ideal environments for beavers. If successful, beavers could return by 2029.
Known as river engineers, beavers were native to Britain but they were hunted to extinction by the end of the 16th century.
Conservationists want to reintroduce them to help boost the health of waterways through their ability to create wetland areas.
But there are concerns, particularly around dams being built in inappropriate places and their ability to destroy trees.
Beavers have been reintroduced already in Norfolk at enclosures at Sculthorpe Moor Nature Reserve near Fakenham, Wild Ken Hill near King's Lynn and on the iver Glaven near Holt.
A pair of beavers have also been spotted in the wild at Pensthorpe Natural Park, which is a short distance downstream from Sculthorpe Moor.
In Suffolk, a colony was introduced at Little Haugh Hall at Norton, near Bury St Edmunds.
River Waveney Trust is holding a series of events to discuss the proposals and it hopes to engage with local landowners in Bungay, Palgrave and Eye.
Lamb added: "Our aim is to ensure that local communities, landowners, anglers and conservation groups have the opportunity to be part of the discussion from the beginning."
While talks are beginning, Lamb said it would be years before any decision would be made, which would require a licence from Natural England.
