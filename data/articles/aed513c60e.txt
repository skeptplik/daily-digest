Icycle
For New Scientist‘s 70th birthday, we wanted to look back at Last Word editors’ favourite questions. The Last Word launched in the 7 May 1994 issue of the magazine, with questions ranging from why some people sneeze in bright light to whether “damp” cold really is colder than “dry” cold. Since then, New Scientist readers and academic experts have been asking and answering what can only be described as an eclectic mix of questions. Here are the favourites of some of the editors who have taken the reigns over the years, along with a selection of answers.
Being asked to pick my top Last Word question is a bit like being asked which is my favourite child, but the one that stands out for me is: Why would anyone take a bicycle to Antarctica and what would they use it for? (Philip Crohn, Melbourne, Australia). Posed in the 6 July 2002 issue of the magazine, this was asked by a reader after they had watched archive footage of Ernest Shackleton’s historic expeditions.
The question of bikes in the Antarctic circles back nicely to the time when I first fell in love with Last Word, when I was working as a biologist at the British Antarctic Survey’s Rothera Research Station. Here, New Scientist was one of the prime offerings in the toilet magazine racks. Last Word was my favourite part of the magazine.
And yes, there was a bike on my Antarctic base too, which the radio operator avidly used to cycle through the snow.
Alison George,
editor from 2020 to 2022
Robert Headland
Scott Polar Research Institute, University of Cambridge
Bicycles have been used sporadically in the Antarctic from the time of Captain Robert Scott’s fatal expedition in 1912. Indeed, one of the relics discovered in Scott’s hut at Cape Evans is a rusted bicycle. This was a gift from a New Zealand firm to the geologist Thomas Griffith Taylor, who later founded the Geographical Society of New South Wales and its journal The Australian Geographer. Prior to joining the expedition, Taylor studied in Cambridge, where cycling is a very popular form of transport. This undoubtedly explains his enthusiasm for bicycles.
This enthusiasm was soon snuffed out when he tried to make a 25-kilometre return journey to Turk’s Head from the Antarctic base camp and got into difficulties. He had to be rescued and later wrote: “I made a vow that the first bicycle ride in the Antarctic would be my last, and have every intention of keeping that vow.”
While I agree that bicycles are not a very practical form of transport in the Antarctic, they certainly have their uses – especially their wheels. Herbert Ponting, the famous polar photographer, used a cart that was fitted with bicycle wheels to take his equipment over the ice, hauled by a sledge dog. Antarctic researchers use bicycle wheels, fitted with cyclometers and attached to the back of sledges by a flexible link, to measure distances.
Bicycles rarely get a mention in expeditionary accounts, but they occasionally appear in photos, as in Shackleton’s Endurance expedition. Most are taken south by cycling enthusiasts but are generally more useful in ports visited on the way.
McMurdo station, a very large US installation in Antarctica, has several bicycles in use, mainly mountain bikes, that are used for both recreation and transport.
Squeezing out
In 2023, Last Word received a question that readers really threw themselves behind: What is the shortest space a parked car can get out of? Or a parking car can get into? (Toby Ebert, Ilfracombe, Devon, UK).
As editor of this section for three years, I learned that there are some things our readers particularly love: maths questions and sharing personal anecdotes. This offered the opportunity for both, and the answers poured in. Pure joy – although, despite one assertion that “this can probably be done with 50 millimetres or less of spare space”, my own opinion that I need at least 50 per cent longer than the length of my car was not changed.
Alison Flood, editor from 2022 to 2025
Hillary Shaw
Newport, Shropshire, UK
Since cars are essentially rectangular, the very shortest space a car can theoretically enter or exit, by Pythagoras’s theorem, is the diagonal from one front corner to the back corner on the opposite side, so 4.47 metres for an average car of 2 m × 4 m.
However, at this diagonal point, the (for example) left rear corner of the car is touching the kerb, the right rear corner is touching the car behind, the front left corner is touching the car in front and you have no space to manoeuvre to get fully in. Geometrically, if the car could rotate, you would need the same 0.47 m at the front to swing the car fully in, total space required 4.94 m. However, car wheels on an axle cannot physically turn by 90 degrees, so even this 4.94 m space would require an approaching-infinite-point turn to enter. You can spend approaching eternity to get in that space, or look for another space of maybe 5.5 m.
It is also possible to shift an adjacent car either by shunting or simply by nudging up and using your engine to overcome the other car’s brakes. But I don’t think this parking method is approved in the UK Highway Code.
Patrick Forsyth
Maldon, Essex, UK
Many years ago, when I was at school, a favourite trick was to get a gang of pupils together and lift a master’s Mini, wedging it between two trees. You only needed a couple of centimetres each end to get it in, but it could only be got out by more lifting. I think cars are heavier these days.
Blank sky
While I’m relatively new to editing Last Word, having started with the 15 August 2026 issue of the magazine, I’m not new to loving it. It was genuinely challenging to whittle down my long list to just one favourite question, but it was also a lot of fun.
I landed on one from the 17 May 2025 issue – How would human civilisation differ if the sun, moon and planets were always obscured by clouds? (Simon Rockett, Debenham, Suffolk, UK) – as it combines my bias for anything to do with humanity’s story with a personal preference for more speculative Last Word questions. From empires to time-keeping and plant evolution, the responses take you on a thought-provoking journey – even if it is one that couldn’t rely on the sun and stars for navigation.
Sophie Berdugo, editor from 2026
Guy Cox
Sydney, Australia
Probably not much would have been different. If the stars and planets were always hidden, there would be no astrology – a plus.
We would still see day and night, and the seasons, so we would have calendars. Sundials are obviously out, but clepsydras (water clocks) have been used by ancient civilisations all over the world since at least 1300 BC, so we could tell the time.
The biggest difference would probably be that we couldn’t navigate by the sun and stars, so travel across large bodies of water wouldn’t be practicable. That wouldn’t stop humans from populating most of the globe, but it would have stopped colonial powers from taking over distant countries.
Alex McDowell
London, UK
Navigation at sea would have been difficult because, prior to the invention of radio beacons, inertial guidance, satellite navigation and more, sailors used celestial bodies to navigate. They would have needed to rely heavily on dead reckoning.
Some argue that, before the magnetic compass, Vikings used crystals called sunstones to locate the sun in overcast conditions. Sunstones are able to take advantage of the fact that light scattered through cloud vapour is polarised, but light straight from the sun is not.
The first clocks were sundials, but these would be useless if we were always under cloud cover. But sunstones could be used to find which direction the sun was in, and when. One could use sunstones together with magnetic compasses to work out the time.
Perhaps these difficulties would have led to mechanical clocks being developed more quickly.
Astronomy would be delayed. Celestial bodies would be visible only if one could get above the clouds. If no mountain went above them, people would still be curious to know what was there, and this could lead to balloons and other aircraft being developed faster. Until people could get above the clouds, there would have been a lot of speculation about what was in and above them – and perhaps stories like Jack and the Beanstalk would have sounded credible!
Life would also have evolved differently. Plants may have developed a type of chlorophyll that uses more of the electromagnetic spectrum, absorbing even green light, and thus appearing black.
Anecdotes from the first editor
In the early days of Last Word, I used to put some questions to the test. For example, I remember buying a case of champagne and drinking it over the course of a week to disprove the fact that a teaspoon in the neck of the bottle keeps it fizzy (we needed a lot of controls). Such a hardship. It doesn’t, by the way; it stays fizzy for a long time if simply left alone.
Also, in the late 1990s and early 2000s, there was a penchant for voluminous restaurants in London with lots of wooden floors and steel fittings. We wanted to know if they were breaking legal workplace noise levels. So I got to eat at about 10 of them, taking a decibel meter with me, and we found that the noise way exceeded legal limits, meaning the staff should all have been wearing ear defenders.
As for favourite questions, I remember we asked why snot was green. Throughout my entire time at New Scientist, I was constantly asked that. But my fave question was from the 20 March 2004 issue: How fat do you need to be to be bulletproof? (Ward van Nostram, via email).
I always thought that was the perfect Last Word question: amusing, quirky, but having some scientific basis, and I was pretty sure somebody would have done the research. And it turned out they had. The US military had looked into it and found that you needed something like a 60-centimetre-thick layer of fat around your torso, thus rendering any soldier immobile and not very effective.
Mick O’Hare, editor from 1994 to 2018
