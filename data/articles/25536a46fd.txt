There are few things Star Trek fans love more than a good crossover. Bringing together characters from different eras of the franchise is practically guaranteed to generate excitement, particularly when those characters happen to be two of Starfleet's most legendary captains. But what happens when the movie designed to unite them doesn't quite live up to expectations?
Released in 1994 and now streaming free on Pluto TV, Star Trek: Generations brought Captain James T. Kirk (William Shatner) and Captain Jean-Luc Picard (Patrick Stewart) together on the big screen for the first and only time. The result was a movie that divided fans, delivered one of the franchise's most controversial deaths, and destroyed one of television's most beloved starships. Yet more than three decades later, Generations deserves another look, particularly for what it has to say about growing older, accepting loss, and knowing when it's time to move on.
Star Trek: Generations has a surprisingly powerful message about growing older
The film opens with Kirk, Montgomery Scott (James Doohan), and Pavel Chekov (Walter Koenig) attending the maiden voyage of the Enterprise-B. Kirk is retired, although it's obvious he hasn't entirely adjusted to life outside the captain's chair.
When Kirk discovers that the ship's helmsman is Demora Sulu (Jacqueline Kim), daughter of his former crewmate Hikaru Sulu (George Takei), he's surprised his old friend found time to start a family. Scotty reminds him of something Kirk himself has always said: If something is important, you make the time.
It's a seemingly insignificant exchange that establishes one of the movie's central themes. Kirk has spent his life exploring the galaxy, but what has he missed along the way? He's saved countless lives and made history, yet he's struggling to find purpose without the Enterprise.
Decades later, Picard faces his own reckoning with mortality after learning that his brother Robert and nephew René have died in a fire. The tragedy leaves him confronting the possibility that his family line will end with him, along with the realization that the life he chose came at the expense of another he might have had.
Both captains eventually find themselves inside the Nexus, a mysterious energy ribbon that offers its inhabitants a seemingly perfect existence. For Picard, that means experiencing the family life he never had. Kirk finds himself back in a peaceful home, with the opportunity to reconnect with a woman from his past. Perhaps Edith Keeler?
The temptation is understandable. Who wouldn't want the chance to revisit a happier moment or correct a regret? But Generations makes the case that a meaningful life isn't necessarily a perfect one. Sometimes it's the choices we make, and the sacrifices that come with them, that give our lives purpose.
Star Trek: Generations is better than its reputation suggests
None of this means Generations is without problems. Far from it. The movie's villain, Dr. Tolian Soran (Malcolm McDowell), wants to return to the Nexus so badly he's willing to destroy entire star systems to make it happen. While McDowell brings considerable menace to the role, Soran never quite becomes the memorable antagonist the story needs.
Then there's Kirk's death, which remains one of the most debated moments in Star Trek history. After surviving Klingons, Romulans, Khan, and countless other threats, the legendary captain meets his end beneath a collapsing bridge. For a character who spent decades boldly going where no one had gone before, it's not exactly the grand farewell many fans imagined. And yet there's plenty here to appreciate.
Stewart delivers some of his finest work as Picard struggles with grief, particularly during a quiet conversation with Counselor Deanna Troi (Marina Sirtis). The destruction of the Enterprise-D is also genuinely shocking, especially for viewers who spent seven seasons watching the ship become almost as much a character as the people aboard it.
There's also the introduction of Data's (Brent Spiner) emotion chip, which provides some welcome humor, even if the movie occasionally leans a little too heavily into the android's newfound feelings.
Most importantly, Shatner and Stewart make the film's long-awaited meeting worthwhile. Their scenes together aren't built around elaborate action sequences or clever one-liners. Instead, they feature two captains reflecting on what it means to make a difference. It's a quieter approach than some viewers might have expected, but one that feels appropriate for these characters.
Star Trek: Generations marked a changing of the guard
Beyond its story, Generations occupies a unique place in Star Trek history. It was the franchise's first theatrical film built around the cast of Star Trek: The Next Generation, arriving just months after the television series concluded its seven-season run.
For years, Kirk and his crew had been the faces of Star Trek on the big screen. Now it was Picard's turn. The movie makes that transition literal. Kirk begins the film as a celebrated former captain watching a new Enterprise depart without him. By the conclusion, he's helping Picard save millions of lives and making one final sacrifice before leaving the future in another captain's hands.
The destruction of the Enterprise-D reinforces that transition. The ship that carried Picard and his crew through seven seasons of television adventures is gone, clearing the way for a new chapter. The Next Generation cast would return in three more films, beginning with 1996's Star Trek: First Contact.
There's something fitting about a movie so concerned with the passage of time serving as the bridge between two eras of Star Trek. The franchise was changing, just as its characters were learning to accept change in their own lives.
Generations may not be the best Star Trek movie, but beneath the spectacle, questionable decisions, and collapsing bridges is a thoughtful story about two men who devoted their lives to making a difference. When Kirk gets one last opportunity to do exactly that, he doesn't hesitate. For all the movie's imperfections, it's hard to imagine a more appropriate final choice for the captain of the Enterprise.
