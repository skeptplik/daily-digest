What is great "game direction"? We love asking those in charge because everyone's answer is different.
Players' minds tend to jump to cinematic AAA blockbusters, where cutscenes and spectacle make the director's hand easy to recognize. But the beauty of video games is that greatness comes in endless forms. To prove out the point, Polygon assembled some of the creative minds behind 2026's most ambitious games to dig into what we believe remains a misunderstood art: the delicate balance of artistic vision, technical execution, and player experience that can transform a collection of ideas into a Game of the Year contender.
For some developers, great direction starts with the conviction to attempt something nobody else has done. Control Resonant director Mikael Kasurinen argues, "You need to be distinctive. You need to be doing things that nobody else is doing." For others, originality is beside the point. "I don't wanna spend a lot of time figuring out how to make it special and different," says Queen's Domain creative lead Eric Berger. "I just wanna make every aspect of it as good as we can figure out how to make it." And sometimes an entire creative vision begins with a question as ridiculous as the game it inspires: Denshattack director David Jaumandreu wondered what would happen if you could play Tony Hawk with a train.
These wildly different philosophies all arrive at the same challenge: How do you make something that feels unmistakably your own while remembering that somebody actually has to enjoy playing it?
Across this wide-ranging conversation, the people shaping the next generation of games reveal how those ambitions survive contact with reality. From Berger's two-person team building the strange, interconnected world of Queen's Domain to Capcom's developers balancing terror and action in Resident Evil Requiem and inventing an entirely new gameplay language for Pragmata, the scale of the challenge changes, but the fundamental questions remain. The creators of Cairn explain how to make players feel the physical and emotional weight of climbing a mountain; the minds behind Mina the Hollower, Star Wars: Zero Company, 007 First Light, and Zero Parades wrestle with everything from difficulty and player freedom to narrative, collaboration, and the sacrifices required to make a singular vision work.
There's no universal formula for great gamemaking — but everyone who joined us for this video has the creative guts to throw themselves into the work to see what happens. Here’s my GOTY prediction: If you stick around to the end, you'll come away with a richer understanding of how invisible decisions fuse with big-picture vision to produce games we’ll be talking about long after 2026.
