Nearly half of people from minority ethnic backgrounds have experienced racism at work and the majority have no confidence that HR would take their concerns seriously, a survey has found.
It also found that 75% of people from the UK’s five largest minority ethnic communities had experienced racism or unfair treatment linked to their race, ethnicity or religion in the previous year, and three-quarters of those who had experienced racism said it had affected their mental health to some degree.
Susan Solomon, a senior research manager and lead author of the Centre for Mental Health’s report, said: “When three-quarters of people who have experienced racism report an impact on their mental health, it is clear that racism is not just an equality issue. It is a public mental health issue.”
The findings of the survey of 2,506 adults come amid reports that racial equality is worsening and discrimination is becoming more common in the UK.
Jabeer Butt, the chief executive of the Race Equality Foundation, said the research demonstrated “how widespread racism remains in everyday life and the cumulative toll it takes on the mental health of Black, Asian and minoritised ethnic people”.
In the workplace, 45% of ethnic minority workers said they had experienced racism at work in the past year, most often passed off as “banter”, while 56% had no confidence that HR would take them seriously.
The research also revealed that 42% of ethnic minority people who had faced racism linked it to anti-immigrant protests and demonstrations, 57% saw negative posts about immigrants or minorities on social media every week, and 46% heard politicians blaming immigrants for the UK’s problems just as often.
Butt said the results showed that “racism does not occur in a vacuum”. He said: “Hostile anti-immigration rhetoric, amplified in politics and across social media, can legitimise racist attitudes and behaviour, echoing our findings following the racist riots of 2024.
“The response cannot be to ask people to become more resilient or simply to report individual incidents. Political leaders, employers, public institutions and social media platforms must challenge the narratives that fuel racism and take effective action to prevent it.”
The research highlighted a gap between experiencing racism and seeking support and help. Of the respondents who had experienced racism or unfair treatment, half said they felt they had to be constantly “on guard” and ready for racist encounters, while only 5% of people who experienced racism sought professional mental health support.
Solomon said: “Our findings show that the harm does not come only from overt acts of discrimination but from the constant effort of anticipating, managing and responding to racism in everyday life. People described living in a state of vigilance, weighing decisions about where they go, how they behave and whether it is safe to speak up.”
She added: “The research also shows that the mental health impact of racism is not limited to personal experiences of discrimination. It is compounded by exposure to anti-immigration and discriminatory rhetoric in political speeches, newspaper coverage and social media. If we are serious about protecting mental health, action must be taken to prevent the harm caused by racism before it reaches crisis point.”
