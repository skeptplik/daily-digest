A Vulcan rocket operated by United Launch Alliance, a joint venture of Boeing and Lockheed Martin, failed about 10 minutes after liftoff from the Cape Canaveral Space Force Station on Sunday. The incident resulted in the loss of all 40 AmazonLeo internet satellites aboard, marking the company's first mission failure.
"Based on data received by our Launch Director, we confirm that the Vulcan vehicle launched from Space Launch Complex 41 suffered an anomaly 10 minutes into the flight and failed to deliver the Amazon mission to orbit," ULA said in a statement.
The exact cause was not immediately clear.
"Every flight generates data, and every flight teaches us something. Since the anomaly just occurred, we don't have any specific data yet to understand what occurred today," ULA added.
The failed launch marks a major setback for ULA's new flagship rocket. The two-decade-old company was set to begin a regular cadence of launches for Amazon's burgeoning satellite internet program.
Mission failure raises fresh concerns over US launch capacity
The failure is expected to trigger a lengthy technical investigation, potentially grounding the Vulcan rocket for months.
US companies could face reduced launch capacity amid other rocket failures, industry challenges and uncertainty over the future availability of SpaceX's Falcon 9, the world's most active rocket. SpaceX is no longer accepting bookings for the rocket.
"There is risk with any launch, and that's especially true for inaugural missions like LV-01," Amazon said in a statement, referring to Sunday's mission. "ULA has been a trusted partner for Amazon, and we believe they'll learn from this mission and deliver on their long-term plans for Vulcan."
Is ULA in trouble?
ULA was formed in 2006 through the merger of Boeing's Delta rocket program and Lockheed Martin's Atlas rocket program. The incident marked the first total mission failure in the company's history, ending its record of successful mission deliveries since its formation.
Sunday's launch was Vulcan's fifth since its debut in 2024 and its first in nine months. The gap followed separate technical investigations into its solid rocket boosters and core BE-4 engines, which are supplied by Blue Origin, the space company founded by Amazon founder Jeff Bezos.
The solid rocket boosters had come under scrutiny after anomalies on Vulcan's previous two flights, including a February 2026 mission for the US Space Force. Both missions successfully delivered their payloads into orbit despite the booster problems.
US national security clearance for Vulcan under scrutiny
The Space Force paused Vulcan's national security launches pending the outcome of an investigation and the implementation of corrective measures. Sunday's Amazon mission was expected to provide further data before the military decided whether to clear the rocket for those launches.
An investigation traced the problem to the engine's main oxygen valve. Blue Origin subsequently developed modifications for the BE-4 engines supplied to ULA, and the engines installed on Vulcan were retrofitted, ULA officials said.
It remains unclear whether the BE-4 engines played any role in Sunday's failure.
Nearly 400 satellites launched as Amazon challenges Starlink
Amazon relies on third-party launch providers to deploy satellites for its Leo internet network. Other launch providers have also faced technical problems and delays.
Blue Origin's New Glenn explosion in May occurred during a ground test, just days before the rocket was scheduled to launch Amazon Leo satellites.
Repeated launch setbacks have complicated Amazon's efforts to compete with SpaceX's Starlink satellite internet network, which has more than 11,000 satellites and millions of customers worldwide.
SpaceX's advantage over satellite internet rivals is partly due to its ability to use its own rockets to deploy satellites. Other companies rely on external launch providers, adding to the cost and complexity of building their constellations.
Before Sunday's failed mission, Amazon had launched 398 Leo satellites across 14 missions since 2025, according to Reuters. That represented about 12% of the 3,236 satellites the company needs for global coverage. Amazon had hoped to begin initial internet services this year.
