The New Scientist Christmas gift guide is assembled by our crack team of science editors and experts. If you’re struggling for gift ideas, you will find unexpected and ingenious suggestions for amateur astronomers and animal and nature lovers, as well as those fond of food, beauty and puzzles. And surely that covers all of us?
Puzzlingly beautiful: a science jigsaw
If you’re looking for a quick and easy jigsaw to breeze through while watching holiday movies, Nervous System puzzles probably aren’t for you. But if you want something that’s part puzzle, part work of art, and that will keep you transfixed for hours, they’ll definitely do the trick. Nervous System makes laser-cut wooden puzzles with uniquely shaped pieces and science themes, and they are absolutely beautiful. I’m particularly a fan of their geode puzzles, featuring concentric wiggles of bright colour, including wiggly sides that defy every puzzler’s instinct to start with the edge pieces. For even more of a challenge, they make infinity puzzles that can be assembled and reassembled many different ways – those have no edge pieces at all, and some don’t even have a picture on them! Nervous System also sells absolutely stunning jewellery, lamps and sculptures, but to me the puzzles are the real show-stoppers.
Nervous System puzzles, from $45 (£32)
Leah Crane
Zoom in: splashing out on binoculars
The eccentric filmmaker Werner Herzog famously declared that he travels with only a second set of underwear, a map, a toothbrush and a set of binoculars. I don’t travel quite so lightly, but on my next trip, I definitely want to add a pair of nice binos to my baggage. I tested out three sets at varying price levels, taking them down to my favourite spot on my local river and out to my backyard during a clear night with a half-moon overhead. The Nikon Prostaff P3 8×42 binoculars are extremely well balanced and were the most comfortable in my hands. They were particularly well-suited for twilight and brighter. The other Nikon set I tried was the Stabilised 12×25 S, which is much more compact. For my small hands that was fine, and the stabilisation was marvellous at reducing any shaking, both while looking at something fixed like the moon or moving quickly to track an eagle fishing for dinner. The stabilisation requires batteries, so these are probably best –suited to the more prepared adventurer. The stand–out pair was the CL Companion 8×30 from Swarovski Optik. I’ve never seen anything look better than they did through these lenses. They let in an enormous amount of light, making the world look like the brightness has been turned up. And the moon’s craters looked stunningly crisp. For the birdwatcher, wildlife lover or stargazer in your life, these would be a worthwhile, although pretty major, splurge.
Nikon Prostaff P3 8×42, £159
Nikon Stabilised 12×25 S, £729
CL Companion 8×30 from Swarovski Optik, from £1070
Chelsea Whyte
Sounds good: a bat detector
Thomas Nagel’s famous 1974 essay, “What is it like to be a bat?”, argued that we can never fully know the experience of being a bat (or any other animal). I have been testing that argument with my fantastic bat detector. The device picks up on the ultrasound frequencies emitted by bats as they fly overhead and converts them into clicks that we can hear. It also displays the frequency of the squeak, and that allows us to identify the species. In my London back garden I get mostly pipistrelles, our smallest bat at just 4 grams. Nagel is right – I don’t know what it is like to fly and catch moths by echolocation. But it is quite thrilling to hear the clicks as a bat swoops past in the dark, and that experience has got me a little closer to appreciating what it is like to be a bat. There are several options available, but I used the Magenta 5 bat detector from Wildcare.
Magenta 5 bat detector, £105
Rowan Hooper
Time for bed: a jet lag app
If you have a jetsetter in your life, they are probably by definition the kind of person who is tricky to find a gift for. So why not gift them something money can’t buy (well, almost): an end to jet lag? Many moons ago, I was on a journalism fellowship about sleep at Harvard Medical School, when I visited the lab of sleep scientist Steven Lockley. I was fascinated by his experiments, including work he was doing with NASA astronauts, which looked at circadian rhythms, sleep and what happens when our bodies are out of sync with the natural rhythms of the day. Aside from astronauts, many of us who remain earthbound can still feel these effects – especially those who do regular shift work or long-distance travel. What’s more, a body clock that’s out of whack can have serious health consequences.
I followed Lockley’s research closely over the years, and he was generous enough, whenever I travelled back to Boston, to make me a tailored jet-lag plan – when to sleep or nap in the days before and during my flight, as well as when to wear sunglasses and drink caffeine, all based on what time my flight was and the time difference. Fast forward about a decade, and this service is no longer a personal one – Lockley has created an app called Timeshifter, which allows users to plug in their flight details and instantly receive a step-by-step plan to minimise jet lag. There’s a version of the app for shift workers, too.
I recently tried it on a trip to New York, purchasing a one-off plan rather than the subscription, and I can honestly say that I barely felt any jet lag at all – while my husband was falling asleep in his sushi at dinner.
Timeshifter app, $6.99/month or $69.99/year (£5.30/month or £53/year)

Strong feet: barefoot trainers
Forget spin classes and aerobics – fitness these days is all about strength over heart rate. If a loved one has started talking about “Lifting PBs” and “protein-maxxing”, you could do much worse than getting them some proper footwear for their gym trips. There’s emerging evidence to suggest barefoot trainers strengthen foot muscles compared with regular trainers, including the small, stabilising muscles that improve balance. Vivobarefoot’s Motus Strength are specifically designed for weightlifting sessions – I’ve been wearing them to the gym for the last few weeks and am a total convert. You can also get barefoot shoes for hiking and trail running, for those who prefer the great outdoors to the weights floor.
Vivobarefoot Motus Strength, £150

Collect them all: animal trading cards
If, like me, you grew up collecting Pokémon cards but now shudder at the prices and scalpers, there’s a brilliant alternative, and it’s science-themed. Life TCG lets players collect, trade and play using cards based on real-world creatures, with gameplay reminiscent of Top Trumps. But the artwork is the real highlight, a charming mix of realistic paintings, quirky cartoons and special-edition photography. One of my first packs even revealed a stunning South Chinese tiger card (one of my favourite animals). The gameplay itself is fairly simple, but the cards are beautiful enough to work as collectible art pieces in their own right. Better still, first-edition holographic cards are genuinely rare. Each is printed in numbers matching that animal’s estimated population in the wild. Life TCG also donates a portion of its profits to WWF. Collecting cards that look great, teach you something and support wildlife conservation? That’s a rare win.
Life TCG cards, from £15

Cheers: a guide to the glories of wine
For more than 8000 years (in between struggling to survive), humans have been harvesting and fermenting grapes to make the delicious liquid we call wine. Over that time, we’ve amassed more knowledge about this plant-based product than any other – except the secrets of what gives a wine its distinctive quality. Noble Nine is a state-of-the-art guide to that quest and many other things vinous by researcher Andrew Wood – perhaps the only academic anywhere lucky enough to do a PhD in the biology of wine quality. Our first metaphorical glass comes from the merlot wine grape, and we cheerfully drink our way through the other eight in Wood’s selection, including chenin blanc, pinot noir, shiraz, and ending up with sauvignon blanc. Cheers, everyone!
Noble Nine: The story and science of the world’s favourite wines by Andrew Wood, £20

I’ve got a little herb garden outside on my patio, and there’s no better feeling during the warmer months than popping out to fetch some fresh coriander or basil midway through dinner prep. An indoor herb garden that lets you grow fresh herbs all year round. I’ve tested a few models, and the Auk Mini 2 is the winner for easy setup, stylish design and bountiful harvest. It fits on a shelf in my kitchen and costs about 50p per week to run.

Make your own: a 3D printer
For my son’s 10th birthday, his grandmother wanted to gift him a very extravagant present – a 3D printer. I wasn’t convinced at first, worried it would be too big for the house, too technical, dangerous (who wants to let their kids play with melting plastic?) and generally a bad idea. A few months in, I’m happy to be recommending this as a great present for a very special young person in your life. We went for the Bambu Labs A1 mini, which is small enough to be stored out of sight and brought out for supervised use. So far, it’s been very easy to “plug and play”, and my son has printed a range of plastic tat that nobody needed, and a couple of “gifts” for me, including a stand for my phone while I cook (how thoughtful!). Now that we’ve grasped the basics, the next step is for him to learn to design his own projects, which is where things become more educational. I’m also hoping we can use it to print spare parts when appliances break at home. Unlike toys that get played with a few times and forgotten, a 3D printer is a gift that can evolve with your child’s abilities and get used for years to come.

Sound choice: a sound bath voucher
Feeling yourself immersed in the deep vibrations of a singing bowl isn’t just a pleasant acoustic experience. Sound baths – when you listen, usually while lying down, to acoustic instruments –are deeply, deeply relaxing. Studies have found the experience helps reduce feelings of anxiety and tension, and there’s also evidence they can improve both sleep and cognitive function. Sound baths are a meditative practice and that, for me, is where the true benefits lie. Silent meditation is difficult, and it’s hard not to grow frustrated with myself for my wandering thoughts and inability to focus on my breath. The beautiful music of sound baths provides an external, soothing distraction. They provide a space to pause and allow to-do lists to gently fade away. There are both private and group-class sound baths, either of which would make a thoughtful gift as a shared activity or simply a new year’s reset.
Sound bath vouchers, from £25 (A few options in London, Brighton & Manchester)

Disaster ready: a guide to rebuilding civilisation
A regular topic of conversation when I get together with my wider family is how we would survive in the event of an apocalypse, and who would contribute what skills. With my husband an engineer, my brother-in-law a doctor and my mum a brilliant gardener, we think we’d do pretty well, but I’ve decided the key thing I’d contribute is my copy of The Book. Subtitled “the ultimate guide to rebuilding civilization”, it covers everything from making fire and beer to leathercraft and traps. Running to 400 pages, it was written with the scientific and technical advice of Lewis Dartnell at the University of Westminster, UK, and would be a fun and practical gift for anyone in your life who is eyeing the future with trepidation.
The Book, £99

Grow your own: a mushroom-growing kit
It is widely accepted that growing your own fruit and vegetables is not only mentally rewarding, but that the food tastes especially good when the gap between picking it and eating it is minimised. But, I thought, what about mushrooms? To find out, I grew my own mushrooms with foolproof growing kits from Merryhill Mushrooms. You really don’t have to do much other than keep their box humid; within a couple of weeks, the mushrooms had emerged from the mycelial mass, and I had picked and eaten them. Mushroom steaks and “pulled shroom” curry. Delicious!

Green pampering: innovative body oil
Vegetable oils are in almost everything, from beauty products and sandwiches to even pet food. But producing them requires a huge amount of land, energy and water. Clean Food Group is an exciting British company working to change that, using precision fermentation to produce oils from yeast in vats. It estimates its CleanOil product offers a 90 per cent cut to carbon emissions compared with using palm oil. Scottish beauty company Raven Beauty became the first company to use CleanOil in consumer products this year, releasing a facial cleanser in September, which I road-tested for a New Scientist video. Now Raven has released a range of body oils also featuring CleanOil, which would make a great gift for someone who deserves a bit of pampering. Plus, I think this small company deserves a cheer for being one of the first in line for pioneering green technology.
Body oil, £38

Pinning you down: Your life in data
We may be used to talking about how many times around the sun we have travelled each birthday. But for the inquisitive-minded, there’s more detail to be had. Have you stopped to consider, for instance, how many volcanoes have erupted since the day you were born, or how many times your heart has beaten? How many dinosaurs were named in that time, or elements discovered?
This is the premise behind Days of You, a beautiful and personalised hardback book, packed full of data specifically tailored to a specific person. All you have to do is provide the name, date of birth and place of birth of your special someone, and the team behind Days of You (which, incidentally, is the brainchild of former New Scientist staffer Matt Walker) pulls together stats and data ranging from the shape of the moon on the birthday in question to what the price of tea and the best-selling album were that year. This is essentially a beautifully visualised piece of data journalism, where the subject is your lucky recipient.
It may sound simple, but much of the data is hard to come by – especially from some of the weather stations – and must be tailored to the birth location in question. For that reason, the book is only available for those born in the US and the UK.
Books usually take 5-6 working days from order to door, the team says, but to be sure, they recommend ordering by 10 December. They also make personalised calendars, mugs, and prints for new babies.
The Days of You, £49

Distraction blocker: a Brick tile
Teenagers usually come in for the most scrutiny over phone use, but the truth is we could all do with cutting back on our screen time, no matter our age. Have a chat with your nearest and dearest about their New Year’s resolutions and see if reducing their phone use comes up. If it does, a Brick might be the perfect gift this Christmas. It’s a small magnetic tile, and when you tap your phone against it, a pre-selected group of apps is blocked from use until you tap again. I use mine to curb scrolling during work hours and reduce my phone use around my children at the weekend. I’ve found the extra effort it takes to go and find the Brick to tap and unlock your apps has been enough to rewire my behaviour pretty significantly. Brick has also just launched Brick Night, which blocks selected apps from being used when you enter your bedroom – perfect for preventing doomscrolling before bed.
Brick, £49, Brick Night, £119
