Copper wire replacement caused air traffic control issue that grounded flights across U.S.: White House
The Latest
- The White House on Saturday shared more detail on the issue that took down air traffic control equipment on Friday, grounding flights nationwide for hours.
- Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy said a contractor was doing routine maintenance to upgrade copper wire to fibre optic cable when something went wrong, causing an air traffic control communications outage.
- He said the investigation will determine whether the fault was due to "incompetence" or "a faulty router." The contractor doing the work said it was "supporting" that investigation.
- Airlines across Canada and the United States are working to catch up on thousands of disrupted flights, with Air Canada's cancellations on Friday alone having affected 27,000 passengers.
Updates
- November 14 Some answers, but disruptions continue Sarah Petz The ripple effects of Friday's nationwide flight shutdown in the United States continued to be felt today as U.S. officials shared some insight this morning on what led to the ground stop that cancelled thousands of flights and left passengers stranded. U.S. Transportation Secretary Sean Duffy told reporters the issue originated from a single router at a remote FAA location where a contractor was replacing old copper wire with fibre. Duffy said that work somehow caused the system to overload. "This was an FAA system that didn't meet the moment, our contractor didn't meet the moment or the equipment didn't meet the moment," he said. That work was part of a modernization project for the country's air traffic control system being undertaken by L3Harris, which said it was working with the FAA in its investigation into the ground stop. Though U.S. officials said the worst of the air travel disruption was over, flight data showed airlines were still recovering from the nation's longest ground stop in 25 years. By midday Saturday, close to 18 per cent of departing flights in the U.S. had been cancelled, or about 4,100 flights, according to data from aviation analytics firm Cirium. In Canada, airports warned of continued delays and urged travellers to check their flight status before heading to the airport. Some passengers at Toronto's Pearson airport this afternoon were also waiting on hold while trying to reconfigure their upended travel plans, as headaches continued nearly a day after flights across the U.S. were grounded. At the same news conference with Duffy, FAA administrator Bryan Bedford said the U.S. air travel schedule should be back to normal by the end of Sunday.
- Trump has yet to publicly comment Sarah Petz Meanwhile, U.S. President Donald Trump has yet to publicly comment on what was the longest ground stop since the Sept. 11, 2001, terrorist attacks. Duffy said he had repeatedly spoken with Trump since the ground stop began, however, and Fox News reported on speaking with the president via a phone call Friday night. But Trump did take to his Truth Social website to comment on the state of a golf course in Washington, D.C.
- Some relief, some stress for passengers in Toronto Jennifer Yoon At a dining area in Toronto Pearson’s Terminal 1 you can hear passengers on the phone, on hold with airlines, as they try to reconfigure travel plans that were mangled by Friday’s hourslong ground stop in the U.S. While some are stuck on hold, others have been able to get through and find a way to get where they were hoping to go, albeit through tangled routes. Rena Persofsky had her flight to Boca Raton, Fla., via Fort Lauderdale cancelled. After hours on the phone with Air Canada representatives last night, she says she’s been rebooked on a route to Montreal, then Fort Lauderdale, then finally Boca Raton. Persofsky said it's been an incredibly stressful experience. "I just got back from Japan, so I was exhausted, jet lagged, I had had a bad fall. So I was like, 'This can't be happening.'" She said her rebooked flight takes off later today. “Now I’m relieved, and I’m hopeful that everything goes OK," she said. Other passengers said the explanation for yesterday's ground stop from U.S. authorities hasn't inspired confidence. "I think their response — 'the equipment is old' — is not going to reassure anybody," said Anne Murray, who was travelling to Fort Lauderdale.
- More than 4,000 U.S. flights cancelled so far today Sarah Petz As for the U.S., close to 18 per cent of departing flights have been cancelled so far today, according to data from aviation analytics firm Cirium. That works out to around 4,100 cancelled flights as of about 12:30 p.m. ET, up from 3,200 this morning. Looking at international flights to the U.S. today, 64 of the 360 that were scheduled to depart from Canada have been cancelled thus far. Air Canada said it cancelled approximately 250 flights yesterday, affecting close to 27,000 passengers. "This was a significant disruption, and its effects have carried over into today, with additional cancellations as aircraft and crews are out of position across our network," said spokesperson Yasmine Mehdi, who noted that returning to normal "will take some time." Mehdi said the airline has added extra flights where possible to help alleviate the situation.
- Dozens of flights still cancelled at Toronto's Pearson airport, but backlog is improving Jennifer Yoon CBC's Jennifer Yoon speaks to one of thousands of Canadian passengers affected by the U.S. ground stop at Toronto Pearson Airport. Here at Toronto Pearson airport's Terminal 1, travellers are checking in bags and rushing to get through security lines. For all intents and purposes, it looks like a regular day at Pearson, despite the massive air traffic control communications outage in the U.S. yesterday. The only hints that you see on the surface: a few cancellations on the board for destinations like Chicago and Denver. A Pearson airport spokesperson said 63 U.S. flights were cancelled today — an improvement from the 143 U.S. flights cancelled yesterday. Air Canada, which had cancelled all flights to and from the U.S. on Friday, said it is gradually resuming operations, but the impact on flights will continue through Saturday.
- Contractor has been working on upgrades linked to outage for months Sarah Petz During today's news conference, Duffy said the issue that led to the ground stop was due to work being done by a contractor for L3Harris as part of a nationwide effort to replace old copper wire with fibre at FAA facilities. On its website, L3Harris says it completed these upgrades at 4,400 sites, or 70 per cent of FAA facilities, across the U.S. as of Nov. 2. The sites include air route traffic control centres to remote radar sites and weather stations, the website said. A news release from May marking the halfway point toward the completion of the modernization project said the project would not only replace aging infrastructure but enable "advanced capabilities" such as real-time weather data sharing, cloud-based information management and enhanced surveillance systems. The telecommunications modernization is expected to be complete sometime next year, the statement said. In a post to X on Saturday, the company said it is working with the FAA in its investigation into the ground stop. "L3Harris takes the nationwide ground stop and its impact on travelers, airlines and air traffic operations seriously," the post says.
- No data on passenger medical issues during ground stop Sarah Petz Responding to one of the last questions of the news conference, Duffy said he didn't have any data or reports of passengers who had medical issues as a result of the ground stop. But he added that airport staff "rallied in those moments yesterday to accommodate passengers." "We don't want to leave passengers on airplanes," he said. Duffy ended his remarks with a final appeal for more money from Congress, referring to funding as a "great end-of-the-year Christmas present for the American people." He also said his team will continue to provide updates.
- There is more copper wire to swap out, secretary says Anya Zoledziowski When asked whether the FAA has to swap out copper wire elsewhere, Duffy said yes. About 75 per cent of the system has been transitioned to fibre so far, he said. "Is it disruptive? Yes," Duffy said. "People may not get to a wedding. They might not get to a birthday … [but] I want them to get home safely."
- Updating America's aerospace infrastructure will cost billions, Duffy says Sarah Petz Duffy says he's asked for $30 billion US ($42 billion Cdn) to make the upgrades necessary to modernize FAA infrastructure. But he added that the issue goes beyond telecommunications infrastructure, saying that he wants to look at upgrades to airport infrastructure like runways and air traffic control towers as well. "We have aging infrastructure throughout the airspace," he said. "It's not a cheap proposition." As mentioned earlier, Congress has been controlled by Republicans — Duffy's party — for the past two years.
- Stranded passengers will get support: FAA Anya Zoledziowski Bedford told reporters that all affected passengers in the U.S. will be supported — either by getting onto a new flight or by getting a full refund. He also said passengers should reach out to their air carriers directly. Canadian travellers are also urged to track their flight status, and airlines in Canada have rebooked flights and even offered a one-time option to change or cancel some flights without penalty. Looking ahead, though, Bedford essentially said it's too soon to tell what the FAA can do differently next time. For now, the agency needs to figure out the "root cause" of the glitch, he said, and then they can establish what needs to happen differently.
