Manufacturers are unlikely to drop console prices until at least 2028, according to a new analysis by S&P Global Market Intelligence Kagan, which judged that planned expansions in manufacturing capacity for DRAM – a key component in gaming hardware that has rocketed in price due to the demands of the AI industry – will not come online until then.
The firm projects that DRAM capacity additions will reach 184,000 300mm-equivalent wafers by the end of this year and 434,000 by the end of 2027, before nearly doubling to 819,000 in 2028. At that point, supply might catch up with demand and prices may drop accordingly – although that's far from certain. 2028 is merely "the earliest point at which launching new consoles at accessible price points looks prudent," said S&P analyst Neil Barbour, while pointing out that any improvement would rely on demand remaining constant and planned new manufacturing plants coming online on schedule.
"If data centre demand grows, then it could quickly eat into the extra supply," Barbour tells GamesIndustry.biz. "The memory [fabrication facilities] are not expanding with an eye specifically toward the consumer market. They're racking up record revenue and margins on the data centre build-out, so these supply expansions are primarily meant to feed that demand. "
"However, if the build-out plateaus for regulatory reasons or simply because the demand isn't as strong as they had anticipated, then more of that capacity could become available for traditional DRAM. What this data suggests is that the conversation doesn't really even start until 2028."
In the meantime, prices are expected to remain at least at current levels throughout 2027, although Barbour stops short of projecting further increases in that period. "I wouldn't say further price increases are guaranteed in this environment, but I certainly would not expect any price cuts in the near term."
S&P Global is projecting that global DRAM capacity will grow 82% to 3.64 million wafers per month by the end of 2030, with Samsung, SK hynix and Micron accounting for roughly 80% of that expansion.
2028 is the rumoured launch year of Project Helix, the next-generation Xbox console, which reports have suggested will come with a $1,200-$1,800 price tag. Barbour sees an increase in DRAM manufacturing capacity as an essential prerequisite for its release. "If Microsoft is suffering from supply shortages with the current Series consoles, as it has said a few times over the past year, then they will almost have to wait for the upstream component supply to shore up before they make a big push with a new console."
Xbox CEO Asha Sharma previously said the company is prioritising "efficiency and affordability alongside performance" for its next-generation console amid rising hardware costs and innovations with AI.
"With the crisis that's currently happening in hardware and components, it is absolutely something all of us need to be thinking about and innovating on and giving people more choice in terms of how they play their console and how they purchase their console."
Earlier this year, industry experts discussed the impact of AI on video game hardware, noting that major chip producers are prioritising the growing demand from AI data centres worldwide, leading to significant price rises for consumer hardware.
In June, SK Hynix, Samsung and Micron were sued over allegations of "concerted anticompetitive behaviour" in the DRAM market, with lawyers claiming the companies cut the supply of consumer DRAM in defiance of "all economic and business logic."
