Yousef Diriyeh and his son Hadi, both paramedics in the occupied West Bank, were lifting a wounded young woman into their ambulance when they heard shots crack and bullets whistle past their heads.
Less than an hour earlier, armed Israeli settlers had attacked the village of Qusra, killing three Palestinians and wounding several more. They were now shooting at the paramedics who had come to help.
Diriyeh urged his son to hurry as bullets struck the windscreen. It was the first time he and Hadi had come under attack.
As he sped to a hospital in Nablus, Diriyeh, now 50, remembers thinking to himself that the events of that day – 11 October 2023, shortly after Hamas launched the attack on Israel that triggered the Gaza war – would mark the beginning of a series of attacks on emergency responders such as him and his son.
Since 7 October 2023, Israeli soldiers and militant settlers have repeatedly attacked Palestinian paramedics and blocked them from reaching the wounded in the West Bank. Violence against healthcare workers rose by more than 350% between 2022 and 2024, according to Physicians for Human Rights Israel, a non-profit organisation which documents human rights abuse affecting the right to health in the occupied Palestinian territory.
Insecurity Insight, a Geneva-based nonprofit humanitarian research organisation, recorded 1,020 attacks on health workers in the region from 7 October 2023 to 15 September 2026.
Physicians for Human Rights Israel shared dozens of videos with the Guardian, including of the incident shown above in October 2023 in Huwara, showing militant Israeli settlers throwing stones at ambulances, using cars to block their passage, and, in some cases, shooting at them. The footage also shows damaged ambulances, bloodstains on drivers’ seats and injured paramedics. It does not include the attack on Diriyeh and his son, which was not filmed.
The Guardian joined a team of paramedics near Nablus and spent a day in their ambulance, with Diriyeh at the wheel as he recalled that moment.
“That day I realised the militant settlers had fired directly at us with the intent to kill,” said Diriyeh, a tall, broad man with a thick, greying beard, driving along Route 60 towards villages recently targetted by settlers.
Israel has retained effective control over the occupied West Bank, including its borders and the movement of people, despite the Palestinian Authority’s nominal autonomy. As an occupying power, Israel is obliged to keep civilian medical services running, yet Israeli forces and settlers systematically subject emergency workers to abuse and violence.
“Over the last two years, [attacks] have increased at an insane rate – meaning there are attacks taking place every single day,” said Diriyeh. He has been based for the last 15 years in Aqraba, a village near Nablus for the last 15 years. “Sometimes there might be three, five or even six separate attacks taking place simultaneously.”
Heading south from Nablus, the village of al-Sawiya comes into view.“It was near here that settlers killed my colleague and friend, Mohammed,” Diriyeh said, pointing towards the houses. Mohammed Awad Allah Musa, an ambulance driver for the Palestine Red Crescent Society, was killed on 21 April 2024 taking wounded Palestinians to hospital.
He was one of 13 West Bank health workers killed since 7 October 2023, according to Insecurity Insight.
In news reports of recent settler attacks, reports have regularly suggested that ambulances have been prevented from reaching injured people in the region.
Ambulance crews in the occupied West Bank depend on Israeli military approval, passing through checkpoints to reach patients and take them to hospital. By December 2025, the UN had recorded 925 obstacles in place in the occupied West Bank, including checkpoints, road gates and roadblocks.
Militant settlers have also obstructed ambulances, Diriyeh said.
“They try to prevent us from passing by driving very slowly in front of us. Sometimes, when a settler’s vehicle is ahead, I might attempt to overtake, only for another settler to block the way to stop me,” he said.
In some West Bank villages, militant settlers have locked the gates, leaving ambulances stuck at their stations and unable to answer emergency calls. Since first occupying the West Bank in 1967, Israeli forces have continued to install road gates at the entrances to some Palestinian villages.
“Sometimes they come and lock the gates in and out of Aqraba – you’d practically need a helicopter to get past them,” Diriyeh added. “Some women have even given birth inside the ambulance itself when these gates are blocked.”
Omar, a 34-year-old emergency medical worker from Nablus, told Physicians for Human Rights Israel that soldiers at a checkpoint in Awarta stopped an ambulance carrying a woman in labour.
“Traffic was backed up, and the soldiers were refusing to let anyone through,” he said. “By the time they finally let us through, there was even more traffic ahead. In the end, while we were still on the way, the woman gave birth.”
The Pulitzer prize-winning book A Day in the Life of Abed Salama follows a father’s search for his son after a school bus crash in 2012 near Jerusalem. The writer, Nathan Thrall, who is based in Jerusalem, said more than a decade later that restrictions on Palestinian movement in the West Bank had become far worse.
“For many, they are the most severe they have experienced since the occupation began,” he said. “The goal, as ever, is to constrict Palestinians into ever-tightening spaces so that their land may be taken over for Israeli settlement and de facto annexation, a project that is not only steadily succeeding but accelerating rapidly.”
Imran Anati, a researcher for Physicians for Human Rights Israel, said: “These are not isolated incidents, but a systematic assault on Palestinian emergency medical care for which Israel bears responsibility.”
The Israeli military denied allegations that it had obstructed the work of Palestinian paramedics or prevented them from reaching the wounded, saying it “enables freedom of movement for medical teams” throughout the Palestinian territory. It said it condemned all acts of violence against medical teams and ambulances.
Diriyeh’s job has become one of the most dangerous in the occupied West Bank, as settler attacks become ever more frequent. The Israeli opposition leader Gadi Eisenkot described the attacks as “Jewish terrorism”, while the UK foreign secretary, Ed Miliband, called the actions “ethnic cleansing”.
Diriyeh said he had made the “difficult decision” to ask his 20-year-old son to stop working with him.
He said on his first day of training as an ambulance driver in late 2013, the first case he had to handle involved a settler’s vehicle which had overturned on the road to Jericho.
He said Israeli emergency services couldn’t locate the vehicle, so Palestinian paramedics arrived and provided medical aid to the settlers on-site.
“In my career, I have provided medical aid to Israeli soldiers, too,” he said. “Whether they are Palestinian, Israeli, Christian, Muslim, Druze, or Jewish – it is of no concern to me. My mission is simply to provide humanitarian aid to anyone who needs it. That is my job.
“And it’s painful to think that today, a Palestinian life is worth less than an Israeli one.”
