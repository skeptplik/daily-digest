During talks on ending the war in Ukraine, Russian representatives in Abu Dhabi presented a document containing far-reaching demands, The New York Times reports.
Dated November 10, the document reportedly calls not only for an end to Western arms deliveries to Ukraine but also for NATO to withdraw its troops and bases and move them back to where they were in 1997.
What does Russia mean by 1997?
The year 1997 refers to the NATO alliance before its first eastward expansion, according to the Kremlin. At the time, NATO had 16 members. Poland, the Czech Republic and Hungary had already been invited to join but did not formally become members of the defense alliance until two years later. In the following years, more countries in Central, Eastern and Southeastern Europe joined, followed most recently by countries in Scandinavia. Today, NATO has 32 member states in Europe and North America.
According to the report, Russia is not explicitly demanding that Central and Eastern European countries leave , but rather that the NATO alliance end its military presence in those countries.
What would be the consequences of a NATO withdrawal?
A withdrawal of NATO troops and bases from member states that joined the alliance after 1997 would significantly weaken their defense capabilities. For example, since 2004, NATO allies, including Germany, have taken turns policing the airspace over Estonia, Latvia and Lithuania.
Because the Baltic states do not have fighter jets of their own for independent air policing, NATO provides the capability. Since 2026, the mission has been part of a broader air defense presence. The planned deployment of the German Lithuania Brigade, which is expected to be fully operational by the end of 2027, would also have to be halted.
In Romania, meanwhile, the Mihail Kogalniceanu Air Base near Constanța on the Black Sea is currently being expanded. Once completed, it is set to become Europe's largest NATO air base by area — even larger than Ramstein in Germany.
"Under no circumstances will we allow anyone to establish new security rules for this part of the world over the heads of Poland and Europe," Polish Prime Minister Donald Tusk told TVP World. His Bulgarian counterpart, Rumen Radev, called a NATO withdrawal "unthinkable and unrealistic." No country should be forced by external pressure to reverse its decision on which alliance to join.
Estonian Prime Minister Kristen Michal also rejected any Russian say in changes to Europe's security order: "We have nothing to discuss with Russia about Europe's security order; it is working well."
The strong rejection from the capitals of NATO's eastern members, however, masks fears along the alliance's eastern flank that a deal between Washington and Moscow could turn their security into a bargaining chip.
Why isn't the mutual-defense clause enough?
In the event of an armed attack on a NATO member, the other alliance members are obligated under Article 5 to come to its aid. Each member decides for itself what measures it considers necessary, which may include the use of military force.
For the mutual defense commitment to deter a potential aggressor, that country must believe NATO can intervene militarily quickly and effectively in a crisis. The deployment of allied troops along the eastern flank serves precisely this purpose. A withdrawal would make defense more difficult and could lead Russia to believe that an attack on NATO territory would involve fewer risks for Moscow.
Who decides which alliances a country can join?
As part of their security framework, European countries have agreed that states are free to decide which alliances they wish to join. NATO points to Article 10 of its founding treaty, which enshrines its open-door policy for European countries. The 1997 NATO-Russia Founding Act also explicitly stated that neither side had the right to veto the other's decisions.
Back in November 1990, the participating states of the Conference on Security and Cooperation in Europe (CSCE), including the Soviet Union, had already agreed in the Charter of Paris to respect each country's freedom to choose its own security arrangements.
Was there a promise not to expand NATO eastward?
During the negotiations surrounding German reunification, then-US Secretary of State James Baker and German Foreign Minister Hans-Dietrich Genscher used language that suggested putting limits on NATO's eastward expansion.
Their remarks came during discussions about unified Germany's membership in NATO and the possible deployment of NATO forces on the territory of the former East Germany. However, no written, legally binding general promise was ever agreed that NATO would never expand to include other former Warsaw Pact countries.
In the 1997 NATO-Russia Founding Act, both sides declared that they no longer considered each other adversaries. NATO stated that, under the security conditions prevailing at the time and in the foreseeable future, it intended to ensure its collective defense through reinforcement rather than the additional permanent stationing of "substantial combat forces." The agreement explicitly gave Moscow no veto over NATO decisions.
In the 1994 Budapest Memorandum, Russia, the United States and the United Kingdom reaffirmed their commitment to respect Ukraine's independence and existing borders and to refrain from the use of force. These assurances were linked to Ukraine's decision to give up its nuclear weapons. However, the memorandum did not oblige the United States or the United Kingdom to defend Ukraine militarily in the event of an attack. NATO membership was not addressed at the time.
Why is the demand back on the table?
Just over two months before Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine, the Kremlin presented two draft treaties to the United States and NATO. In them, Russia demanded an end to further NATO enlargement. The draft agreement with NATO also proposed that Russia and NATO members that had already joined the alliance as of May 27, 1997, refrain from deploying additional troops or weapons on the territory of other European countries beyond the levels stationed there on that date.
Security guarantees for the postwar period and a future security framework for Europe were also on the agenda in Abu Dhabi. Negotiating NATO troop deployments and alliance enlargement alongside an end to the war in Ukraine would give Moscow additional leverage. Russia has long maintained that the "root causes" of the war must be addressed. The Kremlin includes NATO enlargement and the alliance's military presence in Eastern Europe among those causes.
So far, no significant Western player has expressed willingness to accept Russia's demands in Abu Dhabi. On the contrary, former Polish intelligence coordinator Stanislaw Zaryn viewed the talks as an attempt to buy time and weaken the West ahead of another confrontation.
