Israeli settler violence at unprecedented levels during Palestinian olive harvest
Critics say Netanyahu’s far-right government is letting it happen
"It’s so random and doesn’t have any rules. You don’t know where it could come from, or in which intensity. Which weapon, how many?"
Wala Zoran is talking about attacks by Israeli settlers in the occupied West Bank during the annual olive harvest.
We met her on Oct. 11 while she was standing with a group of fellow Palestinians, warily watching some settlers standing alongside Israeli soldiers on the other side of an olive grove in the village of Abu Mash’al. One settler had a rifle slung across his back.
Zoran and her family, including her elderly grandparents, were attacked by settlers in the grove the day before while trying to harvest their olives.
"They started throwing big stones at us and we realized that we can’t take the olives — we can’t take anything, actually," she said. "We started running, but they were so close. Everybody was hit with the stones, like my father, my sister. I was. My cousin was shot in the leg, actually, and had to spend the night in hospital."
Wala Zoran and her family were attacked by settlers on Oct. 10, while trying to harvest their olives in the West Bank village of Abu Mash’al. (CBC)
This despite their efforts to avoid a confrontation.
There have been more settler attacks against Palestinians and their property during this year’s olive harvest than in 20 years of record-keeping by the United Nations.
Like many Palestinians, Zoran’s family has changed how they harvest in a bid to stay safe. They try to gather their olives in quick bursts, not waiting until the big tarpaulins they put on the ground to catch the olives are full before ferrying them back home.
They also wait to begin picking until other families from the village are able to join.
But safety in numbers isn’t working.
Israeli soldiers and settlers are seen side by side in Abu Mash’al in the Occupied West Bank. According to the United Nations, Israeli settler attacks, often with Israeli military protection, numbered close to 400 for October alone. (CBC)
'People depend on olive oil here'
Zoran lives in France but returns home to help her family with the annual harvest. She’s worried her parents will have to make another attempt this year to finish the harvest.
"People depend on olive oil here," she said. "We can’t just say, 'OK, I don’t feel like doing it this year.'"
The olive industry contributes up to 15 per cent of agricultural GDP in the Palestinian economy.
A family harvests olives in the Palestinian town of Beit Surik, on the western edge of the occupied West Bank. Some groves lie behind the separation barrier built by Israel in the early 2000s, which snakes onto Palestinian land in some parts, cutting farmers off from their trees. (CBC)
On the other side of the family's olive grove, the IDF soldiers eventually left after lobbing tear gas into the trees where a few Palestinians had remained.
The settlers then gathered up the olives left behind and drove off with them in the direction of what’s known as an outpost.
All Israeli settlements in the occupied territories, including East Jerusalem, are illegal under international law. Israel continues to expand them while also tolerating — and often facilitating — a growing number of outposts, small camps from which Jewish extremists try to push Palestinians from their land. Even Israel considers them illegal, at least on paper.
More than 180 outposts have been created since 2023, according to the Israeli settlement watch group Peace Now, many of them close to Palestinian population centres.
Political analyst and researcher Ibrahim Rabaya’a says it’s one reason violence is surging during this year’s harvest. He describes the speed with which the outposts are spreading as a "strategic escalation" by settlers emboldened by Israeli Prime Minister Benjamin Netanhayu and his far-right government.
"We are talking about … a very organized process, very organized players," said Rabaya’a during an interview at his home in Ramallah. "[The settlers] are well-trained. They are working in general as an army, militias."
'Every single day we have attacks'
It's especially striking how emboldened the settlers are compared to even a year ago — whether in groups of two or three harassing Palestinian herders and their livestock or forming battle lines in large numbers, masked and terrifying.
CBC News witnessed the latter while visiting Abu Falah, one of a cluster of villages in the central West Bank, on Oct. 13.
We arrived just a few hours after settlers had descended from a hilltop outpost above the town and taken axes to dozens of olive trees.
The residents had blocked the road leading from the base of the hill into the town with heavy stones to stop the settlers from going any further by vehicle.
When the Palestinians led our news crew to see the damaged trees on foot, the settlers spotted us and started back down the hill, some on foot, running at full speed with sticks and slingshots. Others were driven down in the backs of white pickup trucks. They were masked and dressed in black.
When the call went out to Abu Falah’s residents to defend their groves, they picked up rocks of their own on the way, moving through the olive trees to the outer perimeter, later firing firecrackers at the settlers as they advanced.
"Every single day we have attacks and every single day we’re out in the field defending people," said Diyaa Murra, a university student volunteering with the Palestinian Red Crescent.
Palestinian emergency services are under increased pressure during the harvest, with the Israeli NGO Yesh Din charting more than 25 settler incidents a day during the harvest, from vandalism to physical assault.
Ambulances try to position themselves in areas where repeat attacks take place to avoid being delayed by Israeli military checkpoints or imposed closures.
"I think the only way to defend ourselves from the settlers is to engage with everybody in defending the village," said Murra, who was born in Abu Falah. "So I decided to work with the Red Crescent to be part of the defence system. That’s the only way."
The melee continued in Abu Falah until the Israeli Defence Forces (IDF) arrived, firing tear gas canisters in the direction of the Palestinians retreating from the bottom of the hill.
A few days later, the settlers attacked Abu Falah again, making international headlines after a U.S. photojournalist for the Washington Post named Heidi Levine was severely beaten by settlers who surrounded her.
Violence affects livelihood
Before we left Abu Falah on the day we visited, we counted at least 40 settlers involved in the attack clearly visible and back up on top of the hill.
Yet we witnessed only one arrest: a Palestinian man handcuffed and blindfolded as Israeli soldiers led him toward a military vehicle.
Later, we learned the father of five had been held for three days and fined for throwing stones.
The Palestinians say they have little else with which to defend themselves.
"[Palestinians know] most of the settlers are protected by the army," said Mohammad Ahmad, a Palestinian American waiting for the olives he managed to collect to be processed at a mill in Silwad, a town about 10 kilometres from Abu Falah.
"Even the young settlers [… ] under the age of 18 and 17 years old, they have weapons, you know."
Olives picked in the West Bank are processed at a mill in Silwad, a town about 10 kilometres from Abu Falah. (CBC)
Ahmad says this season’s violence is impacting livelihoods and yields, including his own.
"I have never experienced or felt the way we’re feeling right now, because of the settlers and because of what’s going on," he said. "And we’re not allowed to reach most of our lands."
Tensions with IDF
Many Palestinians are required to co-ordinate access to their own olive trees with the IDF, depending on where they’re located.
On Tuesday, the IDF issued a statement on social media saying that 90 per cent of what it called "co-ordinated harvests" had been completed. It also denied claims that security forces were deliberately preventing the olive harvest.
The Palestinian Farmers Union responded, saying that as of late October, 55 per cent of the overall olive crop "remained inaccessible or unharvested."
Israeli forces arrived early in the morning of October 21st, keeping the Palestinian villagers of Deir Jarir from reaching their olive groves. (CBC)
Israeli security forces can often declare areas of confrontation between Palestinians and settlers closed military zones.
That’s what happened when our crew arrived in Deir Jarir, another town in the central West Bank, on Oct. 21.
After settlers ambushed an initial attempt to harvest olives, the Palestinians invited Israeli and international activists to accompany them on a second try, hoping they might offer protection.
But when they arrived at the muster point carrying ladders and olive rakes, the villagers found an armoured vehicle and a handful of IDF soldiers blocking the road.
"They are telling us that our farms are closed military zones and we cannot leave, and we’re stuck here," 72-year-old Abu Asif told CBC News.
The IDF has said its security forces operate to "prevent friction and violence, enabling residents to harvest safely."
In some instances, Israeli soldiers themselves have uprooted entire olive groves, saying they could be used by Palestinians to hide in and as a launching point for attacks.
Palestinians say it’s simply one more way Israel is trying to remove them from their land.
"Our life under occupation has always been difficult," said Abu Asif. "But this time, this season, it’s the most difficult and the hardest."
'The Palestinians are defenceless'
Prime Minister Netanyahu has dismissed the settler attacks as the work of a "small minority."
But Israeli lawyer and peace activist Daniel Seideman says that what’s happening in the West Bank couldn’t be done without Netanyahu's "knowledge, consent and blessing."
"The Palestinians are defenceless," he said in an interview at his office in Jerusalem. "The military is either in bed with the settlers, doing the settlers' bidding or turning a blind eye and saying, 'This isn’t our responsibility.'"
"It’s clear that the settlers see this as a once-in-a-lifetime opportunity to ethnically cleanse the West Bank," Seideman said, adding that he didn’t use the term lightly.
Seideman spoke to CBC News ahead of last month’s Israeli election, which many believed would see Netanyahu’s tenure come to an end. At the time of publication, it's still unclear who will ultimately form a government.
Analyst Ibrahim Rabaya’a says while a change in government could rein in some of the settler violence, few Palestinians believe it would slow the de facto annexation of the West Bank.
"It is also a strategy for other political players in Israel, but they try to do so without bringing anger from international society," he said. "What we can [call] silent annexation."
Political analyst Ibrahim Rabaya’a says the proliferation of settler outposts in the West Bank is one reason violence is surging during this year’s olive harvest. (CBC)
None of the other main Jewish political parties are on the record supporting a Palestinian state, although Yair Golan’s Democrats have indicated they would potentially support it in the future.
The olive harvest is a favourite target for Israeli hard-liners, precisely because it is such a potent cultural symbol of Palestinians' connection to the land.
Israeli flags everywhere
Rabaya’a says that in addition to the daily hardships of living under occupation, the settlers have been trying to increase psychological pressure on Palestinians.
Over the past several months, Israeli flags have started appearing along the main road from Ramallah up to the northern cities of Nablus and Jenin.
And along that road in the town of Luban al Sharqiya, settlers arrived one night in October to completely swamp a Palestinian roadside café with Israeli flags.
The owner, Muayad Hussein, who lives with his family in a house just behind the café, says that when he heard them, he was too afraid to come out.
He showed CBC News the images captured by his security camera: dozens of young Jewish extremists pounding the flags into the ground. An Israeli police car can be seen parked on the other side of the road. Someone hands out drinks as they work.
Hussein told us one of the settlers from a nearby outpost warned him that if he took the flags down, they would come back.
He’s afraid they will. So the flags remain.
Muayad Hussein, who owns a roadside café in the town of Luban al Sharqiya, looks at the Israeli flags that were hammered into the ground outside his shop by a group of settlers. (CBC)
