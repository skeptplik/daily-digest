Democrat Scott Colom wins election to US Senate from Mississippi
The Associated Press now projects that Democrat Scott Colom has been elected to the US Senate to represent Mississippi, defeating the Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith.
The AP made the call after Colom’s lead expanded to 4,669 votes on Wednesday.
Colom will be Mississippi’s first Black senator since 1881, when Blanche Kelso Bruce, who was born into slavery, served during the post-civil war Reconstruction era. In 1874, Bruce was elected by Mississippi’s state legislature to serve in the US Senate, a seat he held from 1875 to 1881.
Colom, a local prosecutor, waged a savvy campaign to take advantage of Donald Trump’s deep unpopularity, making a viral campaign ad that focused on the high price of beef and even talking to Trump supporters waiting on line before a rally the president held for Hyde-Smith in Mississippi the week before the election.
After Hyde-Smith appeared on stage withTrump, Colom’s campaign posted video of her praising the unpopular president with the theme music from Curb Your Enthusiasm added to the clip.
We are standing by for Scott Colom’s victory speech, but a cheer just went up from the crowd as his family appeared and filed into their seats.
Among those present are his father, Wilbur Colom, a lawyer and former Republican who served on Ronald Reagan’s presidential transition team and ran as a Republican for Mississippi state treasurer in 1987, before leaving the party two decades ago to become a Democrat. His mother, Dorothy Colom, is a retired judge and the first Black person elected to a Chancery Court judgeship in their part of the state.
In 1987, Will Colom told the Washington Post that he one day hoped to be the state’s governor. “I really believe Mississippi is going to be the first state to elect a black governor,” he said then. “It might as well be Will Colom.”
The senator-elect’s daughters, who starred in several of his campaign ads, started the ceremony by leading the pledge of allegiance.
Scott Colom’s election party may have been delayed, but it wasn’t denied. About a thousand attendees are gathered on the Woodworth Chapel Lawn at Tougaloo College, a historic black college in Jackson.
It’s a jubilant, cookout like experience. A DJ is playing classic southern line dance tunes and much of the crowd is obliging, dancing while they wait for Colom to arrive. Others are hugging. Most are smiling.
Babies and toddlers are here, arriving in strollers or on their parents shoulders. Elders who participated in the Civil Rights movement and who remember just how hard the fight to secure the Voting Rights Act was are eagerly awaiting the arrival of the Senator elect.
It’s a historic moment, Maxine Bolden, a Tougaloo alum who was born in the 60s said. Her parents were active in the Civil Rights movement. While they’re no longer alive, Bolden said that she knows this moment is important to them too.
Democrat Scott Colom, who became the first Black US senator ever directly chosen by Mississippi voters on Wednesday, is scheduled to speak at a victory celebration at 8pm Central Time at Tougaloo College in Jackson.
A live feed from the campus, where a soundcheck is underway, shows that Colom will be speaking from behind a sign that reads “Picked Another Senator”, a reference to his wildly successful viral campaign ad.

Mississippi today has the highest share of Black voters in the United States, at close to 40% of the electorate.
In a statement on Colom’s historic victory, the president of the NAACP, Derrick Johnson, pointed to the obstacles Black voters in Mississippi had to overcome to cast their ballots and have them counted.
“Mississippi voters made history because they refused to be intimidated, discouraged, or counted out. The NAACP stood by voters as they registered, they organized, they stood in long lines, and they voted. In a state that has witnessed some of the nation’s most difficult battles for voting rights, Black voters once again demonstrated the power of civic engagement and the strength of our democracy.
“Even in the face of efforts to remove voters from the rolls, attempts to intimidate voters and election officials, and bids to create fear around the electoral process, Mississippi voters showed up. They showed up for their communities, for their families, and for the future of their state. They reminded the nation that we the people have the power.”
The viral ads that propelled Scott Colom to victory
As the Guardian reported last week, Democrat Scott Colom’s Senate campaign was propelled, in part, by a series of viral ads on social media that defined his opponent, Republican incumbent Cindy Hyde-Smith, as out of touch with the impact of Donald Trump’s unpopular policies on the lives of Mississippi voters.
One ad, released in mid-October, racked up more than 20 million views across social networks. It seized on a remark by Hyde-Smith, who tried to dodge a question from a TV anchor about the high price of beef by saying: “You have so many proteins to choose from”.
The ad, which begins with that gaffe, then cuts to three Mississippi voters responding to the senator. “Pick another protein?” a man grilling burgers says. “Shit, I’d rather pick another senator instead.”
“Pick another protein?” a woman eating says next. “She done lost her damn mind. I’d rather pick another senator.”
“Pick another protein?” a woman in a grocery store says. “What the f-”.
According to Colom, the idea for the ad came from Patrick Gaspard, a former US ambassador to South Africa and adviser to president Barack Obama and it was directed and edited by Ryan Sayles. Sayles, Colom wrote this week, “should be considered the breakout-star of this cycle. He’s going to be a giant in the media consultant game. He did GAME CHANGING work with SO little.”
Another comedic ad, a Halloween parody of the film Sinners called Sinators, was written and directed by Colom’s twin brother, Andrew Colom, who also starred in it along with the candidate.
At the end of the campaign, Colom starred in a much more serious ad, showing the candidate at the Mississippi home where the civil rights activist Medgar Evers was murdered in 1963 for registering Black voters.
“I’ve been a prosecutor for 10 years and unfortunately I’ve stood in a lot of houses where something terrible happened,” Colom says in the ad. “This isn’t any house. This isn’t any tragedy. This is where Medger Evers died. He fought for this country and Normandy. He came home to Mississippi and they wouldn’t let him vote. So he organized, helping other people register.”
“He was killed for helping Mississippian vote,” Colom says at the end of the ad. “On Tuesday, use your vote and honor everyone who died for that right.”
That ad was scripted by Arun Chaudhary, a filmmaker who worked on Barack Obama’s 2008 presidential campaign, then served as the official White House videographer during Obama’s first term and later become the creative director for the 2016 Bernie Sanders presidential campaign.
Several of Colom’s ads also gave starring roles to his wife and two daughters.
It is perhaps not incidental that the most recent video on Hyde-Smith’s YouTube channel was posted in October 2020 and the page’s title still use the logo from her previous campaign. Hyde-Smith won that higher-turnout election in a presidential year with 709,511 votes. This year she received 468,667 votes.
Scott Colom makes history as first Black US senator ever chosen by Mississippi voters
As our colleague Adria R Walker reports from Mississippi, the state has not elected a Democrat to the Senate since 1982 and not not since 1875 has it sent a Black senator to Washington.
As with much of American political history, both of those facts need some added context to fully understand the historic nature of a Black Democrat, Scott Colom, being elected to the US Senate to represent Mississippi on Wednesday.
To start in the more distant past, Colom will be Mississippi’s first Black senator since 1881, when Blanche Kelso Bruce, who was born into slavery, ended his single term during the post-civil war Reconstruction era.
But Bruce was elected to the US Senate not in a popular vote, but by Mississippi’s state legislature, in 1874. He then held that seat from 1875 to 1881.
The post-civil war Mississippi legislature that chose Bruce was dominated by Republicans, then the anti-slavery party that had been led through the war by Abraham Lincoln, the first Republican president of the United States.
Over a century later, the last Democrat to win election to the US Senate from Mississippi was John Stennis, who won a seventh term in 1982. But Stennis, a white segregationist, was a member of a very different Democratic party whose leaders in the Senate and elsewhere fought a long battle to deny voting rights and equality to Black Americans.
In the decades since, many white southerners who started their careers as Democrats defected to the Republican party as the two parties realigned.
Cindy Hyde-Smith, the Republican incumbent Colom just defeated, was first elected to local office as a Democrat, before leaving the party to become a Republican in 2010.
Cindy Hyde-Smith concedes Mississippi Senate race to Scott Colom
In a video statement posted on social media on Wednesday, the Mississippi Republican senator Cindy Hyde-Smith acknowledged that she has lost her seat to Democrat Scott Colom.
“While this is not the outcome we worked for or hoped for, I respect the decision of Mississippi voters,” Hyde-Smith said. “I want to congratulate Scott Colom and wish him well as he takes on the tremendous responsibility of representing our great state.”
The ad is simple, less than half a minute long, but many say it was the moment the nation took notice of Mississippi’s US Senate race.
In it, the incumbent Republican senator Cindy Hyde-Smith, a cattle farmer, answers a reporter’s question about the rising price of beef: “You know, you have so many proteins to choose from,” she says.
The ad then cuts to a man flipping burgers on a grill. “Pick another protein?” he says. “Shhiiiiii – I’d rather pick another senator instead.”
The viral ad was for Hyde-Smith’s Democratic challenger, Scott Colom, who won the Senate seat on Wednesday, becoming the first Democratic senator in Mississippi since 1982 and its first Black senator since the Reconstruction era. His ads were relevant to Mississippians and created memorable slogans – an early ad highlighted the history and progress of the state, while a more recent one spoofed the hit film Sinners, filmed in Mississippi. But his efforts to engage Mississippians didn’t start there.
Colom spent the past year reaching Mississippi residents where they were. From driving a “pick another protein” truck that offered free tacos, to talking to students and alums at college homecomings, to chatting with voters queued to see Donald Trump, Colom campaigned on an idea that’s not novel to Mississippians, but one that the rest of the country is only starting to fully realize: “Mississippi Matters.”
And he did it with little help from the Democratic party.

While Mississippi has no state law mandating a recount, Colom now leads, with 49.2% of the vote to Hyde-Smith’s48.7%, which would put his lead above the half-percentage-point margin that triggers an automatic machine recount in other states, like Florida.
According to the Mississippi Free Press, a non-profit newsroom in the state, Mississippi’s largest county, Hinds county, reported on Wednesday that it had completed its review of so-called affidavit ballots, which were those with some sort of technical error. The county accepted 817 affidavit ballots, but rejected 1,609 others, a rejection rate that is higher than most counties in the state.
The rejection of so many ballots for minor, technical flaws frustrated many Democrats, including Brandon Presley, the Democratic nominee for governor in 2023 and a second-cousin of a late Mississippi musician some readers might be familiar with.
“Watching provisional ballot after provisional ballot get thrown out because the POLL WORKER, not the voter didn’t initial the ballot makes me sick,” Presley posted on social media on Tuesday.“In those cases, the voter did nothing wrong and was GIVEN the ballot by a POLL WORKER, the affidavit is signed by a POLL WORKER. That’s a preponderance of evidence it’s a legit vote.”
On Wednesday, as Colom’s lead expanded, Presley urged the AP to “call the race” because “this is done, done, done. To delay this call only erodes credibility.”
