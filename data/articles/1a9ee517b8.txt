New figures show that children of Polish nationals account for the largest share of Germany’s child benefit payments abroad, fuelling calls from some politicians to restrict payments to families living outside the country.
Kindergeld is the German child benefit paid by the Familienkasse (German Family Benefits Office) to parents who work or are self-employed in Germany. In simple terms, it can be seen as the equivalent of Poland's 800+, since both benefits support families raising children and, under European Union rules, are treated as the same type of benefit.
In 2026 Kindergeld amounts to 259 euro per month for every child, that is around 1 140 zł.
The data requested from the Federal Employment Agency (Bundesagentur für Arbeit - BA) by the Sojusz Sahry Wagenknecht (BSW), and first reported by "Bild", show that at the end of 2025 Kindergeld was being paid for 291 292 children living abroad. According to BA figures, this number almost tripled in 14 years: in 2011 there were 103 559 such children, and in 2015 there were 158 169.
Children living abroad account for about 1.66 percent of all 17.6 million children for whom Germany pays child benefit.
The figures show that for 112 384 of the 291 292 children living outside Germany, the parent entitled to receive Kindergeld is a Polish citizen. That is almost 39 percent of children living outside Germany. The second largest group is made up of 36 727 children of German citizens, which may include, for example, families where one parent works abroad. According to "Die Welt", next come citizens of the Czech Republic (33 872) and Romania (32 459).
Kindergeld paid into foreign bank accounts
According to the German portal Merkur.de, transfers of Kindergeld to foreign bank accounts have also clearly increased. In 2025 about 528 million euro was paid into such accounts, compared with just 45.7 million euro in 2011, more than eleven times less. However, these amounts cannot simply be equated with benefits for children living abroad. Kindergeld for such a child can be paid into a German account, while families with children in Germany can in turn use accounts abroad. The level of payments is also affected by increases in the benefit itself, so the rise in transfers does not allow the conclusion that the number of claimants has grown in proportion.
Why does Germany pay for children abroad?
Payments are based, among other things, on European rules on the coordination of social security benefits. Under these rules Germany may be the competent state for family benefits even when the child lives in another EU country. The right to Kindergeld stems from the parent working in Germany, not from where the child lives. A typical case is a parent working in Germany while the family still lives in the country of origin, for example Poland.
If several states are responsible for family benefits, priority rules apply which take account, among other things, of where the parents work and where the child lives. In certain circumstances Germany therefore pays not the full amount but only the difference compared with the lower benefit of the other state. A child living abroad does not therefore mean that the payment is unjustified. The statistics also do not show how often cross-border payments are abused.
Poles working in Germany can receive the benefit because the German child benefit is payable regardless of where the child lives. Receiving Poland's 800+ does not prevent the German benefit from being granted, but it has to be declared in the application. The application is submitted after working in Germany for at least four months.
German politicians are looking into the issue
German media report that politicians from the CDU/CSU and AfD are "deeply concerned" about the transfer of benefits from the German budget abroad. They acknowledge that the payments are for children of parents working in Germany, but point to the difference in living costs.
Günter Krings, deputy chair of the CDU/CSU group in the Bundestag, said that "Germany is the main exporter of child benefits to other EU countries".
He said: "The cost of living in Germany is significantly higher than in the countries where the children have remained. EU law currently forces us to pay the same child benefit for a child living abroad as for a child living in the country, provided the parents work here, completely ignoring the fact that the cost of living here is far higher than in many of those countries (...). The father works in Germany, his children live in Poland, Romania or the Czech Republic and receive German child benefit. Yet the current 259 euro per month for the first child is much less than, for example, in Timișoara. On average the cost of living in Romania is about 40 percent lower than in Germany".
The founder of BSW, Sahra Wagenknecht, goes furthest, calling for Kindergeld in principle no longer to be paid for children living outside Germany. In an interview with "Bild" she criticised the fact that the state transfers money abroad while people in Germany depend on food banks (Tafeln).
She wrote on X: "In Bitterfeld people are queuing up at food banks, and taxpayers' money is going to Bucharest. The German state is being plucked like a Christmas goose. Politicians need to think more about Bitterfeld again than about Bucharest. There must be no more benefits for children who do not live in Germany at all!"
AfD, for its part, has submitted a motion in the Bundestag to tie child benefit in EU countries to local living costs.
René Springer, a Bundestag member for AfD, said: "It is unfair that we are transferring child benefits at the full rate to foreign accounts. Romania pays 58 euro per child. We transfer 250 euro. Do these children really exist? Nobody checks that. For nine years we have been hearing that this is impossible. Now the issue has made it into an action plan, but only as a vague promise that we will one day ask the EU about it. The European Union will not represent our interests. We have to do that ourselves. When we are in government, we will reduce child benefits. And if the EU says this will not pass and sues us, we will simply pay less. We are the number one net contributor. This is a policy guided by the interests of German taxpayers".
CJEU: adjusting benefits is contrary to EU law
Such a rule is not easy to introduce at national level. European provisions on the coordination of family benefits oblige member states to recognise cross-border claims under certain conditions, so abolishing benefits across the board would require changes at European level.
Attempts have already been made to restrict the benefit. A few years ago the governing coalition announced a plan to limit Kindergeld for children living abroad, but this would have required amending EU rules, which prohibit such a move.
Austria provides an example. From 1 January 2019 Vienna adjusted benefits to the cost of living in the child's country of residence, and in June 2022 the Court of Justice of the EU found this to be incompatible with EU law (source in Polish). According to the Court, this amounted to indirect discrimination on grounds of nationality, without objective justification. The ruling was criticised by, among others, CDU MEP Daniel Caspary, who argued that Kindergeld is not part of remuneration but a state social benefit intended to support children where they live. In 2026 the Court also ruled that linking the Bavarian family allowance to the child's country of residence is contrary to EU law.
One percent of spending on children abroad
Parsa Marvi of the SPD took a different view. In an interview with "Die Welt" she recalled that equal treatment in relation to family benefits is an important element of the free movement of workers in the EU. She said: "Those who work here and help finance our society should not be discriminated against simply because of where their children live".
Janine Wissler, deputy chair of the Left party's parliamentary group, drew attention to the scale of the phenomenon: "We are talking about roughly one percent of total spending on child benefits".
The agency's figures show a similar proportion. In 2025 a total of 55.32 billion euro in Kindergeld was paid out, so the 528 million euro transferred to foreign accounts accounted for about 0.95 percent of all spending.
