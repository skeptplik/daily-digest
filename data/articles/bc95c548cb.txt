MILAN – It’s quite a challenge not to notice Daiana Giorgi.
Her outfits pique interest for their tasteful combination of vintage pieces and unexpected accessories. That same curation marks Frida — The Future of the Past, the venture she launched during COVID-19 after spending nearly a decade at Prada, mainly as retail merchandising manager helping the brand’s store openings in Switzerland and, prior to that, at Swiss retailer Bongénie.
She poured her expertise in luxury retail, eye for product and her personal fascination with archival fashion into Frida, the solo project named after her grandmother that’s devoted to vintage finds across fashion, accessories, design and art.
After pop-ups at Rinascente in Milan, Turin and Rome and a temporary rooftop concept in Milan, Frida opened its first stand-alone boutique in Saint Moritz in 2024, followed by an outpost in Cernobbio on Lake Como in 2025.
Now it’s setting down roots in Milan, where Giorgi quietly opened a three-level unit in an intimate courtyard in Via Manzoni 23, a few steps away from the 19th-century Grand Hotel et de Milan and Armani hotels.
Offering WWD a preview of the space ahead the official ribbon-cutting on Dec. 2, Giorgi underscored how the city has always played a key role in her life, during both her fashion studies and her time at Prada.
“It’s a city I adore, and for me it’s very important to open Frida in the places where I like to go,” she said.
Set within a historic Milanese building, the 538-square-foot store is intentionally tucked away from the buzz of the shopping street, reflecting both the city’s understated nature and the brand’s ethos grounded in reaffirming the pleasure of discovery.
“Frida is all about the sense of curiosity and surprise, which I think are very much missing nowadays because we’re being bombed with images and information all day long,” said Giorgi, who wanted Frida to be “a place you discover rather than simply come across.”
The building’s decorative arches and cobblestones contrast with the store’s contemporary interior, which was designed by acclaimed architectural duo Ludovica Serafini and Roberto Palomba. The concept hinges on an interplay of materials, from resin flooring and subtly textured walls to satin-finished steel and wood, passing through glass elements and the dark veined marble that defines the staircase connecting the different levels of the intimate space.
“Everything you see here is for sale,” said Giorgi, as she underscored her mission of bringing together rare and one-of-a-kind finds from around the world. These include furniture, like a Japanese wooden cabinet hailing from the 1920s dominating the ground floor; retro displays spotlighting ceramics or jewelry, such as a statement necklace by artist Nag Arnoldi, as well as artworks like the bronze sculptures by Spanish artist Miguel Berrocal that hide rings among their modular constructions.
While Giorgi’s intention is to dedicate the first level of the unit to gatherings and in-store activations in a bid to foster the local community around Frida, overall the location will be reimagined every two to three weeks, giving clients a reason to return and to refresh their feeling of discovering.
The fashion selection will be a main driver. Giorgi focuses on garments tracing back “mainly to everything that happened before 2000,” with the core spanning from the ‘70s to the ‘90s.
Established brands — spanning from Chanel and Hermès to Marni and Vivienne Westwood — mingle with pieces that come with no label.
“Of course I have my favorite brands, like Prada, not surprisingly,” Giorgi said. “Then there are brands like Chanel that we know customers grow attached to. But there are also unbranded, bespoke pieces like this one,” she said while showing an ankle-length skirt in chunky wool embellished by a fringed hem. “It gives me goosebumps because it makes you realize there were so many people who could have been famous fashion designers but lived in different times. And yet they created such an incredible, mind-blowing work.”
Asked to elaborate on her scouting process, Giorgi said that “it’s all about the garment itself — what it elicits in me. It could be a specific detail or a type of fabric, it could truly be many different things.”
“In the beginning, it was mostly about me searching and finding pieces while traveling to Amsterdam, London or Paris. But with time it has evolved into something truly wonderful, because a community of clients has gathered around Frida, and they have become also our suppliers,” she continued. “It’s really a circular process. I’ve got to know people who have incredible personal collections, not just of clothing but of art and design, too. They invite me into their homes and let me take items they no longer want, perhaps because they’re looking for a change in their own houses.
“So these days we have so many people reaching out to us: They see what we do and they like it, because they can truly see that we add value to these garments and accessories. We show and photograph them in a way that’s completely contemporary and modern, they don’t look like old things at all,” Giorgi said.
Her distinctive styling and sophisticated combinations in presenting the pieces and suggesting outfit solutions have to be credited, too. Such a curation is also what sets the offering of each Frida store apart. “That’s the fun part: I have a wintery location, a more summery one on the lake and now an urban space. Depending on where I am, there’s a different result [in the styling]. And that’s the beauty of it: We create like small capsule collections,” she said.
To be sure, Giorgi has a past as a stylist. She spent years ping-ponging between New York and Los Angeles — giving acting a try in between, as well.
“It was my dream, so I took the leap. New York is the city where you can try to do 10,000 different things because it never sleeps — and I didn’t sleep that much in those years, indeed. But it was such an essential school for me,” she said.
The city sparked her passion for vintage, as Giorgi started collecting unique finds while living there at the age of 19. “Vintage stores were not as popular as now here, but in the U.S. they were way more common,” she recalled. “Yet I don’t see Frida as an alternative to another vintage store, more as a venture with its own identity. I’d like customers to think about shopping here as like going to Miu Miu or Antonia,” added Giorgi, teasing that after Milan she aims to have an outpost in New York.
Across its locations, Frida attracts an international audience of discerning women, mainly hailing from the worlds of art and design and who “are not after logos, but rather seek for uniqueness. They appreciate more a piece they know they are the only ones to have.”
That’s the trick to the overall boom in the popularity of vintage and secondhand stores and platforms across generations, according to Giorgi. “We are increasingly surrounded by the same things, all accessible in an instant. But I think now people want to reclaim their own personality and individuality. And this is especially true for young generations….That’s why we can see that even major brands are digging into their archives,” she concluded.
