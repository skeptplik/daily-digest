If two chromosomes are better than one, why not three? Wouldn’t this give a better chance of surviving environmental change?
Hazel Russman
London, UK
Living creatures with three sets of chromosomes do exist and are known as triploids. However, they are sterile because of the way that chromosomes behave during the formation of gametes (ova and sperm).
During this process, matching chromosomes pair up and exchange genetic material, then separate again. Eventually, gametes with a single set of chromosomes are produced. Because of the gene exchange that took place earlier, each gamete is unique, with a different combination of gene variants (alleles). This is good for providing raw material for natural selection, but it would obviously not work with an odd number of chromosome sets.
Among plants, tetraploids with four sets of chromosomes and hexaploids with six do exist. Bread wheat is a hexaploid. As long as the chromosome number is even, the system works as described.
RÓisín Long
Trinity College Dublin, Ireland
Humans usually have two copies of each autosomal (i.e., non-sex) chromosome, and so two copies of each gene. This has many benefits. If one gene copy has a disease-causing mutation, the other can sometimes buffer this. Further, during meiosis (the process by which sex cells with one copy of each chromosome are created), genetic material is shuffled between chromosomal pairs, promoting genetic variation and facilitating environmental adaptation. So, would having more chromosomes be even better?
Having full extra sets of chromosomes is termed “polyploidy”. Many lineages across animals, plants and fungi experienced polyploidy in their evolutionary history, before returning to having two chromosome sets, and some species are currently polyploid. These polyploidy events correlate with times of environmental stress, and so polyploidy probably conferred an immediate survival advantage under specific conditions. Extra genetic material also presents the opportunity for novelties to evolve over time. However, polyploidy rarely becomes established in lineages, and has many immediate disadvantages.
Polyploidy disrupts cell size and the ratio of surface area to volume, disturbing regular cellular functioning. The significant increase in genomic material interferes with usual cellular processes and gene expression. Polyploidy can also disrupt even division of chromosomes into sex cells during meiosis. While polyploidy is more common in plants, it is rarely observed in animals. Polyploidy is a common cause of miscarriage in humans.
Further, having extra copies of individual chromosomes (termed “trisomy”) alters balanced gene expression and development and also often results in miscarriage. Down’s syndrome (trisomy of chromosome 21) is the most common autosomal trisomy among live births, one reason for this being that fewer extra gene copies are present on this small chromosome.
So, while there can be advantages to having three or more chromosome copies, the consequences at the gene- expression, cellular and organismal level are complex.
