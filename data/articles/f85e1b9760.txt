When Diego started seeing bright flashing lights in his field of vision and felt his legs go weak, he thought he knew immediately what was happening. His mother had a stroke in her 60s, and although Diego was active and (unlike his mother) didn’t smoke, he had always expected that the same would happen to him. So it was a huge relief to be told – after seeing a stroke specialist and having a brain scan – that he had not had a stroke at all. Unfortunately the relief was short-lived. The visual disturbances and the weakness were real and debilitating. If this wasn’t a stroke, what was it?
Some illnesses aren’t caused by disease or damage, but arise when the coordinated functioning of the body goes awry. In a percentage of these cases a person’s psychological health is an important element in their symptoms. Intuitively this seems mysterious. We are accustomed to thinking about physical symptoms as being caused by physical events; viruses, inflammation, bleeding. How can something as intangible as a thought have any impact on something as physical as a leg? How can the mind make us ill?
The work of Freud still dominates the public and medical imagination here, even though there are important ways in which he was wrong. Freud proposed that quite dramatic physical symptoms can arise as a result of repression, where the memory of an upsetting experience is pushed into the unconscious, leaving a sort of free-floating emotional energy. He saw the mind as being like a pneumatic device: emotions represented energy and that energy had to go somewhere, often leading to the development of a physical symptom.
These days, psychologists are more sceptical about this, but the influence of Freudian psychology lives on in the way we talk about psychosomatic illness – a condition where there’s no damage or disease, but clear impairment nonetheless. The terms “conversion” and “somatisation” are derived directly from Freud and still pop up frequently in the literature.
Their use is a stark reminder of the fact that, when it comes to explanations, our language can lead us astray. Importantly, the words conversion and somatisation strongly imply an active, even deliberate, process; that the patient with a psychosomatic illness is doing something that brings their symptoms about. They are converting their distress into paralysis, or somatising their sadness into pain. Is that how things really are?
There is still much to be explained about how, in the absence of damage or disease, a body can produce a symptom as dramatic as paralysis or a seizure-like episode. But we are beginning to understand what seems to be important in getting these experiences going.
First, remember that our bodies often respond to acute distress in dramatic ways. Strong emotion can entail so called tonic immobility, in which motor areas in the brain are inhibited, and voluntary movement prevented. More familiarly, it can induce trembling, speechlessness and confusion, all of which resemble neurological symptoms. Our perception of these experiences is important. Recognising strong emotion for what it is makes it more manageable. If I tremble while understanding that I am afraid, I might give myself time for the fear to pass and my nervous system to settle down.
But when these feelings are taken as a potential sign of disease, we tend to become anxious, with consequences for how we experience and attend to our bodies. Fear increases pain, so any feeling that there is bodily damage (a frightening idea) can be enough to turn up the perceptual volume on physical discomfort or unusual sensations. Paying attention to and trying to control a shaking limb will tend to increase the trembling; attending to a difficulty with movement is likely to increase how severe we think the problem is. What might have started small becomes amplified.
People vary in terms of how easy they find it to identify emotions. The term “alexithymia” (translatable as something like “no words for emotions”) refers to a difficulty with labelling and distinguishing feelings. It can range from a near full-on emotion “blindness” to a coarser grain of emotional experience, with distinctions only between, say, feeling “good” and feeling “bad”. It is not uncommon for people with psychosomatic illnesses to report that they struggle to identify emotional states.
What this could point to is that psychosomatic illnesses arise not because of what a patient is doing with their emotions, but because of what they have not been able to do with them. When we label our emotions, when we allow them to find their expression, we are allowing ourselves to know more fully what we are feeling and what we need. In a clear departure from the Freud-inspired model, it’s not that patients with these problems are “converting” their emotions into something else. It’s that they aren’t always registering them as emotions in the first place. Instead, they’re interpreted as something having gone wrong with the body, beginning a vicious cycle of anxious attention.
This is still an uncertain area of clinical practice. Emotion is not the whole picture, but it is important for understanding why some people find themselves with otherwise unexplained symptoms. For example, one way of thinking about non-epileptic seizures (a deeply distressing and disabling experience with all the hallmarks of a seizure, but without any of the expected electrical anomalies in the brain) is that they might start off with physiological elements of a panic attack – the muscular tension and the involuntary movement – but without the accompanying subjective feeling of dread; “panic without panic”.
Diego found it helpful to consider that his symptoms could be rooted in bodily expressions of anxiety and physical overload. He was a determined and upbeat guy who had taken on a second and third job to support his children financially. He had studiously avoided engaging with the possibility that this might be getting to be too much. His symptoms showed him his limits.
Psychosomatic illness is sometimes dismissed as being “all in the mind”, but this gets things back to front. When our emotions get wrapped up with physical symptoms, they are very real – “all in the body”, in fact. Bringing our emotions into our minds by thinking about them, and heeding the needs they represent, can be an important step in finding relief.
