Abdulhadi Dalhatu is something of a rarity in Nigeria politics.
At 38, he is among the young people seeking elected office in a country where about 80% of the population is under 40.
He hopes his age will stand in his favour as he campaigns for a seat in the state assembly in the northern Kano state in January's general elections.
"Younger people are the majority in this country and should be deciding the future," Dalhatu tells the BBC.
But Nigeria's political history suggests he faces an uphill battle.
Since the return to civilian rule in 1999, Goodluck Jonathan has been the youngest person elected president, taking office at the age of 53.
The current president, Bola Tinubu, is 74 and would be 79 by the end of a second term if he is re-elected in January.
His main challengers are also considerably older. Atiku Abubakar, seeking the presidency for the seventh time, is 79, while Peter Obi is 65.
Of the 18 presidential candidates, only one is under 40 - Moses Olusoji Adebisi, a 35-year-old businessman representing the Democratic Leadership Alliance (DLA).
Although he says he is confident of winning, few Nigerians appear to know much about him and he has not been seen much on the campaign trail.
The contrast is striking in a country with one of the world's youngest populations, where young people have repeatedly demonstrated their ability to shape political debate and mobilise for change.
In the previous election, Obi, then 61, galvanised millions of young Nigerians through a social media-driven movement known as the "Obedients".
His campaign promised a break from the established political order, appealing particularly to young people struggling to find jobs and earn a decent living.
But his failure to win the presidency left many of his supporters disillusioned.
"At the time we were so hopeful, but unfortunately the result wasn't what we wanted, which made many lose hope in the system," former Obedient Nonso Chukwudi tells the BBC.
The movement had built on the momentum of the EndSars protests in 2020, which grew into one of Nigeria's biggest youth-led movement in decades.
Initially sparked by anger over police brutality, the demonstrators developed into a wider call for political reform, accountability and greater youth participation in governance.
But the protests were eventually crushed by the authorities. About 56 were reportedly killed, although officials dispute this figure, while hundreds were arrested and some had their bank accounts frozen.
Both EndSars and the Obedients demonstrated the capacity of young Nigerians to mobile around political causes.
But turning that enthusiasm into sustained electoral participation has proved much harder.
Hajara Musa, 33, is among those who have lost faith in the political system. She has no plans to register or vote in the near future.
"I just feel like things are already determined and I'll just be wasting my time. It is going to be the same people all over again," she tells the BBC.
The electoral register appears to reflect this growing disillusionment.
Although the number of registered voters has increased by 10 million to over 103 million since the last election, enrolment of those under 35 has actually fallen, by about seven million.
Young people now account for just 29% of registered voters, down from 40% in 2023.
Nigeria's electoral commission, Inec, says it has made efforts to encourage more young people to register ahead of the 2027 general elections.
This includes appointing nine ambassadors from the entertainment industry, spanning film, music and comedy, to reach younger audiences.
But the figures suggest these efforts have yet to make a significant difference.
Dalhatu says he understands the frustration but argues that young Nigerians cannot expect change, if they stay away from the political process.
"Unfortunately, many prefer to just post on social media" instead of getting involved, he says.
Of course being young does not mean you are necessarily the best candidate.
For those who want to participate, there is a formidable obstacle to getting into politics - money.
Dalhatu dreams of becoming president one day, but says he has previously had to abandon campaigns because he could not raise enough funds.
The cost of running for office in Nigeria is notoriously high.
Under the Electoral Act, presidential candidates were allowed to spend up to 5bn naira (Â£2.8m; $3.7m) on campaigns ahead of the 2023 elections. That spending limit has since been doubled.
The cost of nomination forms alone puts the presidency beyond the reach of most aspiring young Nigerians.
The ruling All Progressive Congress (APC), along with the Peoples Democratic Party (PDP) and the African Democratic Congress (ADC) have each set their presidential nomination fee at 100m naira ($74,000; Â£56,000), making it extremely hard for new challengers to emerge.
Since the return to democracy in 1999, only two parties have governed Nigeria - the PDP from 1999 to 2015 and the APC since 2015.
These parties, alongside the ADC, formed last year by politicians who had defected from the other two, have established nationwide network of branches, supporters and campaigners.
Those aspiring candidates without the financial resources to secure nomination forms and compete in the internal party contests of the main parties have little chances of becoming president.
The barriers are also evident in the National Assembly.
Not one of Nigeria's 109 senators is under 40, while only 14 of the 360 members of the House of Representatives fall into that age group.
"I have been in politics for many years and I will tell you that young people in this country lack the resources to become president," says Dalhatu, who is running under Obi's Nigeria Democratic Congress.
Fatima Zahra Umar, a young politician in the APC who has previously tried to run for a seat in the House of Representatives, agrees.
"Young Nigerians are poor. Nigerian politics is cash-intensive, so participation is guided by money," she says, adding that young people are often content with having a bit of influence as a special advisor to a senior politician.
But political analyst Seun Ajayi believes the problems go beyond the financial barriers.
"We have some of the most brilliant young persons in the world, but they are either not interested in politics or feel satisfied when given a small role in government," he says.
"The old men are not going to sit idle and allow us to push them out. We have to fight for the big positions, but at the moment I don't see the zeal."
Umar believes changing attitudes towards young leadership is equally important.
"Culturally, we are not primed to trust or accept the brilliance of a younger person, so it will be very difficult," she says.
"Old men are still holding on to the system."
Some Nigerians took inspiration from the wave of success of young candidates in the recent midterm elections in the US, some of whom have roots in Africa.
"America Has Done It, Can Nigerians Do the Same?" entertainer Charly Boy wrote on X, in a post that was widely shared.
He said the US results served as a warning to Nigerian politicians that no matter how powerful the political machine, voters can change the direction of government.
He called on young people to interrogate candidates about their past promises: "Has life improved? Where are the jobs, security and affordable living?
"Democracy is not a spectator sport, nor is it about sharing rice," he wrote.
