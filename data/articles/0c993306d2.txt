El Niño is getting some monster-sized nicknames. But is the weather actually getting more extreme?
Scientists weigh in on the increasingly dramatic labels and what this powerful El Niño could mean for Canada
Godzilla has battled giant moths and three-headed dragons. But even the king of the kaiju might have trouble keeping up with this year's El Niño.
First came "Super El Niño." Then "Godzilla El Niño." Now there's a new monster in town: "Mega El Niño." At this rate, King Ghidorah may be next.
But has this year's El Niño really been upgraded?
"As far as I can tell there's no reason to invent a new category above 'very strong' that we have right now," said Emily Becker, a research associate professor of atmospheric sciences at the University of Miami's Rosenstiel School.
But David Phillips, a climatologist emeritus with Environment and Climate Change Canada (ECCC), was more enthusiastic, given the unprecedented nature of this El Niño.
"Maybe 'Godzilla' is not strong enough for it."

What's behind the 'mega' headlines?
El Niño is a climate phenomenon that occurs when unusually warm water in the central and eastern tropical Pacific alters atmospheric circulation patterns, changing the weather around the world, according to ECCC.
Scientists measure the strength of an El Niño by averaging the sea surface temperature over three months in a large section of the Pacific Ocean, as opposed to measuring extreme temperatures in localized areas.
The latest name gained attention after U.S. meteorologist Ryan Maue posted a map on X on Sunday showing what he said were sea surface temperatures as much as 10 C above normal near Peru and the Galápagos Islands.
"This is now a Mega El Niño," he wrote.
But the U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA)'s analysis doesn't show temperatures that high at the ocean's surface, said Michelle L'Heureux, its El Niño team lead and a physical scientist with the U.S. National Weather Service.
Instead, it shows temperature anomalies of roughly 4 C to 6 C in the eastern Pacific, she said.
NOAA hasn't changed its terminology for classifying El Niño events, said meteorologist Erica Cei, a National Weather Service spokesperson.
The agency's Climate Prediction Center simply classifies El Niño events as weak, moderate, strong or very strong.
NOAA's Nov. 12 update reported extremely high sea surface temperatures in parts of the eastern Pacific. However, isolated temperature readings do not necessarily reflect the overall intensity of an El Niño.
Instead, NOAA uses the Relative Oceanic Niño Index (RONI) to measure average sea surface temperature anomalies in the central tropical Pacific over a three-month period, while accounting for warming across other tropical oceans. By that measure, this year's El Niño has reached NOAA's highest classification: very strong.
"This event is running on par with our previous very strong events," Becker said, referring to the El Niños of 1997-'98 and 2015-'16.
While "mega El Niño" is not an official category, the term has appeared in the scientific literature. A 2023 peer-reviewed study used it to describe the very strong El Niño events of 1982-'83, 1997-'98 and 2015-'16.
Is this El Niño really exceptional?
There is no question this is an unusually powerful event.
NOAA's Nov. 12 forecast gave it roughly a 60 per cent chance of surpassing the strength of previous El Niños dating back to 1950.
But that remains a forecast, not a record already broken.
Mandy Freund, a climate science lecturer at the University of Melbourne who has written about "Godzilla El Niño," said some ocean temperatures recorded near South America are unprecedented.
She also defended the dramatic terminology.
"I believe if the language serves the purpose of raising awareness of a dangerous situation, that’s acceptable," she said in an email to CBC News.
But Freund said making bold predictions before forecasts become reliable could undermine public confidence.
Becker also cautioned against assuming that an exceptionally strong El Niño will always bring extreme weather: "We don't necessarily expect to see extreme impacts from an extreme El Niño."

What could a very strong El Niño mean for Canada?
Western and central Canada typically experience a milder winter during El Niño years, but impacts can be quite variable.
ECCC's Nov. 1 seasonal forecast also suggests that much of Eastern Canada could experience above-normal temperatures this winter.
Phillips said the current season could be compared with the very mild winter Canada experienced in 2023-'24. But even with very strong El Niño conditions, cold spells and snowstorms are possible.
An area of great uncertainty this winter is the polar vortex, a large circulation of winds in the upper atmosphere over the Arctic. When it is disrupted and weakens, cold Arctic air can push down into Canada and the United States.
Phillips compared this coming winter to a clash straight out of a Hollywood blockbuster: "It's the Godzilla El Niño meeting the frigid polar vortex," he said.
The lack of winter snow and moisture could increase the risk of spring drought and lead to an earlier start to the wildfire season, Phillips said.
Is climate change making El Niños more powerful?
It is still unclear whether human-caused climate change is making powerful El Niños more frequent or intense.
Canada's Changing Climate Report 2026 finds, with medium confidence, that year-to-year climate fluctuations associated with El Niño have been larger since 1950 than in the previous century, but expresses low confidence that the change is due to human activity. This is because El Niño itself is highly variable, making it difficult to separate natural fluctuations from the effects of climate change.
There is no evidence to suggest that exceptionally powerful El Niños are increasing in frequency, Becker said.
However, global warming can make the effects of a strong El Niño even worse because warmer air holds more moisture and can cause more rainfall in regions that are already wet during an El Niño year.
Phillips called the combination of a powerful El Niño and a warming planet a "double whammy."
So while there is no official classification for a "mega" El Niño, the El Niño monster is out there somewhere.
