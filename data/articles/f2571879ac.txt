Nvidia and its co-founder Jensen Huang have been at the centre of the artificial intelligence (AI) spending boom.
No company or chief executive has arguably been more consequential to the development of AI in recent years, with the US microchip manufacturer under Huang positioning itself as a critical cog in the development of the world-changing technology.
That partnership has now ended following Huang's resignation over revelations of extramarital affairs, one of which is alleged to have been with a Chinese spy.
The company has been rocked by the scandal, with hundreds of billions of dollars wiped off its stock market value and questions swirling about the extent of the impact of Huang's departure.
While Nvidia is not as much of a household name as other Big Tech players such as Apple, Amazon and Microsoft, the company has been just as integral to the booming US stock market of recent years.
The billions of dollars being funnelled into AI and the construction of data centres has bolstered the American economy, with Nvidia being one of the biggest spenders.
Originally focusing on chips which process graphics for computer games, it pivoted to making the chips used in AI systems and now dominates the market.
It is now one the most valuable firms, worth $3.8 trillion (Â£2.9 trillion), and on Tuesday said its rapid growth is poised to continue as the firm's quarterly sales doubled from a year earlier.
But there's now an air of uncertainty over Nvidia's future direction. Huang played a major role in the company, leading its shift towards AI, and maintained a firm grip on the company, with up to 200 people directly reporting to him.
Dan Coatsworth, head of markets at investment firm AJ Bell, says because Huang was also a key figure in "flying the flag" to drum up enthusiasm and investment for the wider AI industry, his departure posed a "real risk" that Nvidia could now decide to change its current strategy and rein in spending.
Such a move could spell a wider slowdown in AI investment, which would have an economic impact. How big? That's "unknown", Coatsworth says.
"If they decide to go with the much more cautious route... there could be a knock-on effect in that you have got various companies in the AI infrastructure space suddenly finding they don't have a key source of finance," he says.
Kunjan Sobhani, senior technology industry analyst at Bloomberg Intelligence, expects the impact of Huang leaving to be "limited" for now as Nvidia has strong commitments and a deep order book. But he said this picture could change in the long run.
Spider's web of financing
That is because Nvidia, on its journey to the top of the AI industry, has created a spider's web of deals through what is known as "circular financing".
Not only does it sell microchips to its customers, which include the likes of Meta, SpaceX and OpenAI, it has also provided funds for them and other firms in the supply chain by either handing out loans or investing in them directly.
The practice has prompted concerns among some investors, who worry what might happen if Nvidia were to turn off the spending taps. If investment dried up, would other firms in the supply chain cut spending or jobs themselves? Would that spread to the wider US economy?
That risk is perhaps why tech bosses including Elon Musk and Tim Sweeney, the CEO of Fortnite maker Epic Games, have called for Huang's reinstatement.
Musk claimed that "critical deals" involving Nvidia's technology would be lost following Huang's resignation, which has also prompted a campaign by shareholders and supporters to "Bring Jensen Back".
Huang himself has said he is open to a return, just days after resigning.
Coatsworth says he has never seen circular financing used at this scale before, but admits there are competitive rivals around, which could insulate the wider AI industry from any potential slowdown at the firm.
When Nvidia's share price plunged in the wake of the affair revelations, the stock of its rival semiconductor makers including AMD, Broadcom and Marvell Technology jumped.
"I don't think all AI activity grinds to a halt if Nvidia slows down what it's doing and if it pulls some financing, but it does change how people would look at the whole situation," Coatsworth adds.
'No CEO is irreplaceable'
The risk of a slowdown has already rattled stock markets. Investors took fright at Democrat gains in the midterm elections, with many having campaigned for tougher regulation of AI firms.
Questions also remain about whether the huge sums being invested in AI will actually pay off.
Investment in AI is expected to exceed $1 trillion in 2026, according to investment bank Goldman Sachs, who said the technology would remain the "dominant driver of business investment growth" next year.
Nvidia's interim boss Colette Kress said it was "business as usual" without Huang at the helm as the company's rapid growth remains on track.
The value of the shares in Big Tech companies have cushioned the US economy from global economic shocks, including tariffs, buoying investments and pensions for people worldwide.
Despite Huang saying he would be "honoured" to return, Nvidia's board has said it is not considering bringing him back.
And while Coatsworth thinks "no CEO is irreplaceable", he admits the pressure is on for whoever succeeds him.
