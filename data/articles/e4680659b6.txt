Vladyslav Vlasiuk, Ukraine's sanctions commissioner, voiced disappointment over the decision to leave Irish-made alumina out of the latest EU sanctions package against Russia.
Ukraine has urged the European Union to be bolder in its sanctions against Russia and target sectors that remain shielded by political sensitivities, such as Rosatom, alumina and maritime services.
It comes as Moscow intensifies its deadly airstrikes against Ukrainian cities and energy systems to make the winter unlivable.
"There are plenty of ideas," Vladyslav Vlasiuk, Ukraine's presidential commissioner for sanctions policy, told Europe Today on Tuesday.
"We may be facing some difficulties with having those ideas accepted by the majority of member states."
Vlasiuk welcomed the European Commission's proposal for the 22nd sanctions package, which targets banks, crypto platforms, soldiers and athletes, but said more is needed to bring Russian President Vladimir Putin to the negotiating table.
"We expect to maximise the economic pressure on him. So at some point, maybe, that will lead to the result of him being forced to the negotiations," he said.
Ukraine has long pushed for sanctions on Rosatom, Moscow's state-owned nuclear giant, but resistance from member states that operate nuclear energy plants with Russian-made parts has left the sector untouched.
Meanwhile, a full ban on maritime services for oil tankers is stuck in political limbo due to objections from Greece, Malta and Cyprus.
Kyiv has also set its sights on alumina, the white powder used to make aluminium, a lightweight metal commonly found on the battlefield.
Earlier this year, a media consortium exposed the links between Aughinish Alumina, a sprawling refinery in western Ireland, and Russia's military-industrial complex. Under mounting pressure to cease the exports, Dublin launched a government probe.
The investigation ended in a grey area: it did not find evidence to either confirm or refute a direct link between Irish-made alumina and Russian weapons.
Instead of an export ban, as Kyiv demanded, Brussels has reportedly proposed a mechanism to monitor and map Aughinish's alumina sales.
"Certainly, I am disappointed," Vlasiuk said, calling the exclusion a "clear example" of political sensitivities obstructing a "rightful measure".
"This is not exactly about just Ukraine. This is about alumina being exported from an EU member state to Russia, where it is being used for the production of weapons. And then you'll be facing these weapons all across different regions."
Vlasiuk referred to last week's incident in Poland, when two missiles and one jet drone crashed in Poland amid a major Russian attack on Ukraine.
"Be sure that some good part of Irish alumina was used to produce those exact cruise missiles," he said.
But the issue goes beyond alumina.
Despite 21 sanctions packages, and another on its way, European-made components continue to find their way into Russian weapons, exposing a dangerous loophole.
"That is a really big question for all the Ukrainian people. We certainly don't like being hit with these Russian UAVs and missiles with European parts inside," Vlasiuk said.
"It's less than it used to be in the first years of the full-scale invasion, but it's still happening," he added.
"We've been approaching a lot of the law enforcement organisations of the EU member states and, most importantly, the manufacturers themselves. I think that they certainly can do more."
