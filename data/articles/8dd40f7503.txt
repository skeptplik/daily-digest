Public health specialist discusses the aftermath of the munitions detonation in Iowa
NPR's A Martinez speaks with public health specialist Will Sander of the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign about the aftermath of the Iowa munitions detonation.
A MARTÍNEZ, HOST:
When the Army destroyed its stockpile of lead azide on Wednesday, Linda Ross heard and felt the explosion.
(SOUNDBITE OF EXPLOSION)
MARTÍNEZ: She lives about a mile from the Iowa Army Ammunition Plant in Middletown.
LINDA ROSS: And then the dogs started barking. We heard, you know, like, things on the window panes just kind of rattle. And that was it. And it seemed a little anticlimactic.
MARTÍNEZ: FEMA calls its work to contain the detonation one of the most complex mitigation efforts in history. Even so, as we heard earlier, the government is still testing. So could the impacts be detected later?
We're going to ask Will Sander that question. He's an associate professor of preventative medicine and public health at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign. So, Will, the EPA says lead leaks were not detected. If we're having this conversation weeks from now, could that statement still be true?
WILL SANDER: I think the air statement may still be true, but we may still find additional detections, particularly as you start doing more testing in the soil and water around the area.
MARTÍNEZ: OK. So many of the mitigation measures used by the Army were only implemented, really, after a legal challenge and public outcry. So do those measures make a difference to the outcome, you think?
SANDER: I do think so. I think that the groundswell that happened over the last week and a half certainly helped move the needle forward and provide more mitigation in place.
MARTÍNEZ: Now, FEMA lifted the shelter-in-place order last night, saying that people can resume their normal activities. I have trouble thinking that I would be able to resume my normal activities after this, but is there anything residents nearby should still be mindful of while they're resuming their normal activities?
SANDER: I think they should still be looking around and making sure that if there's any dust or accumulation of materials, as recommended before the blast, that they're washing that off, as well, and then obviously paying close attention to what the results come in over the next few days.
MARTÍNEZ: If you happened to live there, Will, would you be back right away? Would you stay away? What would you do?
SANDER: I mean, I think I would probably want to stay away a little bit longer until I got some more confirmatory testing that everything was all clear. But it certainly sounds encouraging at this point.
MARTÍNEZ: If you had to go back, would you do something like wear a mask or wear maybe a full-on bodysuit, something like that?
SANDER: I mean, I'd certainly want to be staying inside for another day or so, predominantly, and if I had to go outside, probably still wearing a mask. I think after this next 24 hours or so, I would be less worried about inhaling any sort of material that might be in the air.
MARTÍNEZ: If there was, say, like, some kind of weather, like rain or something like that, would that affect maybe any lead that still remains - anything, like, with the environment that would happen if, say, it rained or snowed there?
SANDER: Yeah. Certainly, we're doing some interesting weather in the area right now. And so I think the - actually, precipitation may help in helping to wash off some of this material, too, if there's anything on hard surfaces right now.
MARTÍNEZ: OK. So it wouldn't set things into the dirt or anything like that?
SANDER: Well, I think it's going to get into the dirt, regardless. And we already have some background lead contamination in our dirt system. And so this is not going to markedly increase that even if there was lead from this blast.
MARTÍNEZ: Is there a way to assess long-term risks at this point, or is it still too early on that?
SANDER: It's still way too early on that, I think. So we need to keep monitoring this for the next weeks to potentially months, just to ensure that we don't have any long-term impacts.
MARTÍNEZ: And is this one of those things where - because it got so much attention, are people going to be paying attention to this for years to just kind of track to see, like, what the progress has been since this explosion happened?
SANDER: I think there'll be a lot of lessons learned, for sure, both on how this was approached and communicated, but then also, you know, future efforts around mitigating some of these materials that we have in various places around the country.
MARTÍNEZ: All right. That's Will Sander, professor at the University of Illinois Urbana-Champaign. Will, thank you very much for breaking this down for us.
SANDER: Thanks, and happy Thanksgiving.
