They started the year crowned the 2026 Serpentine Pavilion architects and are ending it as trailblazer Frida Escobedo‘s pick for the practitioners who will shape the next two decades.
In this interview for Dezeen’s 20×20 series sponsored by Kohler, Lanza Atelier discuss the bathrooms that brought them together, the yin and yang of their partnership, and their hopes for the future.
The 20×20 series, launched to mark 20 years of Dezeen, is a two-stage affair: first, we rounded up 20 designers and architects who we’ve seen make a major impact over those 20 years, then we asked them to nominate the emerging practitioners that they think will make a big impact on the next 20.
With just 10 years between the start of their practices, Escobedo and Lanza Atelier represent two crests on a new wave of Mexican architecture that has brought regional references to the world stage and made adventurous but sympathetic interventions at home in Mexico City.
Escobedo was one of Lanza Atelier’s Alessandro Arienzo’s teachers at the Universidad Iberoamericana in Mexico City, and he and partner Isabel Abascal cite her as an inspiration whose success with kindred ideas has given them the confidence to pursue their vision.
They are becoming known for distilling complexity down to simple and pure gestures, whether in the appropriately snaking crinkle-crankle walls of their Serpentine Pavilion, inspired by the English countryside, their Cancun house with materials and forms drawn from sea snail shells, their Re-Source exhibition design that visitors could take home, or their hierarchy-challenging Family of 4 chairs, prompted by the arrival of their second son.
For Escobedo’s part, she identifies Arienzo and Abascal’s dedication to concept and process, which involves extensive research and testing, as key to their strength.
“Their work is poetic and intellectually rigorous,” said Escobedo. “They work seamlessly across scales, bringing together material intelligence, generosity, and a sense of play that makes their architecture both thoughtful and joyful.”
The Lanza story began in 2015, with toilets. Arienzo was approached by a client who, nearing the end of their bike path development in Mexico City, realised that they needed public bathroom facilities along the route. Arienzo suggested to Abascal, originally from Madrid, that they work on together on the project.

Quickly, their collaboration turned one of architecture’s humblest structures into a nexus for meaning and care.
“We were exploring the scarcity of means and materials, but also the boundaries between inside and outside, and how to make something very simple like a toilet become something larger, like a meeting point and a resting space,” said Abascal.
“And also how to plan for the future of public architecture that might not be very taken care of or maintained by combining landscape and construction.”
The finished project features semi-open structures built from concrete blocks and filled with planting, creating a network of shaded resting points along the exposed track.
From the very first project we did together, we needed to arrive at something that was not Alessandro nor me, but Lanza. Our process has been so similar, in a way [ever since]
Bamboo trees loosely divide the male and female toilets, but they share common sinks – a feature that Lanza Atelier pushed for as part of their vision for a space of respectful coexistence between all users.
“It came out really naturally and fun, and simple,” said Arienzo. “That first interaction made Lanza happen because we needed to build a company after that.”
The nature of the collaboration pushed them to establish a distinct language from the get-go, adds Abascal.
“From the very first project we did together, we needed to arrive at something that was not Alessandro nor me, but Lanza,” she said.
“Our process has been so similar, in a way [ever since]. We explored so many different options, and we were so opinionated about how it should be. We very rigorously really gave all the importance to designing a toilet.”
As Escobedo identifies, Lanza Atelier’s work has a remarkable sense of play considering it lacks the colour, ornamentation or usual curlicues that would prompt association with the word.
Their use of materials is almost austere, in the sense that many of their projects have seen them hone in on just one material and push it to its limits, as in the local Sienna bricks of their Serpentine Pavilion.
Arienzo characterises this “extreme” approach as being “maybe a phase” more than a signature of the studio’s work. Instead, the key feature is how they arrive at the choice.
“We pick materials because of where the project is or what we have close to the site,” Arienzo said. “We analyse what’s the best or least aggressive way to build [in that place], because, we always say, building is really violent.”
Using fewer materials also allows them to focus more on questions of space, and makes it easier for their pavilions and installations, often demountable as a matter of principle, to find a second life.
“The main issue is achieving a degree of simplicity, which is very hard to do, because you start with complexity, and then you have to make decisions and to clean and to extract,” said Abascal.

That’s where the playfulness comes in, the designers identify, allowing them to dig into various elements, flip them, manipulate them and be curious about them.
“That, I think, is the playground,” adds Arienzo.
When asked what qualities each of the duo brings to the table that makes their work together so strong, Arienzo is initially flippant.
“I’m the bad cop,” he says with a smile.
But when the duo finally answers seriously, their responses do seem to reveal something about the alchemy going on behind the scenes.
“I used to call Alessandro’s gaze towards the world ‘the laser eye’,” said Abascal. “He is always pointing things out to me – mostly in our everyday lives, it’s things that the city creates and produces informally, but his laser eye applies to every context.”
“And it’s not only something visual but has to do with how things are and are put together and solved.”
For his part, Arienzo describes Abascal as the powerhouse behind Lanza’s ideas and concepts.
“I think she gives a lot of meaning to the projects,” said Arienzo. “I think without this, I would just do soulless stuff. She connects the work with history, with literature, with art.”
Lanza Atelier’s slate currently includes a house in LA to replace one destroyed by the wildfires, several projects in Mexico City and the design for the Expanded Tradition exhibition on contemporary Mexican craft at Qatari museum M7. When we speak, Abascal is on her way to the Canary Islands to conduct research on bird migration and pregnancy. It’s a patchwork that is idiosyncratically Lanza.
Neither Arienzo or Abascal seems to be the type to have a 10-year plan – asked about professional goals, Arienzo replies that they’re more orientated towards taking better care of themselves and their children – but they identify housing, particularly collective housing, as the type of architecture they most want to work on.
They hope to do it within a global industry that demolishes less and values what’s already built more, and that learns from the original “sustainable architecture” of vernacular construction in places like Mexico.
The country has been in the spotlight in the last few years as one of the few in Latin America with a progressive government, and one whose president, Claudia Sheinbaum, has frequently had to stand her ground against its northern neighbour.
Asked about whether government policy has had any positive effect on architecture and related industries at home, Abascal and Arienzo’s good cop and bad cop come out.
“From architects of the older generation, our generation and the younger generation, I can feel a sense of optimism,” said Abascal.
“I feel a sense of doing nothing,” said Arienzo. “Claudia is way better than what there was before; I’m a leftie. But I think there should be many more competitions around housing in the city.”
Both say they would like to see more public competitions in place of private developments, and that they would eagerly enter.
“Mexico City’s downtown is beautiful and it’s almost empty,” said Arienzo. “There isn’t a lot of density. I think it should be much more about that. Housing is a big issue and will be forever for these big cities.”
There is an ideal future for the architects, then. It’s one that would see the urban landscape of their home city benefit from the respect, creativity and serene beauty that is the Lanza calling card.
