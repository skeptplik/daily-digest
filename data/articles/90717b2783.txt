Why the U.S. midterms might have ended JD Vance's presidential hopes
Trump reportedly irked by vice-president's comments on Iran
JD Vance's presidential ambitions for 2028 could be among the most significant political casualties of the Democratic blue wave in this month's U.S. midterm elections.
His chances of becoming the Republican candidate in two years may have suffered a more recent blow, as reports suggest U.S. President Donald Trump has begun to sour on his vice-president.
"Despite years of work to keep his feet glued to Trump’s MAGA coattails, [Vance] is probably losing any semblance of hope that he’ll get to be a GOP frontrunner in 2028," New York Intelligencer staff editor Chas Danner wrote in an article under the headline "JD Vance’s presidential dream is being snuffed out."
"At the beginning of the year, Vance was widely seen as the GOP’s presumptive 2028 nominee. He may end the year as the presumptive also-ran, if that."
Over at the conservative magazine National Review, senior writer Noah Rothman also weighed in on what he views as Vance's diminishing political fortunes, saying, "Those who invested in JD Vance’s political stock ... may be looking to offload their holdings."
Vance hasn’t publicly said if he will run for president in 2028, but before the midterms, he was considered a front-runner, with perhaps the highest name recognition of any potential candidate.
However, since the midterms, Vance's aspirations "have suffered, because he's Donald Trump's vice-president," said Michael Traugott, a research professor emeritus at the University of Michigan's Center for Political Studies, in an interview with CBC News.
Exit polls suggested Republicans lost the election over issues relating to inflation and the cost of living, government corruption and the war in Iran.
"The problem is that the vice-president can't separate himself very easily from the administration's policies," Traugott said.
Donors express concerns about Vance
According to the Wall Street Journal, after the Democrats' success in taking the House of Representatives and Senate in the midterms, some Republican donors began making calls to senior party members and expressing concerns that they couldn't back Vance, because he's too tied to an unpopular administration.
This illustrates the challenge Vance faces: Do Republican primary voters want a candidate who has broader support among the general public but isn't directly connected to the president, or will they only support someone who has Trump's endorsement?
There are reports that Trump has grown frustrated with Vance's position on Iran. (Mark Schiefelbein/The Associated Press)
"There’s a real challenge of how you position yourself to be viable in a general election while winning a primary that still requires you to bow down and kiss the ring,” Igor Volsky, a Democratic strategist, told Reuters.
Trump hasn’t endorsed Vance or anyone else. Reports have also emerged recently that Vance himself is not so sure about a run, particularly in the wake of the Republicans' disastrous showing in the midterms.
In Republican primaries, Trump still commands a lot of votes and a lot of respect, Martin Cohen, a political science professor with James Madison University, told CBC News.
"I would definitely think that the front-runner would be the candidate with Trump's endorsement, if he does give one," he said.
Vance blamed Iran war for midterm losses
That means Vance will have to get his boss's approval, as well as the support of the MAGA base, while "trying to distance himself a little bit, at least," Cohen said.
Vance's attempt to distance himself from the administration may have already begun, with possible consequences. Following the midterms, Vance, in a lengthy post on X, levelled much of the blame for Republican losses on the war in Iran.
"If you’d like to argue it was worth it, that losing the Senate for the next 6 years was worth it, then do so," Vance wrote. "Argue it was a worthwhile cost to pay. Agree or disagree, that’s at least honest."
Vance, who according to reports has for a while privately expressed reservations about the war, has simultaneously argued that there's "no daylight" between him and the president on Iran.
In response to the X post, Trump agreed Iran may have been a factor in the midterm losses. But he also insisted that invading Iran was the right policy decision.
Trump told reporters that Vance's post should have added, "'If you had it to do again, would you do it again?' The answer is yes."
Soon after the X post, the vice-president, who had been leading negotiations between the U.S. and Iran, was taken off the file.
Posting on Truth Social, Trump said his administration no longer needed a deal with Iran and that he wanted Vance to focus "his complete and total attention" on alleged crime and corruption in Democratic-run cities and states.

Abe Greenwald, the executive editor of Commentary magazine, called Trump's sidelining of Vance a "remarkably skillful work of public humiliation."
Karen Beckwith, a political science professor with Ohio-based Case Western Reserve University, said in an email to CBC News that Trump's removal of Vance from any responsibility for negotiating or interacting with Iranian counterparts "undermines Vance as a potential presidential nominee in 2028."
Meanwhile, Fox News host Brian Kilmeade lashed out at Vance after his interview with Kilmeade's colleague Laura Ingraham, in which Vance said there were some who want the Iran war to go on indefinitely.
Kilmeade rejected that assertion, calling it "another straw man" argument from Vance, while adding that "he never should have been vice-president."
Trump reportedly frustrated with Vance
Beckwith said this could leave Vance without any substantive achievements on which to campaign for the 2028 nomination — unlike potential opponents like Secretary of State Marco Rubio or Republican governors and senators.
Asked about his social media post about Iran at a news conference on Friday, Vance, flanked by both Trump and Rubio, said the president makes the decision, that the president has said he would attack Iran again and that it's his job to support the president.
Vance's comments followed a New York Times report earlier this week that Trump has grown increasingly frustrated with how Vance has framed his assessment of what went wrong in Iran, complaining to aides and allies that Vance should have made a point of saying the president made the right decision in going to war with Iran.
According to the Times, Trump has instead been praising Rubio, a strong supporter of the U.S. war against Iran, as a potential presidential candidate.
While Trump may have soured on Vance, he reportedly has been praising U.S. Secretary of State Marco Rubio, right. (Mark Schiefelbein/The Associated Press)
While past polls have shown Vance as the leading Republican candidate for 2028, a recent poll from Emerson College had Rubio as the top choice.
Presidents in recent history, dating back to Ronald Reagan, have all publicly endorsed their vice-presidents who sought to be their party's candidate for the presidency. That includes Joe Biden's endorsement of Kamala Harris in 2024, despite Biden having been pressured by members of his own party to not run for second term.
"I don't think that Donald Trump is going to be shy about endorsing somebody," Traugott said. "Ordinarily, we would think the vice-president would have an advantage ... so the fact that he may be souring on this relationship is very important. Even if they were to say that [Trump] was remaining neutral, that would be a slap in the face of that."
Yet some political observers are cautious about writing Vance's political obituary just yet. Republican strategist Mark Bednar told CBC News that Vance has developed good connections to a large donor base within the Republican party over the past couple years.
Meanwhile, Republican strategist Barrett Marson told CBC News that Trump may have actually done Vance a favour by taking him off the Iran file.
"That is an absolute blessing. The less one has to do with this immensely unpopular war, the better you are off politically."
Marson added that there's still a lot of time before 2028, meaning things could improve to Vance's benefit. For example, gas prices could come down significantly, interest rates for homes could drop and voters could start to view the economy in positive terms.
"Two years is just an eternity," Marson said. "Especially in Donald Trump's America."
