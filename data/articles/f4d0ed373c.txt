The Irish EU presidency proposes cutting the probation period for newcomers from 15 to 10 years, extendable by five. Member states remain split on suspending voting rights, how to trigger measures and pre-accession monitoring, ahead of a key discussion at EU ambassadors' level.
The Irish presidency of the EU Council is proposing to cut the probation period for new member states from 15 to 10 years, extendable by five more, as governments prepare to finalise their position on the new safeguards.
In recent weeks, EU countries have been discussing how to implement the stronger safeguards the European Commission proposed in early October, including a probation period during which newcomers could see their voting rights suspended.
The Irish government, which chairs the talks, has circulated a draft paper compiling member states’ initial positions. Ambassadors will discuss it on Wednesday as input for Montenegro’s accession treaty.
“The presidency proposed a duration of ten years with the possibility of a five year extension. While there is broad support for this proposal, a number of member states still seek a longer duration of 15 years citing the need to provide an effective deterrence or response to possible backsliding for a longer period post accession,” reads the paper, seen by Euronews.
The Irish admit that member states remain divided on core elements of the scope. Some governments object to triggering the institutional safeguards over a violation of the principle of sincere cooperation, which may be hard to assess objectively.
“Member states have indicated the importance of having suspension of voting rights as a potential measure under this safeguard for particularly serious breaches. However, a small number of member states oppose such an option,” the document continues.
Five founding member states, led by the Netherlands, have been pushing for tighter safeguards to react more swiftly to the emergence of a new Viktor Orbán. The Friends of the Western Balkans, including Italy, want a more tailored approach that avoids putting newcomers on an unequal footing.
“Should the duration of the postponement be tailored depending on an assessment of the acceding State’s readiness at the time of accession or should the postponement be for an initial period of a year, with a possible extension (by QMV) for additional period(s) of one year and until the situation is remedied?” the document asks.
The presidency also concedes that governance needs further discussion. The Commission proposed that the process be triggered unless a qualified majority (QMV) of member states opposes it.
Some countries back this so-called reverse qualified majority, while others argue the gravity of the measure demands unanimity. As a compromise, the presidency proposes using simple QMV.
Pre-accession monitoring
The Commission also proposed a monitoring mechanism to ensure candidate countries honour their commitments before formally joining the bloc, with the option of postponing accession in the most serious cases.
The paper notes broad support for pre-accession monitoring of the so-called fundamentals, such as EU values, the rule of law and judicial independence.
The presidency acknowledged that several member states also want alignment with the EU’s common foreign and security policy (CFSP) added to the monitoring. Such alignment has become a key tool for keeping candidates from drifting into the orbit of foreign powers.
According to the paper, there appears to be general support for a new paragraph to facilitate engagement with acceding states where measures are proposed, and for linking pre-accession monitoring to the postponement clause, with monitoring continuing in the event of postponement.
Several member states also asked for a monitoring mechanism after accession. The presidency left the idea out of the legal text: despite some openness, there are concerns it could duplicate existing measures.
On how to trigger postponement, the document describes a “clear divergence” among member states, split between QMV and unanimity as the basis for decision-making.
As a possible solution, the presidency proposed an optional clause enabling postponement by qualified majority in areas of specific concern, though this raised questions over whether it would also apply to violations of EU values.
“The duration of a postponement remains a divisive matter. Some member states have called for a maximum duration of between 1 - 3 years. Others have requested that postponement continue until such a time as the situation has been remedied,” the paper states.
Sector-specific safeguards
On sector-specific safeguards, the document sees significant support for the presidency’s compromise of 10 years with a possible five-year extension. Stark divisions remain, however, between those pushing for 15 years and those wanting a shorter period of three to five.
“Most member states have supported the presidency proposal to strengthen an extension clause so that it would be invoked where measures have been taken in the first nine years and where sufficient grounds are demonstrated,” it adds.
