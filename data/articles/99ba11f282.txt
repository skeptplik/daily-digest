Around the world, people are increasingly choosing to have babies later in life for reasons both social and economic. As they get older though, they face a mounting biological challenge: genetic errors caused by chromosomes failing to separate correctly. This phenomenon, known as aneuploidy, is the leading cause of age-related infertility, failed in-vitro fertilization (IVF) cycles, and miscarriage, and it increases dramatically around the age of 40. Studies have shown that between 50% and 80% of embryos from women in their 40s carry chromosomal abnormalities.
One of the main drivers of aneuploidy is the loss of a protein complex called cohesin, which acts like a molecular glue holding chromosomes together. In a new study, researchers at the Max Planck Institute for Multidisciplinary Sciences in Göttingen, Germany, engineered a novel protein system capable of restoring normal chromosome connections in aging mouse eggs. The study, published Thursday in Science, raises the possibility of one day developing such a system into a treatment that could reduce aneuploidy in human eggs used for IVF.
“There is still work ahead, but their proof of concept was highly successful,” said Rajiv McCoy, a biologist at Johns Hopkins University who was not involved in the work. McCoy’s lab is focused on pregnancy loss, the leading cause of which is aneuploidy. It is estimated that about half of all human conceptions are lost before birth, including the roughly 15% of known pregnancies that end in miscarriage. Technologies that could reduce the odds of miscarriage and expand women’s biological reproductive windows by addressing aneuploidy would have far-reaching consequences for the IVF industry and for society at large. “The stakes are very high,” McCoy said.
When a cell prepares to divide, its chromosomes replicate, forming two copies, or sister chromatids, that are all lined up and organized by a cellular winch system known as the spindle. Within the spindle, the sister chromatids are tethered to each other by cohesin — its ring-like structure traps DNA from both sisters inside it. In most cells, cohesin only has to do this work for a few hours before other enzymes cleave it apart, freeing the chromatids to separate into two new cells. But in oocytes, cohesin has to keep this bond going for decades.
That’s because female babies are born with all the oocytes they will ever have. And already at birth, those 1 to 2 million immature egg cells have begun to divide, and the cohesin’s job is only finished when fertilization occurs. That triggers the end of the division process called meiosis in which the egg cells eject half their genetic material so they can fuse with sperm to make an embryo.
Over all that time, the cohesin molecule doesn’t seem to be reloaded, said Melina Schuh, a director at the Max Planck Institute who led the new work.
For the last two decades, Schuh has been finding new ways of peering into egg cells to better understand how meiosis works and what happens when it goes awry. In 2015, her lab showed for the first time how the structures holding chromosomes together deteriorate in human eggs as they age. Not long after, Schuh began to wonder if they might be able to engineer a system of proteins that could step in and stabilize the chromosomes in older eggs. Over the last eight years, her team tested many different designs before settling on one that did everything they wanted it to.
Called TALhesins, the proteins are designed to attach to specific DNA regions near the centers of each chromosome, tethering them together. The research team also baked a deactivation switch into the TALhesin system —based on cohesin’s natural cleavage sites — so that it would automatically release the chromosomes only when fertilization occurs. In experiments with eggs from older mice, the team found that the TALhesin system reduced premature chromosome separation, increasing the proportion of eggs with properly connected chromatids from 47% to 74% — a level comparable to that found in young mice.
Schuh told STAT her lab has already taken the next step of adapting the TALhesin system to bind to chromosomes in human eggs. “We have initial signs that there is a positive effect on cohesion,” she said. “But what we have not done yet is to really test in human oocytes whether we can reduce errors by stabilizing the chromosomes.”
That’s a critical next step, said Eva Hoffman, a molecular geneticist at the University of Copenhagen, who was not involved in the work. After that, researchers would need to establish that this method for reducing aneuploidy doesn’t introduce other kinds of problems. “The clinical potential depends upon regulatory aspects including safety studies for embryonic development,” Hoffman said. Even with the long road ahead, she commended the German group for generating data that challenges the conventional view of aneuploidy as a condition impossible (or impractical) to intervene in. “Since cohesion is established during fetal life and has to be maintained for decades, its depletion over time was thought to be irreversible,” Hoffman said.
Efforts to address age-related ovarian infertility are long overdue. For older women struggling to get pregnant, currently the only solution that’s available to most patients is to try IVF over and over. (For women between 35 and 40, the odds of getting pregnant are about one in three IVF cycles; for women over 40 it drops to one in ten.) This is both expensive and inefficient, and online support groups are overflowing with heart-wrenching stories of women spending a fortune and years of their lives on failed fertility sagas.
Many clinics also offer a panoply of “add-ons” — experimental treatments with shaky scientific backing — to boost the odds of having a baby. Some clinics overseas have even begun to market mitochondrial replacement techniques (MRT), a controversial method for reducing the risk of mitochondrial disease transmission, as a potential solution for age-related ovarian infertility. A recent STAT investigation found that hundreds of women in their late 30s and 40s have traveled to clinics in the Ukraine, Albania, and other parts of the world seeking MRT to boost the odds of IVF, despite scant evidence that it can overcome issues of aneuploidy.
Schuh has co-founded a startup, Ovo Labs, in Munich and London that has licensed TALhesin from the Max Planck Institute and is trying to commercialize the technology. A key hurdle is that most eggs retrieved in IVF have already undergone the first meiotic division, so cohesin loss could have already caused chromosome errors that can no longer be fixed. While retrieving and treating immature eggs appears to prevent some of these earlier errors, this then requires maturation of eggs in lab dishes, which is not widely used and has its own drawbacks, including that many eggs fail to mature successfully. Shuh is hopeful they can establish a system where oocytes are removed and treated a bit earlier — during the first phase of meiosis — which would require less time and fewer steps to mature.
Ovo Labs has competition from a U.K.-based startup called U-Ploid, co-founded by Schuh’s Max Planck colleague and study co-author, Alexandre Webster. U-ploid’s approach is slightly different, designed to preserve a cell’s natural structures for keeping chromosomes together.
Its lead candidate, Lyvanta, is injected into immature eggs prior to IVF.
Last month, at the 2026 meeting of the American Society for Reproductive Medicine, U-Ploid reported results from a study using immature eggs donated by patients at six U.K. fertility clinics. The study found that 65.5% of eggs treated with Lyvanta matured without detectable chromosome errors, compared to 35.7% of untreated eggs in a control group. The effect appeared in donors both under and over the age of 35, however eggs in that study were not fertilized. Lyvanta has not yet been tested in patients or demonstrated the ability to improve pregnancy or live birth rates.
