On Tuesday, a US jury convicted one of China’s key telecommunications and AI giants, Huawei Technologies, of stealing trade secrets from US tech firms and evading sanctions against Iran.
Although the longrunning charges stem back to 2018, the verdict coming now threatens to inflame US-China tensions at a sensitive time, just as Donald Trump prepares to increase tariffs on China. In a press release, however, the US Department of Justice celebrated the outcome as critical to protecting the US financial system, the global telecommunications industry, and US national security.
The jury convicted Huawei of charges including bank fraud, conspiracy to defraud the US, money laundering conspiracy, and “substantive” violations of the International Emergency Economic Powers Act.
Among crimes, Huawei was found guilty of “repeatedly” lying to banks about its business in Iran. Huawei also frequently lied to the US government that “it did not assist Iran’s government, military, or intelligence community,” the DOJ said. In reality, the DOJ proved at trial that Huawei was conducting business in Iran under the name “Skycom” to evade sanctions, while providing Iran with “turnkey services with respect to an Internet backbone and broader accessibility for mobile networks.” Further, Huawei helped the Iranian regime “monitor and censor its users’ access to the Internet” and provided surveillance cameras to monitor protests, the DOJ said. In a statement, the acting principal deputy assistant attorney general for national security, M. Scott Proctor, said Huawei “empowered our enemies” and “harmed our national security.”
Huawei stole US trade secrets
As the DOJ successfully argued, Huawei lied about its Iranian business specifically so that it could continue obtaining billions in US dollars by participating in a global marketplace that gave it necessary access to steal US tech. Among companies targeted, US Attorney for the Eastern District of New York Joseph Nocella Jr. said that Huawei blatantly stole from T-Mobile and Cisco, as well as a semiconductor storage company called CNEX and an antenna manufacturer called Quintel.
