Big tech companies may face “multibillion-dollar” fines and individuals could sue social media companies in court under Australia’s new digital duty of care laws, with the Greens to support the bill’s passage after extracting major amendments from Labor.
From late 2027, Australians will also be able opt out of addictive online algorithms under the new legislation, while the “endless scroll” of social media feeds may also cease.
The communications minister, Anika Wells, confirmed that, in extreme breaches, social media and tech companies could face fines of up to 5% of their global revenue if they flout the new laws. She said she had already met with the US ambassador, David Brat, to discuss the laws, and had stared down potential legal challenges from some of the world’s most powerful firms.
“I was at the high court a couple of weeks ago as the second respondent in the case against the social media minimum age. If people want to have another go, bring it on,” Wells said on Tuesday.
The government’s legislation will pass parliament this week, potentially as soon as Tuesday afternoon, after the Greens agreed to support the bill after intense negotiations. The Coalition had opposed the laws from the outset, citing free speech and censorship concerns, leaving the Greens to negotiate with Labor.
The planned framework would apply new online safety standards to social media, games, apps and chatbots. Labor said it would place “an overarching obligation” on tech platforms “to provide a safe online environment for all Australians” and replace a range of existing online codes and standards with a broader duty-of-care requirement to protect users from certain “harmful” content. Wells had suggested content promoting misogyny, terrorism, suicide, self-harm and eating disorders would be captured by the laws.
Following discussions with the Greens, Wells on Tuesday confirmed amendments to the bill would see Australians given a “private right of action” – a local equivalent to successful laws in the US allowing citizens to sue large companies.
The government will consider setting up an independent ombudsman or watchdog for big tech, and the maximum fines for breaches of rules could reach $109m, or up to 5% of global revenue.
The Coalition will not support the bill, which is expected to be voted on in the Senate on Tuesday night. The shadow minister for industry, Andrew Hastie, claimed the duty of care would lead to “a massive imposition on free speech and political communication”.
“Whilst the government is dressing this in the language of protecting children, they’re actually moving towards censorship here, which is our main issue here,” he told the ABC.
“It vests huge amounts of power in the minister of communications and the eSafety commissioner, who basically will regulate very vaguely defined definitions of online harm. We think that’s contrary to free speech. We’d rather have debate – and this puts our country and our culture of debate at risk.”
Labor’s legislation could also draw the ire of US-based tech giants and the Trump administration.
Wells has previously said Donald Trump’s Truth Social platform would be subject to the laws but that the government had committed to “constructive dialogue” with the US, including during her Tuesday meeting with Brat.
“I think we would say there are points of differences, and we’re ironing them out,” she said.
Meta, Google and TikTok were contacted for comment. The Tech Council of Australia, an industry body which counts Google, Microsoft, LinkedIn, OpenAI and Pinterest among its members, said it supported the legislation, but voiced concern about the amendments.
“A private right of action is a complex undertaking with significant consequences. How this is implemented is critical, and we need proper consultation to ensure that businesses are not hindered from investing in Australia. If we get this wrong, it will be counterproductive to encouraging innovation in Australia,” a spokesperson said.
Other major changes to the bill include adding “addictive” features to the list of risks that tech platforms must address, and clarifying the definitions of “hostile attitudes to women and gender equality”. Wells said this was “not about disagreeing with feminism or expressing an opinion”, but would force tech platforms to avoid exposing children to content promoting hatred of women, including violence, death threats, coercion or harassment.
She has previously stated “manosphere podcasts” would not be affected.
Amendments to the bill will also expand eSafety Commissioner powers to remove “nudify” apps and content, and stronger privacy protections.
“The Greens have long campaigned for big tech to be held accountable for the harm they profit off online,” the Greens senator Sarah Hanson-Young said on Tuesday.
The party had long pressed for stronger fines, an ombudsman scheme and stronger action on addictive design features. Hanson-Young said that provision would have the effect of “dumping the doomscroll with rules that force tech companies to prevent harm from their addictive design feature”.
This would go alongside original requirements for social media platforms to offer an “opt-out” feature for its algorithm and suggested content.
Wells said those elements would come into force 12 months after the laws passed. She nominated design features such as “the toxic popularity meters, the endless scroll” as those which could be banned under the addictive features stipulation, but said final decisions would be made by eSafety.
The Telecommunications Industry Ombudsman, which has volunteered to act as a big tech watchdog, welcomed the move to set up an independent complaints mechanism.
“Online services are a core part of our lives. It’s great that Australians will soon have access to a free, fair and independent dispute resolution scheme when big tech gets things wrong.”
