Regarding Huw Green’s article (How can we explain psychosomatic illnesses?, 22 November), the body and the mind/brain have strong connections both ways. It is therefore not surprising that major psychological distress can lead to physical symptoms, and the other way around.
However, as a GP, I would recommend being extremely careful about equating “otherwise unexplained symptoms” with psychosomatic illness. No explanation doesn’t mean no physical illness, it just means we haven’t found one (yet).
We should be honest about that, to ourselves and to our patients. I have seen diagnoses of “functional” or “psychosomatic” illness being overturned by proof that patients had anything from vitamin B12 deficiency to severe neurological disorder to inflammatory bowel disease.
A recent study found that autoimmune diseases, such as thyroid disease, systemic lupus and rheumatoid arthritis were often initially misdiagnosed as psychological or functional. It could take years, and more often so in women, before the right diagnosis was found. A misdiagnosis of a psychological cause was made in the majority of women in first instance and a substantial proportion of men.
Therefore only when there is a clear connection between psychological distress and physical illness may a tentative diagnosis of psychological illness be made. Even then, we should keep on the lookout for any evidence that there is a physical disease after all.
Willemina Rietsema
Oxford
Emotions, perception and expectations are all biological processes. Our nervous systems are constantly interpreting bodily signals, anticipating demands and adjusting physiological responses to maintain stability. When these processes become less flexible, physical symptoms can emerge, with attention, expectations and emotional responses potentially reinforcing them, but emotional distress doesn’t necessarily have to be the cause.
We need to consider the wider context in which symptoms develop, including physiological demands, environmental pressures, perceived threat and our capacity to adapt. Separating psychological and physical processes risks overlooking how closely they interact.
Carrie Eckersley
Holmes Chapel, Cheshire
