Hot off its record-breaking console launch, the developer of Grand Theft Auto 6 is facing a problem â and it's one that is increasingly affecting the wider games industry.
Rockstar is yet to announce an official PC version of GTA 6, but with the emergence of powerful AI tools, coders and gamers have taken matters into their own hands.
The developer has typically released Grand Theft Auto on PC long after its initial console launch â 19 months later in the case of GTA 5.
This time it may not have that luxury â in part because of an apparent leak of files from an Xbox development build of the game, but also due to the rapid advance of AI coding platforms.
These tools are being used to recreate or adapt existing games, sometimes within days, taking away the control developers have over their products.
Barely a week after the release of GTA 6, efforts are already under way to get an unofficial version playable on PC, although some projects have faced takedown notices from the game's publisher.
For some gamers, it's an exciting prospect, despite the legal questions surrounding the process.
For developers, it may be a warning: if a game as big as GTA 6 can be recreated, what does that mean for everyone else?
Like 'looking at a painting and making your own replica'
The recent wave of AI-assisted game recreation has been widely linked to Anthropic's Opus 5.5 model, released in September, and to agentic tools such as Claude Code.
Mark Brown, creator of the YouTube channel Game Maker's Toolkit whose own game Word Play was recently recreated using AI, said cloning largely took two forms: replication and reverse engineering.
Replication is where someone asks an AI chatbot such as Claude or ChatGPT to build a new game based on an existing one, possibly using screenshots or a description.
This, he said, was similar to "looking at a painting and making your own replica" â something which is "a very close copy, but is ultimately a new painting".
It's a particular issue among smaller studios, many of whom worry that AI makes it easier for others to copy their work.
When indie developers Sodoj and Marco from the studio Robotonic noticed a version of their unreleased puzzle game 204Snake appear on the free games platform Yandex with the same name, they were "appalled".
"The game was essentially the exact same idea, the exact same mechanics as we had, but with the art looking a bit off," Marco told the BBC.
The duo were able to issue a copyright strike to Yandex and Google to get the copy taken down, but said it only felt like a "small victory".
The second approach â reverse engineering â involves working backwards from a finished game to understand how it was created.
One way of doing this is decompilation, which attempts to turn the game's final version into something closer to its original source code.
Brown compares the process to "taking a cake and somehow turning it back into the original recipe, with the exact quantities of flour, eggs, and milk used, so anyone can remake the same cake".
Reverse-engineering projects have helped bring games such as Halo: Combat Evolved and Grand Theft Auto: Vice City to web browsers.
A related technique, recompilation, can help adapt a game that has been reverse-engineered to run on different hardware. This is one of the methods being explored in efforts to get GTA 6 onto PC.
Coders wanting to attempt this with Rockstar's latest game face a difficult task because of its sheer size and complexity.
Earlier in November, Cyberleek â a hacker or group of hackers â released what they claimed to be a near-complete Xbox development build of the game. They later leaked what they claimed to be the missing .exe file needed to launch the game.
It gave those working on an unofficial PC version a crucial new starting point.
"Now people are using AI to try and decompile it, make a modified version that could run on PC, and compile a new version," said Brown.
Since the leak, several groups have posted claims of their progress on platforms such as X, ranging from getting the game to boot up to making it run at a stable rate.
One attempt seen by the BBC on X purported to have some of the game running nearly as smoothly as it does on console, albeit on a high-end PC.
Accounts and sites have even sprung up to track the various attempts, excitedly sharing clips of the latest milestone passed and scoring their efforts.
Rockstar and its parent company, Take-Two Interactive, have not confirmed the authenticity of the leaked build, and neither has responded to the BBC's request for a statement.
However, Take-Two has issued take, externaldown notices, external of public projects aiming to port the Xbox version to PC on platforms such as GitHub.
"There are no remedies available to rectify this clear attempt to facilitate copyright infringement based upon Take-Two's copyrighted property," one notice stated.

Available for over a year
News of the leak has been celebrated by some gamers on Reddit, who resent having to wait for the PC release.
"Why should the PC players have to wait half a year if you can develop the game in weeks as shown so far!" wrote one gamer, external.
"Will be funny when the decomp runs way better than whatever Rockstar can make," said another.
Christopher Dring, editor-in-chief and co-founder of The Game Business, said part of the reason why games are often released on PCs months after consoles is that PC versions are "complex" with "different hardware configurations", as opposed to the more standardised set-up of consoles.
He added there could also be a more commercial reason, as Rockstar can "have a second moment when the PC version is ready".
In an interview with The Wall Street Journal, external, the chief executive of Take-Two Interactive, Strauss Zelnick, said while piracy was a "fact of life" in the entertainment business, it had until now been "less of an issue" for video games due to their "file sizes and complexity".
However, Zelnick also reportedly told an investor Q&A that the company was "utterly focused on enhancing our technical defences and... legal activities appropriately".
When asked about the legality of reverse-engineering code, Dr Tommy Thompson, an AI consultant who presents the AI and Games YouTube channel, said the practice of decompilation exists in a "grey area" - until it "reaches a point where copyright and intellectual property [IP] are infringed upon".
He added that decompiling code could be legal within limits, such as for educational purposes, but "as soon as you attempt to use it to redistribute or repackage existing software", then this is "largely illegal".
"In most cases, decompiling or recompiling code is likely to involve unauthorised use of the game IP," said Aaron Treble, a UK intellectual property lawyer for Lewis Silkin.
Anyone who releases or sells a clone "will almost certainly be infringing [on copyright]", Treble added, though he said the law on AI companies' own responsibility is still evolving.
Anthropic has reportedly since told Claude Code to steer away from recreating specific existing games, external, saying this would breach its usage policy.
Despite this, people are continuing to work on an unofficial PC game, hoping to make it playable before Rockstar releases the official version.
"Seeing as this has already affected industry giants with very deep pockets, the proverbial bear has been poked," said Brown.
"We'll have to see how it reacts."
