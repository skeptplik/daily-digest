The upcoming world chess championship between Uzbekistan’s Javokhir Sindarov and India’s Gukesh Dommaraju in Geneva has all the makings of a barnburner.
Earlier this year, Sindarov, 20, won the Candidates Tournament as an outside longshot with a magnificent attacking style that overwhelmed the competition, locking up his title shot with a string of dazzling early wins.
His opponent Dommaraju, also 20, is the reigning champ. He’s hugely resilient under pressure, facing complications and crises with astonishing equanimity. Sindarov can blow people away and Gukesh is extremely hard to blow away. It’s a classic set-up: irresistible force meets immovable object.
Play over the board will be only one aspect of the significance of the struggle in Geneva. A world chess championship is a kind of status report on intelligence itself, and human intelligence is in a state of radical flux. If you want to understand AI, what it changes and what it doesn’t, chess is the first place to look.
The world championship is also an opportunity for tens of millions of ordinary chess lovers, like myself, to realize, once again, just how much we suck at chess.
My current rating on Chess.com hovers around 1,800. For some, that number might seem impressive – it puts me somewhere around the top 80,000 players on a site with 174 million members. For real chess players, that number is laughable. A master rating typically starts at about 2,300. I’m nothing.
What makes that rating a tad shameful is how much work has gone into it. I was a teenage chess club kid in the 1990s, when it was literally the definition of geekiness, the late-to-puberty crowd disputing fianchetto fortresses and knight posts on the sixth rank. I have played chess almost every day since without serious interruption. I have read many, many books, living in hope. I have gone through the drudgery of memorizing endgames, although I forget them all a month after studying, which is probably the root of my problem.
Put it this way: If I had spent the time I had spent playing and studying chess on, say, learning Mandarin, I would be one of those white guys you see on Chinese TV doing the equivalent of “Peter Piper picked a peck of pickled peppers.” And I’m at 1,800.
Every other skill I have tried to improve at, I have. I wanted to get better at cooking. I can now, if you give me a Sunday, make gâteau de crêpes à la Florentine or an (admittedly crooked) croquembouche. I started doing crosswords a decade ago. I finish them more often and faster. It’s the same with almost anything. Except chess.
Why do I suck at chess? I think, by now, I have expended enough energy to know the answer: my brain simply isn’t built for it. It’s just not that good at the spatial, logic problems that are the essence of the game. The question then shifts: why am I obsessed with something my brain can’t do very well?
Perhaps the answer is in the question. Obviously, part of the appeal for me is that it’s a break from the monstrous, unending struggle with words and their meanings which is my profession. That, and the gift of sucking at chess is the same as its curse. The blessing is the agony. I can see the beauty in what I cannot master, although I admit it troubles me that, in India, there is a six-year-old kid who would not lose a game to me if we played 100 times and there’s nothing I can do about it.
Chess is, more than anything, a game for people who love to think, which is why it has always served as a harbinger of intellectual currents, always ahead of its time. The first world chess championship, in 1886, was a conflict between the nostalgic Romanticism of Johannes Zukertort and the modern positional play of Wilhelm Steinitz, between chess as gusts of sudden drastic insights into tactics and chess as the revelation of the power of systematic, accretive advantage – the efflorescence of nature against the incipient power of industrialization.
The hypermodern school that emerged in the 1930s reflected both modernism and postmodernism. Its proponents rejected traditional play towards the centre in favour of the margins on the board. Traditional structures contained weaknesses which could be exploited. Chess, as much as literature or architecture, bears the imprint of the zeitgeist.
Occasionally, politics infuse the game. The most famous world championship match, between the American Bobby Fischer and the Russian Boris Spassky in 1972, stood in for the cold war as a whole because it posed a test of whether idiosyncratic genius could compete against the power of the state. The 1985 Kasparov-Karpov match, contrasting Anatoly Karpov’s cold precision with Garry Kasparov’s wild, effervescent creativity, represented the triumph of a reformist younger generation over an entrenched bureaucratic elite. Gorbachev first used the term glasnost, the policy of opening up the Soviet state, while the match was under way. Today, India’s newfound prominence as a chess superpower is evidence of the rise of the global south.
But when Sindarov and Gukesh sit down to play, the question hanging over the board will be bigger than politics. It will be a question of the nature of the mind itself: what is the place of human intelligence in a world increasingly dominated by machines?
You probably started thinking about artificial intelligence as something that might affect your life in or around 2023, when ChatGPT launched. Chess has been grappling with the implications of AI since 1996.
The dream of building a machine that could outthink humans at chess is centuries old. The Mechanical Turk, a chess-playing automaton which wowed imperial courts in the 1770s, was the first “thinking machine”. It turned out to be a little person tucked inside the machinery. Alan Turing himself wrote the first chess algorithm in 1948. And the moment Deep Blue beat Kasparov in the late 1990s was the first clear triumph of the technology, the first sign that the machines were coming for the brain.
Chess today is what an intellectual activity looks like after AI has gone through it.
In the past, if you lost a game, you would play it over with a friend or coach, to see where it all started to go wrong. No need to spend days examining your every move any more. The machine tells you. Throughout the 70s and 80s, in the pages of Chess Informant, theoretical debates raged over opening lines. Debates unfolded over years sometimes, and often not to any definite conclusion.
World championships, and the candidates tournaments that led up to them, used to involve teams thrashing out new strategies, searching for exciting imbalances their opponents had not encountered. Tournaments sometimes became laboratories of individual openings, and preparations were research into the nature of the game itself. Now you plug in a line and the computer tells you if it’s right. There’s no debate.
Positions have right and wrong answers now. The competition between styles and schools that defined chess since its beginning is no longer meaningful.
All that grand intellectual tradition … right in the bin.
Here’s the thing: you might think that computers would end chess. If a phone can beat the best player in the world, why bother?
But AI didn’t end chess at all. The opposite. Chess has never been more popular.
The world used to have room for one, maybe two, chess celebrities. You probably knew who Bobby Fischer was, and you might have known Spassky, but probably not his first name. Kasparov was the chess guy for about two decades. Now you don’t need to be world champion, or even to be close to being world champion, to be a chess celebrity.
There are numbers of fans devoted to the dazzling calculative ability of Hikaru Nakamura, the absurdly dynamic daring of Alireza Firouzja, the elegance of Fabiano Caruana (my favourite player), and even the somewhat villainous, psychologically intimidating Hans Niemann.
In my neighbourhood in Toronto, any given week, I can play on Monday nights at the local Japanese listening bar, where a room full of glamorous 25-year-olds, some of them on dates, lounge in the dark over boards, sipping $20 yuzu cocktails. There’s also a Wednesday night meetup at what can only be described as a frat boy pub; they literally call themselves chess bros. There’s also the official club in the Zen Buddhist temple on the corner. And that’s just within walking distance of my house.
The sheer quantity of online chess is overwhelming. The Botez sisters, who combine master-level play with the charming dramas of their sisterly intimacy, have more than 3 million followers. GothamChess, with over 10 million, is one of the world’s top influencers, period.
The lesson of chess, for anyone trying to understand what artificial intelligence does, is that it removes the sense of human superiority from an activity and it reinscribes that activity as sport.
And chess is an amazing, if bizarre, sport. In 2024, I attended the candidates tournament in Toronto. In the hall itself, there was a smell of violence, intoxicating, hormonal. The smell of confrontation. A bit like a boxing match. Even though they were just sitting in front of each other, there was a sense of men having their brains beaten out.
In a basement under the hall, away from the players, I and a couple of hundred other fans thrashed out the variations of the games under way. Again, the engines had altered the experience completely. Even lowly players like myself, with access to the technology, know the correct next move, so we sit around watching the superstars make mistakes.
Patzers like me clutch our hair and shout, “How could you blunder like that, Fabi?” exactly like football fans who couldn’t run a mile screaming at the world’s greatest athletes: “I could have scored from there.”
There are other essential lessons about AI that chess can teach the world. The first is that artificial intelligence has serious limits. One of the reasons the current champion Gukesh is so strong is that his coach, the grandmaster Vishnu Prasanna, banned him from using AI until he was a strong player. The AI, he knew, would be a crutch, a way of unlearning. The old-fashioned analysis was essential for the growth of the mind. AI is easy to master after you’ve figured out how to think.
The deeper lesson is that the game people play when they play chess is not the game “chess”. It is the game “human chess”. Occasionally, GothamChess analyzes games between different AI bots. These matches are totally fascinating insofar as they are utterly distinct from human play. Endgame subtleties work themselves out over 30 or 40 moves – totally beyond comprehension. Engines are willing to put up with the stress of keeping their kings in the open in a way that no human could accept simply because of the mental effort required to consider its dangers.
Sometimes GothamChess, which once achieved a rating higher than 2,300, can’t explain the reasoning behind the moves at all. They’re beyond people.
But games between engines are curiosities, fascinating in their own right but of limited interest to the game as it is played. It turns out that what is of interest to humans is what humans find of interest, what they can grasp.
Sindarov-Gukesh 2026 will be a very human affair. The question will be: who is tougher? Who can take the punishment? Who can dole it out? “Chess is war over the board,” Fischer once said. “The object is to crush the opponent’s mind.”
The Chinese player Ding Liren beat Russian Ian Nepomniachtchi in 2024 to become world champion, but the effort seems to have crushed him. He retreated from the game altogether for nine months. “I was exhausted but still couldn’t sleep well. This led to depression. I was treated in a clinic twice,” he told a German newspaper. Reading Louise Glück and Albert Camus offered him some comfort.
When he returned to the board, Carlsen bemoaned that Ding seemed “permanently broken”. He is, now, by some accounts, recovering. But the championship did something to him, like fighting Sonny Liston did something to Muhammad Ali. As in all truly great sports events, there’s an ugliness at the heart of the beauty. Everything intoxicating has its dollop of poison.
AI humbles human ability. “We have discovered that humans are not that good at playing chess,” Magnus Carlsen told a Spanish newspaper recently. That remark is some comfort to patzers like me. Everybody sucks at chess. Some suck more than others. More importantly, it’s a clarification of the state of the human mind itself, and the future of the mind. Humanity lives in a permanent state of misunderstanding.
AI is now coming for everything. Chess was just first. The state of the game is a sign of great hope and despair at once for the future of thinking. It demonstrates the limited connection of the mind to the reality at hand, but damn is it fascinating. Chess, and everything else, is going to remain a mystery impenetrable by our little ape brains. We are never given more than a fragment of the significance of anything, even in games of our own construction. Our role is to appreciate and to marvel at the beauties and the terror of the glimpses we can gather. Our role is to play.
Even in the company of glamorous machines, all we have is each other, and the boards and pieces we put between us.
