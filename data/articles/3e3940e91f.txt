What animal is older than the dinosaurs and accounts for nearly a quarter of species on Earth? It is the magnificent beetle, an insect whose wings rest underneath a hard, protective case that often displays beautiful colours and intricate ornaments. “Wherever you stand on the land surface of the Earth save only for Antarctica, you are never more than a few metres from a beetle,” writes Maxwell Barclay in Interesting Beetles, which showcases some of their stunning variety.
The main image shows the glorious flower chafer (Howdenypa gloriosa), a stunning opalescent green and blue. Its sleek flight from flower to flower, which takes place with the wing cases closed, unlike the clumsier flights of beetles with open wing cases, has attracted attention from aerospace engineers. They have fitted some flower chafers with tiny wireless transmitters to better understand how they manoeuvre while flying.
Below is the Colorado potato beetle (Leptinotarsa decemlineata), which has a destructive relationship with humans. After the potato was introduced to North America, this striped beetle started to prefer spuds to native foods and quickly became a worldwide pest.
Below, the carbuncle frog-legged beetle (Sagra carbunculus) shows off the buff hind legs that earned it its amphibian-inspired name and serve it well in battle – or when competing for an iridescent mate.
The specimens in these images are taken from the archives of the Natural History Museum in London, where Barclay is senior curator in charge of beetles. It was difficult, he writes in his book, to choose which to include, “because each of the 400,000 beetle species has an interesting story to tell”.
The violin beetle (Mormolyce phyllodes) shown below makes up for being an inelegant flyer by having a unique body shape that allows it to blend in with fallen leaves and winged seeds of trees in the steamy tropical rainforests where it resides.
Finally, the desert fog-basking beetle (Onymacris bicolor) shown below is another master of adaptation that can survive in places as arid and harsh as the Namib desert in southern Africa. Elegant in its black-and-white garb, this insect climbs to the top of a dune when conditions are right, points its head down and waits for dew to condense on special structures on its wings and then run into its mouth, keeping it alive.
Barclay writes that beetles can not only survive in all these habitats, from potato fields to tropical forests and deserts, but that they are an integral part of each of them. Without beetles as predators, pollinators, herbivores or scavengers, many ecosystems simply couldn’t function, he writes.
